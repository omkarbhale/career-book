You picked up a book about jobs. Odds are something brought you here: a rejection that made no sense, a job you have outgrown, a first week somewhere new, or a colleague you cannot work out. Good. A book is far easier to learn from when you already have a question it can answer.

This page takes five minutes. It tells you who the book is for, how it is built, and how to get the most out of it, including what every box and badge on these pages means.

## Who this book is for {#howto.sec.who}

**Read on if most of these are true:**

- You are early or in the middle of your career, or you are about to start one.
- You want to get a job, change jobs, or get better at the job you have.
- You would rather be told what the evidence says than be told a confident story.
- You can enjoy a joke and a cartoon without suspecting they make the content less serious.

**You may want a different book if:**

- you need legal advice about a specific dispute (this book points at laws, but it is not a lawyer);
- you want a reference manual of every interview question ever asked;
- you are looking for tricks to appear to be someone you are not. We will make the case that this is a bad trade, and we are not subtle about it.

The examples lean towards technology jobs, because that is where our main character works, but almost nothing here depends on writing code. Where practice differs by country (notice periods, pay-transparency laws, what an employer may ask), you will find a **Field Note** for the regions we could verify.

## The idea in one paragraph {#howto.sec.idea}

Getting a job and doing a job are two different games. In the first, strangers judge a short, edited trailer of you; in the second, colleagues watch the whole film, every day, for years. The skills differ, so the book teaches them separately: Part I is the snapshot game of getting or switching a job, and Part II is the long game of working with people. The thread through both is one person: you. The snapshot should be an honest trailer of the film, and the film is what earns your next snapshot. [[ch01]] makes that case properly.

## How the book is built to teach {#howto.sec.built}

This is not a textbook, but it borrows from the research on how people learn from words and pictures. A few of the design choices, and why:

- **It talks to you.** Rewriting instructional text in a conversational, second-person style has improved both recall and the ability to apply what was learned, though in studies longer than about 35 minutes the benefit shrank to almost nothing [@ginns2013]. So the book talks with you, and it keeps sections short.
- **Pictures carry ideas, not decoration.** Every figure explains something, and its labels sit on the picture rather than in a key you have to match up. In lab studies, placing words next to the parts of a picture they describe, and signalling the structure of an explanation, both helped people understand it [@mayer2003].
- **Jokes have to earn their place.** Interesting but irrelevant material ("seductive details") tends to hurt learning a little [@cheng2026], and cutting it helped in Mayer and Moreno's experiments [@mayer2003]. So the humour here is meant to make a point stick, not to fill a page. If a joke ever gets in the way, that is our failure, not yours.
- **You will be asked to do things.** In a major review of study techniques, testing yourself earned the top rating for usefulness, while rereading and highlighting earned the bottom one [@dunlosky2013]. That is what the exercises are for.
- **One idea at a time.** Working memory holds only a handful of chunks at once (about four, on the best current estimate) [@cowan2001], so each section tries to land one idea before starting the next.

And one thing we avoid: brain myths. "Learning styles" and "left-brain versus right-brain learners" are popular, and a survey of teachers interested in neuroscience found that they believed about half of such myths [@dekker2012]. You will not find them here.

## The boxes, and what each one is for {#howto.sec.boxes}

Most of the book is plain prose and pictures. The boxes below interrupt it on purpose, and each type has one job. Here is a sample of each, so you will recognise them later.

::: scene {slug="INT. ANY OFFICE — MONDAY, 9:02 AM"}
**Scenes** move the story. You will follow Dev and a small cast through both games, and the scenes show the ideas happening to people before the prose explains them.

@dev: This is me. I'll be the one making the mistakes so you don't have to.
:::

::: pause
**Pause** asks you a question and does not answer it straight away. Stop and think before you read on; the guess you commit to is what makes the answer stick.
:::

:::: move {#howto.move.sample} A sample exercise
**Your Move** is an exercise. Do it with a pen, in your head, or in a notes app, but do it before you open the answer. Try this one now: which of the boxes on this page do you expect to skip, and why?

::: answer
There is no right answer to this one. The point is the habit: commit to a guess first, then look.
:::
::::

::: ask
**Ask Anyway** collects the questions people tend to keep to themselves.

Q: Do I have to read the boxes?

A: You can skip them; the main text still makes sense. But the exercises and the Myth Checks are where most of the learning happens.
:::

::: chairs {left="The Pause" right="The Answer"}
@left: I hold back. I make them wonder.

@right: And I'm the payoff, so they come back for me. **Two Chairs** puts two ideas in conversation, so you can hear both sides of an argument.
:::

::: trapdoor
**Trapdoor** marks a common mistake that costs more than it seems to. When you see one, slow down.
:::

::: myth {verdict=busted grade=strong} You only use 10% of your brain
**Myth Check** takes a popular claim and gives a verdict (*Busted*, *Partly true* or *Holds up*) plus a badge for how strong the evidence behind the verdict is. Dekker and colleagues list this one among the classic neuromyths; the teachers in their survey believed about half of the myths they were shown [@dekker2012].
:::

::: receipts {source=dunlosky2013} A study worth knowing
**Receipts** opens up one important source: who did it, what they found, and what it cannot tell you.
:::

::: field {regions="India, UK, US"} Practice differs
**Field Note** flags where the rules change by country or region. We name only the regions we could check, and we say so when we could not.
:::

::: craft
**Craft Note** is practitioner advice: what experienced people tend to do, which no study has tested. It is labelled so you know it is experience, not evidence.
:::

::: craft {by=author}
**Author's View** is our own argument. You are welcome to disagree; we label it so that you can.
:::

::: coming {ref=ch10}
**Flashback** and **Coming Soon** link backward and forward through the book, so you can follow a thread between the two games.
:::

::: rewind
- **Rewind** closes each chapter with the points worth keeping.
- If you only have five minutes, read the Rewind.
:::

## How to read the evidence {#howto.sec.evidence}

Every factual claim in this book points at a numbered source. Click a number and you land in the chapter's reference list, where every source carries one of four grades:

| Grade | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong** | Meta-analyses, large randomised or natural experiments, official statistics, the text of a law. Hard to overturn. |
| **Moderate** | A single well-designed study, a careful review, a large survey with a clear method. Probably right, could shift. |
| **Weak** | Small or unreplicated studies, vendor surveys, practitioner frameworks. A hint, not a finding. |
| **Myth or unsupported** | Contradicted by better evidence, retracted, or with no traceable source. Cited only to show where a myth came from. |

Advice that rests on experience rather than research carries a [!craft] label, and our own arguments carry [!view]. If we could not verify something, we either left it out or labelled it [!unverified]. You will see that last label rarely, because the rule is to cut first.

The full list of sources, with their grades and the chapters that use them, is on the [References](references.html) page.

## A few honest limits {#howto.sec.limits}

Much of the research on hiring and on behaviour at work comes from the United States and Western Europe. Where that matters, we say so. The rules of the hiring game also change: laws on pay transparency, non-compete clauses and notice periods were moving even while this book was being written, so treat any legal detail as a pointer to check, not as advice.

The style of this book owes a debt to the learner-centred approach Kathy Sierra and Bert Bates championed in technical books, and to Richard Mayer's research on learning from words and pictures. Every word and every picture here is original.
