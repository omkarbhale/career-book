Meera is talking about the whole of a working life, and the whole is a different object from any of its parts. So far this book has looked at games you play in months: a job search, a first year, a review cycle, a promotion case. This chapter steps back to the scale of decades, where the most useful questions change. Not "how do I get this job?" but "what do I want the next ten years to have been made of?"

The evidence thins out at this scale, and we will say so as we go. Careers are long, people are different, and nobody has run a randomised trial on a working life. What we can do is put the best findings we have next to each other, and use them to make the next decision better than the last.

## A career is a sequence of loops {#ch22.sec.loops}

The picture most people carry of a career is a ladder: start at the bottom, climb, stop at the top. It is a tidy picture and a misleading one.

Start with how often people move. In the US, people born between 1957 and 1964 held an average of 12.9 jobs between the ages of 18 and 58 [@bls-nlsy2025]. Moves between whole occupations and industries were common too, and became more common between 1968 and 1997 [@kambourov2008]; [[ch20.sec.capital]] drew the other half of that finding, that much of what you learn is specific to a kind of work, so a bigger turn costs more of it. A career, in the data, looks much less like one ladder than like a series of jobs joined by turns.

[[ch01.sec.loop|The loop from Chapter 1]] gives you a better picture. Each job is a full turn of it: a snapshot game to get in, a long game once you are there, a career game that turns the work into skills and reputation, and then, eventually, another snapshot, played with what the last turn produced. A career is that loop, repeated. The loops are rarely the same size, and they rarely point in exactly the same direction.

::: figure {#ch22.fig.loops src="ch22/loops.svg" size=wide}
Alt: Three loops in a row along a timeline labelled "years", each larger than the last. Each loop is a circle in three coloured arcs: coral for the snapshot game on top, teal for the long game on the right, purple for the career game on the left. The first, small loop is labelled "Corvid: tester"; the second, larger one "Lanternfish: developer, then senior"; the third, largest, drawn dashed because it has not happened yet, "next: staff". Arrows run from each loop to the next, and a note under the first arrow reads "what each loop made: skills, stories, people". At the bottom: "not a ladder: each turn starts from what the last one built".

A career as a sequence of loops. Each turn starts where the last one ended, with more material than before, and the loops get larger because each one is played from a stronger position.
:::

That picture has two practical consequences. The first is that the snapshot game never goes away, so the skills from Part I keep their value for the whole of your working life. The second is that the most important output of each loop is not the title you leave with but the material you carry into the next one: what you can do, what you can show, and who will vouch for you.

## Plateaus are normal {#ch22.sec.plateaus}

Every ladder-shaped career eventually stops going up. This is not a personal failing; it is arithmetic.

Organisations are pyramids. In the economists' model of promotion as a tournament, the prize at each level goes to whoever ranks first among the people competing for it, and the number of prizes shrinks as you climb [@lazear1981]. So most people, at some point, stop being promoted. The idea of a "career plateau" was introduced in the 1970s by researchers who described managerial careers as "early rapid upward mobility followed by inevitable leveling-off" [@ference1977].

What matters is how a plateau feels and what you do with it. A review of forty years of plateau research, covering 72 empirical sources, distinguished two kinds [@yang2019]:

- a **hierarchical plateau**, when promotions stop; and
- a **job-content plateau**, when learning stops.

Both went with lower satisfaction, well-being, performance and commitment, and with more intention to quit, largely because plateaued people felt less supported by their organisation. But the effects were weaker for people who cared less about promotion and where the organisation and the person actively worked against the plateau, and proactive behaviour and taking on added responsibilities went with less plateauing [@yang2019]. Most of those studies are cross-sectional, so they cannot show what causes what. The direction is still useful.

Put the two kinds together and you get a distinction worth keeping [!view]. The hierarchical plateau is mostly out of your hands: the pyramid narrows whatever you do. The content plateau is mostly in your hands, because you can usually find something new to learn without anyone's permission. A plateau in title with steady growth in what you can do is a perfectly good stretch of career, and often a better one than a fast climb that stops you learning.

::: trapdoor
**Mistaking a flat stretch for a dead end.** When promotions stop, it is tempting to read it as a verdict and either quit in a hurry or quietly stop trying. Before you do either, ask which plateau you are on. If you are still learning, you may simply be at the top of a narrow pyramid, and the right move might be sideways, or nowhere at all. If you have stopped learning, that is the problem to fix, and it can often be fixed in place (see job crafting, below) or with a lateral move. Leaving to escape a hierarchical plateau can land you on the same plateau one floor up, with less trust than you had.
:::

## Your tools expire; your judgement compounds {#ch22.sec.skills}

Over decades, some of what you know will become worth less. That is a real effect, and it has a slogan attached that is not real.

The real effect is the one [[ch20.sec.capital]] described: in fast-changing fields, the earnings premium for a degree in something like computer science falls by more than half over the first decade in the working-paper version of a large study of US job postings, because the skills jobs ask for change underneath it [@deming2020]. Seen over a whole career, the same study adds two things: in faster-changing careers the age-earnings profile is flatter, and people tend to move out of fast-changing occupations as they get older [@deming2020].

::: myth {#ch22.myth.half-life verdict=partly grade=strong} Your skills have a half-life of five years
The decay is real. The number is not. The labour-market evidence shows that skills tied to fast-changing work lose market value, fastest in technology [@deming2020]. But the "five-year half-life" traces back, as far as our researchers could follow it, to a hedged aside in talk slides by John Seely Brown: the half-life of skills is shrinking, "perhaps as low as 5 years", with no data or method behind it [@brown2026]. By 2010 it was circulating as a talk line [@campbell2010!], and it now appears with confident attributions that we could not trace to any primary report. Say instead: tool-specific skills lose value, sometimes quickly; there is no measured half-life.
:::

So what lasts? The same study suggests an answer: in the working-paper version, the authors find that ability matters relatively more in slower-changing careers because "learning gains accumulate" [@deming2020]. Our reading, which is a view rather than a finding [!view], is that the durable part of expertise is judgement: knowing which problem matters, how systems fail, how to work with people, how to learn the next tool quickly. The specific framework you use this year is a tool. Your ability to choose the right one, and to explain why, compounds. That is why [[ch20.sec.capital]] argued for skills that compound rather than skills that are fashionable.

Age itself is a smaller factor than many people fear: as [[ch20]] noted, it is largely unrelated to core task performance [@ng2008]. Where performance stalls with time, the better explanation is usually that people stop learning, not that they get older [!view]. A meta-analysis of work experience found that the *amount* of relevant experience predicted performance better than time served [@quinones1995], which is the point [[ch20.sec.experience]] makes.

## The middle of the road {#ch22.sec.middle}

Somewhere in the middle of a working life, many people hit a stretch when the question "is this it?" gets louder. There is a famous finding about this, and a famous argument about the finding.

In cross-sectional data from 145 countries, the relationship between age and happiness is U-shaped, with the low point around age 50, after controlling for education, marital status and employment [@blanchflower2021]. Critics point out that cross-sectional data compare different people at different ages, so they cannot show what happens to any one person over time; that the longitudinal evidence is mixed; that older adults often remember midlife as one of the better periods of their lives; and that a single average curve hides very different paths [@galambos2020].

The honest summary is that surveys find a midlife dip *on average*, and that many people do not follow the average [!view]. If you are in a low stretch, it is not a law of nature and it is not permanent. And it is a reasonable moment to ask the questions in the rest of this chapter, about what keeps you going and whether your job could be shaped to supply more of it.

That is roughly what Meera did. Her mug is a joke and an argument. The evidence on sabbaticals is limited, but one careful study is encouraging: in a quasi-experiment, 129 university academics on sabbatical were compared with 129 matched colleagues who were not. During the sabbatical, the people on leave lost fewer resources, gained more and reported higher well-being, and the comparison group showed no change; the gains were largest for people who felt in control, detached properly from work, and spent the time outside their home country [@davidson2010]. Academics take sabbaticals routinely, so this does not tell you what a career break would do for a software engineer, and the study does not say how long the benefits lasted. But it is a reason to treat rest as part of the work of a long career rather than a prize at the end of it, and it fits what [[ch17]] found about switching off [@sonnentag2012].

## What keeps people going {#ch22.sec.motivation}

Over a few months you can run on adrenaline, deadlines and the fear of looking bad. Over decades you can't. So what does the research say sustains people?

The best-supported framework is *self-determination theory*. Its founders' review of the research at work distinguishes **autonomous** motivation, doing something because you find it interesting or because you have made its value your own, from **controlled** motivation, doing it because of pressure, from outside or from inside yourself. Work that satisfies three basic needs, **competence** (getting better at something), **autonomy** (having some say in how you work) and **relatedness** (being connected to people), promotes autonomous motivation, better-quality performance and well-being [@deci2017].

This does not mean money is irrelevant, and the evidence says the two work together. A meta-analysis of 40 years of studies, covering 183 samples and more than 212,000 people, found that intrinsic motivation predicted performance whether or not incentives were present, that intrinsic motivation predicted the *quality* of performance better and incentives predicted its *quantity* better, and that intrinsic motivation mattered less when incentives were tied directly to performance [@cerasoli2014]. Pay for the work, and want to do it: both, not either.

### Meaningful work {#ch22.sec.meaning}

The strongest pattern in this area is about meaning. A meta-analysis of 44 articles, with more than 23,000 people, found that how meaningful people find their work correlates strongly with how engaged, committed and satisfied they are at work, more weakly with their health and life satisfaction, and only modestly with how well they say they perform [@allan2019].

::: figure {#ch22.fig.meaning src="ch22/meaning.svg"}
Alt: A bar chart headed "meaningful work goes with...", with three bars of decreasing length. The longest, dark purple, is labelled "large: r = .70 or more", next to "work engagement, commitment, job satisfaction". The middle bar is labelled "moderate to large: r about .44 to .49", next to "life satisfaction, meaning in life, general health, less wish to quit". The shortest is labelled "small to moderate: up to about .33", next to "helping beyond the role, self-rated performance, less bad mood". A note reads "bar length shows the size of the correlation; ranges from one meta-analysis of 44 articles, not individual values; performance is self-rated".

What meaningful work goes with. The correlations are ranges from one meta-analysis, and they are correlations: they show what travels together, not what causes what. Notice that the strongest links are with how people feel about work, and the weakest with how well they do it.
:::

Those are correlations, so they cannot show that meaningful work *causes* engagement; engaged people may also find their work more meaningful. But the shape is worth noticing. Meaning goes with how work feels far more than with how well it is done, which is a reason to want it for your own sake rather than as a productivity trick.

Meaning has a cost, and the research is clear about that too. Zookeepers with a strong sense of calling found deep meaning in their work, and were also more likely to treat it as a moral duty and to sacrifice pay, personal time and comfort for it [@bunderson2009]. In a series of experiments, observers judged unpaid extra hours and demeaning tasks as more legitimate when the worker was seen as passionate, assuming such workers would volunteer anyway [@kim2020]. And people accept lower pay for work they find meaningful [@hu2017]. Put plainly: meaningful work is good for you, and it is also the easiest thing for an employer to underpay. Love the work, and still negotiate ([[ch09.sec.should]]), and still keep the boundaries from [[ch17]].

::: myth {#ch22.myth.passion verdict=partly grade=moderate} Find your passion first, and the rest will follow
[[ch20.sec.choosing]] met the evidence on this when Dev was choosing a skill; here it is as a verdict on the slogan. Believing that a passion is something you *find*, already formed, has costs: in five studies, mostly with students, people who saw interests as fixed lost interest faster when a new interest became difficult, and, as the authors put it, urging people to find their passion may lead them "to put all their eggs in one basket but then to drop that basket when it becomes difficult to carry" [@okeefe2018]. But the opposite slogan is not proven either: people who believe passion comes from finding the right fit and people who believe it is developed over time can both reach well-being and success at work [@chen2015]. The popular argument that skill should come before passion [@newport2012!] is a practitioner's case, not a finding. The honest version: interest often grows with competence and investment, and you rarely know in advance where it will grow.
:::

## Money, over decades {#ch22.sec.money}

Money matters, and it matters in a particular way over a long career.

The old claim that happiness stops rising above an income of about $75,000 came from a 2010 US study, in which emotional well-being showed "no further progress" beyond that level [@kahneman2010]. Later work with 1.7 million moment-by-moment reports from more than 33,000 employed US adults found well-being rising with the logarithm of income, with no plateau above $75,000 [@killingsworth2021]. When Killingsworth re-analysed the data together with Kahneman, one of the 2010 authors, and a third researcher, they found that happiness flattened at around $100,000 only for the least happy 15 to 20% of people, while for the majority it kept rising [@killingsworth2023]. "Rising with the logarithm" means that each doubling of income is worth about the same: going from 50,000 to 100,000 matters about as much as going from 100,000 to 200,000. These are American, correlational findings, so treat the dollar figures as local.

Two other findings are more useful for decisions. People adapt to pay rises: in German panel data, happiness adapted to income gains within about four years, though not to gains in status [@ditella2010], and people who stayed in the same job became less satisfied over time even as their wages rose [@grund2007]. And losses hurt more than gains help: falls in income reduced well-being by more than equal rises increased it, in large German and British samples [@boyce2013].

The practical rules that follow are our view, built on those findings [!view]. Do not take a job you would dislike for a raise you will get used to in a few years. Take seriously the parts of a job that do not wear off: security, the status of the work, and how you spend your days. And be wary of moves that trade a stable income for a volatile one unless the upside is large, because the bad years will hurt more than the good ones help.

## Shape the job you have {#ch22.sec.crafting}

Before you decide that the problem is the job, it is worth asking how much of the job you could change from inside it.

Amy Wrzesniewski and Jane Dutton called this **job crafting**: employees reshape their work by changing its *task* boundaries (what they do, and how much of each thing), its *relational* boundaries (whom they work with, and how) and its *cognitive* boundaries (how they think about what the work is for), and in doing so change both what the work means to them and who they are at work [@wrzesniewski2001]. That was a theory. Later intervention studies tested it: across 14 studies, training people to craft their jobs increased crafting (g = 0.26), work engagement (g = 0.31) and contextual performance, the helpful behaviour around the core job (g = 0.39); gains in core task performance appeared only among healthcare workers [@oprea2019]. Those are small to moderate effects from a small set of studies with wide uncertainty. But they are real, and they come from changes people made themselves.

Even a job title can be crafted. In one study at a health-care system, employees who wrote their own self-reflective job titles reported less emotional exhaustion five weeks later [@grant2014].

Dev has been doing this for years without a name for it. At Corvid she turned a testing job into one that touched the payments code. At Lanternfish she turned an old tester's instinct for how things break into the testability work of [[ch20]], and the relationships she built through it became the squad she was offered in [[ch21]]. Crafting is also the cheapest answer to a content plateau: you rarely need permission to start learning something next to your job.

:::: move {#ch22.move.craft} Craft your current job
Draw three columns: **tasks**, **people**, **meaning**. In each, write one change you could make in the next month without anyone's permission and one you would need to ask for.

- *Tasks*: something to do more of, less of, or differently.
- *People*: someone to work with more, or a different way of working with someone you already do.
- *Meaning*: a more accurate answer to "who does this work help, and how?"

Then pick one change from the "no permission" row and put it in your diary.

::: answer
Good answers are small and specific. Dev's, at the start of her last year at Lanternfish:

- **Tasks.** *No permission:* spend Friday mornings turning the most common on-call failure into an automated check. *Ask:* own the release-safety review for the whole platform team.
- **People.** *No permission:* pair once a fortnight with the newest engineer. *Ask:* a monthly hour with the head of customer support, who sees the failures first.
- **Meaning.** "I make sure warehouses get the right shipments" is truer, and more motivating, than "I write tests".

Weak answers are vague ("be more strategic") or are really requests for a different job. If every change you can think of needs permission, or none of them would help, you may have learned something important: the problem is not the shape of the job but the job, and the last section of this chapter is for you.
:::

## What success looks like from the inside {#ch22.sec.success}

There is one more finding worth having before the next decision, because it explains why people with apparently successful careers can feel stuck, and vice versa.

A meta-analysis of the predictors of career success separated **objective** success (pay and promotions) from **subjective** success (how satisfied people are with their careers). Human capital, such as education and experience, and demographics related more strongly to the objective kind; organisational sponsorship and stable personal traits related more strongly to the subjective kind [@ng2005]. In plain words: the things that get you paid and promoted are not quite the same things that make you satisfied with how it went. You can be good at one and poor at the other.

That is the career-game version of the thesis of this book. The trailer, the film and the review are different things judged by different audiences; so are your pay slip, your title and your own sense of whether your working life is being well spent. Meera's three regrets were all about the last of those. None of them would show up on a CV.

## The next move, from strength {#ch22.sec.strength}

Which brings us back to the recruiter.

::: scene {slug="INT. DEV'S FLAT — WEDNESDAY, 6:40 PM"}
The call is from a recruiter at Kestrel, a payments company about four times Lanternfish's size. She has done her homework: she has read Dev's public write-up on designing services to be testable before they are built, which a friend of a friend shared, and she has spoken to someone who worked with Dev at Corvid.

@dev: Can I ask how you found me?

"Your name came up twice in a week, from two different people. That doesn't happen often." A pause, and the sound of a screen being scrolled. "Sorry, I have to ask. Dev, as in developer?"

@dev: Staff engineer, actually. Or that's the job you're calling about, isn't it?

The recruiter laughs. The role is a staff engineer leading testability and release safety across a dozen teams: exactly the work Dev has spent two years building, at three times the scale. Dev says she is interested, and that she will need a week before deciding whether to start a process.

After the call she does something she has not done for four years. She opens the old spreadsheet and clicks on the first tab, the one she renamed *Game 1* on a Sunday night in [[ch01]]. Forty-seven rows. Forty-six noes.

She adds row 48: *Kestrel, staff engineer. Came to me.*
:::

Look at the difference between row 47 and row 48, because it is the whole book. The first time, Dev was a stranger to every employer she applied to, with a résumé of duties and nobody inside to vouch for her. This time the employer came to her, because people who had watched her film talked about it, and because she had made some of it visible in public. That is the loop working: the long game and the career game producing the raw material of the next snapshot.

Back to the start of Part I, from strength. The snapshot game has not changed. Dev's position in it has, because three years of long game and career game produced the evidence, the stories and the people that the first search lacked.

Playing from strength does not mean skipping the method. It means the method gets easier, and the answers more honest. Dev goes back to [[ch02]] and runs the same questions she ran four years earlier.

**Push or pull?** Mostly pull: the work at Kestrel is her work at a scale Lanternfish does not have. There is one push: the staff role she wants at Lanternfish depends on a reorganisation that has slipped twice.

**Ask before you leave.** She asks. Tomás is straight with her, which is why she trusts him: the staff role might come "next cycle", and he gives her a date and a condition, which is a real maybe in the sense of [[ch02.sec.ask]]. It is also eight months away and depends on a budget neither of them controls.

**Does switching pay?** In US data, job switchers' wage growth usually beats stayers', but by a small margin on average, and the gap shrinks when the market is slack [@atlantafed-wgt]. External hires are typically paid more than internal promotees, around 18% more in one well-known study, but are rated lower for their first two years, because they start the long game from zero [@bidwell2011]. Dev knows exactly what that means: at Kestrel, nobody has seen her film.

**Search from strength.** She is employed, which helps: employed job seekers get more offers per application and better-paid ones [@faberman2017]. She can afford to run a real process, ask hard questions, and say no.

**The door you leave through.** People who return to a former employer tend to perform about as well as other hires at first but improve less and are more likely to leave again, often for the same reasons [@arnold2021]. If she goes, she wants the pull to be real, so that she is not back in two years, and she wants to leave well, so that she could be.

4. **One honest gap**: something the job needs that your film does not yet show.

::: answer
Dev's version:

1. "I make systems testable before they are built: I run testability design reviews across Lanternfish's platform, and I lead the reliability squad's technical work."
2. Evidence: *reviewed more than forty service designs for testability before they were built, with a log of which suggestions squads adopted; cut failed releases from roughly one a week to one a quarter by adding pre-release checks to every service; wrote the internal guide to testable services that new engineers now start with.*
3. Names: Tomás, and the engineer she named as the squad's lead in [[ch21]].
4. Gap: "I've never set technical direction across a dozen teams I don't work in every day."

If you struggled with item 2, the fix is not a better adjective; it is the *Wins* habit from [[ch11]], started today. If you struggled with item 3, the fix is the long game ([[ch10]]). And item 4 is not a weakness to hide. Saying it plainly is a strong signal in its own right: it shows you know the difference between your trailer and your film.
:::

A trailer is half the preparation. The other half is the decision itself, run properly, as Dev ran it.

:::: move {#ch22.move.revisited} Stay, go or grow, revisited
Run the decision path from [[ch02.fig.decision]] on your current job, today, even if you are not thinking of leaving. For each step, write one line:

1. What is pushing you, and what is pulling you?
2. For each push: could it be fixed here? Have you asked, with a date?
3. For each pull: is it on offer here?
4. Which plateau, if any, are you on: title or learning?
5. If you went, what would you take with you: skills, stories, people?

::: answer
The useful outcome is not a decision; it is knowing what would change your mind. Most people who do this find they are in one of three places. **Stay, and craft**: the pushes are fixable or small, the pulls are available inside, and the job-crafting exercise above has work to do. **Grow**: the pull is real and on offer inside, so ask for it, with a date. **Go, from strength**: the pull is real, it is not on offer, and question 5 has good answers. If question 5 has thin answers, that is information too: the next year's work is to build them, wherever you are.

Dev's answers: 1, pull (scale) and one push (a stalled role); 2, asked, with a date, eight months out; 3, not at Lanternfish's size; 4, neither, yet; 5, plenty. Which is why she says yes to the process.
:::

Kestrel's process takes five weeks. It is the same game as four years ago, and it feels entirely different: Dev tells stories from her story bank instead of inventing answers in the room, asks the questions on her *Questions for them* tab ([[ch07.sec.ask-them]]), scopes the design exercise before starting it ([[ch08.sec.take-home]]), and negotiates once, calmly, with numbers ([[ch09.sec.dev-negotiates]]). When the offer comes, she tells Tomás first, in person, on a Monday morning rather than a Friday afternoon. He is disappointed and not surprised, and he says the thing good managers say: "If it doesn't work out, call me." She hands over the testability reviews with a written guide and two weeks of pairing, because the door she leaves through stays in the building.

::: rewind
- **A career is a sequence of loops, not a ladder.** People change jobs often, and much of what they learn is tied to an occupation. Each loop starts from the skills, stories and people the last one produced.
- **Plateaus are normal.** Pyramids narrow, so promotions stop. A plateau in title with growth in skill is fine; a plateau in learning is the one to fix.
- **Your tools expire; your judgement compounds.** Fast-changing skills lose market value, but the "five-year half-life" is a guess that became a statistic. Age is largely unrelated to core task performance.
- **The midlife dip is an average, and a contested one.** Rest is maintenance, not a reward for finishing.
- **What sustains people:** competence, autonomy and relatedness. Intrinsic motivation and pay work together. Meaningful work goes strongly with engagement and satisfaction, and is also easy for employers to underpay.
- **"Find your passion" is half right:** interest often grows with competence and investment.
- **Money:** happiness keeps rising with income for most people, but you adapt to raises within a few years and losses hurt more than gains help. Don't take a job you dislike for a raise.
- **Craft the job you have** (tasks, people, meaning) before deciding it is the wrong job. When you do move, make the move from strength: the same questions as [[ch02]], with better answers.
:::
