Dev is not in crisis. Nobody is shouting at her; nobody would describe her job as bad. She likes most of the people and most of the work. She is simply doing slightly too much of the wrong kind of thing, for slightly too long, and it is starting to show: in the evenings she spends answering messages, in the irritation she feels when the channel lights up, in the small voice that has started asking what the point is.

That is what pressure usually looks like at work. Not a dramatic collapse, but a slow drift, in workload, in boundaries and, sometimes, in standards. This last chapter of the long game is about holding your shape under that drift: what burnout actually is, why helpful people get buried, how good people end up doing things they would once have refused, and how to tell when the job itself is the problem.

## What burnout is, and what it is not {#ch17.sec.burnout}

"Burnout" gets used for everything from a bad week to a serious collapse, which makes it hard to think about. It helps to start with the definition.

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases describes burn-out as an **occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition**: a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It has three dimensions: feelings of exhaustion; increased mental distance from the job, or feelings of negativity or cynicism about it; and reduced professional efficacy. It applies specifically to work, not to the rest of life [@who2019]. That matches the model Christina Maslach and colleagues developed over decades, which places burnout in the *relationship* between a person and their work, rather than only inside the person [@maslach2001].

Two consequences follow. The first is that burnout is not weakness. The definition itself says it comes from chronic stress at work that was not successfully managed, which is a statement about a situation, not a character. The second is that you should be very sceptical of headline statistics. A review of 182 studies of physician burnout, covering more than 109,000 doctors in 45 countries, found prevalence estimates ranging from 0% to 80.5%, because the studies used at least 142 different definitions [@rotenstein2018]. When an article says "X% of workers are burned out", the first question is: by whose definition?

### Demands and resources {#ch17.sec.jdr}

What causes it? The most widely used model, the **Job Demands-Resources** model, says that strain comes mainly from high *demands* (workload, time pressure, emotional effort, conflict), while motivation comes mainly from *resources* (control over how you work, support from colleagues and managers, feedback, chances to learn), and that resources buffer the damage demands do [@bakker2007]. Its authors present it as deliberately flexible [@bakker2007], and that flexibility makes it hard to prove wrong [!view]. But as a way of looking at your own job, it is extremely useful, because it says there are two ways to feel better: reduce demands, or increase resources.

On the right side, teal weights labelled with resources: "control over how you work", "support", "feedback", "switching off". The seesaw tips to the left. Demands and resources. Strain comes mostly from the left side and motivation mostly from the right, and the right side buffers the left. Dev's week has piled up demands while her resources stayed the same, so the most useful question is not "how do I cope?" but "which weights can I move?"
:::

Some demands have particularly good evidence behind them:

- **Long hours.** A joint WHO and International Labour Organization analysis attributed an estimated 745,194 deaths from heart disease and stroke in 2016 to working 55 or more hours a week, when 8.9% of the world's population worked such hours [@pega2021]. That is a population estimate, not a prediction for any individual, but it is a reminder that long hours are not merely a matter of feeling tired.
- **Being expected to be always on.** In three studies, organisational expectations that employees monitor email after hours raised their anxiety and harmed their health and relationship satisfaction, and their partners' too, even apart from how much time they actually spent on email [@becker2021]. A survey of 570 working adults found the same expectation working through worse detachment and emotional exhaustion [@belkin2020]. The *expectation* is the problem, not just the email.
- **Faking feelings.** As [[ch10]] showed, surface acting, displaying emotions you do not feel, is substantially associated with impaired well-being [@hulsheger2011]. A job that requires a cheerful mask all day is a demanding job, whatever its hours.

And the resource with the best evidence is surprisingly simple. Mentally detaching from work in your own time, not just being away from it but not thinking about it, goes with better life satisfaction and less strain, without any loss of engagement at work [@sonnentag2012]. Switching off is not slacking; it is part of the work.

There is one fix for the always-on problem that anyone can apply to other people tomorrow. In eight pre-registered studies with more than 4,000 people, recipients of non-urgent emails sent outside working hours overestimated how quickly the sender expected a reply, which stressed them; a single line from the sender saying a reply was not urgent reduced that stress [@giurge2021]. If you send messages late, add the line. It costs five words.

### What actually helps {#ch17.sec.helps}

If you look for advice on burnout, most of what you find is about the individual: sleep, exercise, meditation, resilience. Those are worth doing for their own sake. But the best evidence on interventions points somewhere else. A meta-analysis of controlled trials of interventions to reduce burnout in physicians found that they helped only modestly overall (a standardised mean difference of −0.29), and that interventions directed at the *organisation* (changes to workload, scheduling, teamwork) worked better (−0.45) than interventions directed at the *individual* (−0.18) [@panagioti2017]. That is doctors, not everyone, and the studies were few. But it fits the definition: if burnout comes from the relationship between a person and their job, changing only the person is half a fix.

So the most useful thing you can do about your own strain is often the least comfortable: talk to the person who can change the demands. That is usually your manager, and it is a workload conversation, not a confession [!view].

:::: move {#ch17.move.inventory} A burnout inventory
This is not a diagnostic test, and no score here means you are or are not burned out. It is a way of seeing your own situation through the two ideas above. Use the last month.

**Part A: the three dimensions.** For each, choose *rarely*, *sometimes*, *often* or *most days*.

1. I feel drained by work, and rest doesn't fully refill me.
2. I feel distant, cynical or negative about my job, or about the people it serves.
3. I feel less effective at work than I used to be.

**Part B: the seesaw.** Write down your three heaviest demands and your three most important resources (or the resources you most lack). For each demand, mark it *reducible*, *movable to someone else* or *fixed*.

::: answer
Read the pattern, not a total.

- **Mostly "exhaustion", with cynicism and effectiveness intact?** That usually points at the demand side: too much, for too long. Your *reducible* and *movable* demands from Part B are the agenda for a workload conversation, which is the kind of organisational fix the intervention research favours [@panagioti2017].
- **Cynicism high?** That dimension is about your relationship with the job [@maslach2001]. Ask what changed: a decision you disagree with, a feeling of being disrespected, a role you never chose. Those are worth naming to someone who can change them.
- **Effectiveness low?** Look at the resource side: feedback, support and control are the resources the model says buffer demands [@bakker2007]. A job with no feedback can make a competent person feel incompetent.
- **All three, most days, for months, and spreading into the rest of your life?** Take it seriously. Talk to your manager about the job, and to a doctor or other clinician about you. [[more.sec.health]] has starting points.

If Part B has no *reducible* or *movable* items at all, that is information too, and the end of this chapter is about what to do with it.
:::
::::

Before acting on any of this, two popular ideas about tiredness and positivity are worth checking.

::: myth {#ch17.myth.willpower verdict=partly grade=strong} Willpower is a tank that runs dry
The idea that self-control draws on a limited resource that gets used up ("ego depletion") was one of the most popular findings in psychology, and it is often used to explain why people are exhausted by the end of the day. A registered replication across 23 laboratories, with 2,141 participants following one agreed protocol, found an effect close to zero (d = 0.04) [@hagger2016]. That replication tested one protocol, and researchers still argue about whether other versions of the effect exist, which is why the verdict is "partly" rather than "busted". But the strong claim, that willpower is a fuel tank, is not supported. If you are worn out by work, the cause is far more likely to be the demands and the lack of recovery than a depleted inner reserve.
:::

A second idea from popular resilience training deserves the same treatment.

::: myth {#ch17.myth.positivity verdict=busted grade=strong} Flourishing needs a positivity ratio of about 3 to 1
A 2005 paper claimed that people flourish when their ratio of positive to negative emotions is above about 2.9 [@fredrickson2005], and the same mathematics had been used to claim a similar ratio for high-performing teams [@brown2013]. In 2013, a critique showed that the fluid-dynamics mathematics behind both ratios had been misapplied, and concluded that the claim to have found a critical positivity ratio was "entirely unfounded" [@brown2013], and the journal went on to publish a formal correction to the 2005 paper [@fredrickson2013]. That does not mean positive emotions are unimportant, or that negative interactions don't weigh more than positive ones; there is good evidence that bad experiences often have more impact than good ones [@baumeister2001]. It means there is no magic number, and no reason to count.
:::

## The helping trap {#ch17.sec.helping}

Look again at Dev's calendar. Very little of it is unreasonable on its own. Covering two interviews is helpful. Reviewing a colleague's code is part of the job. Running onboarding for testers uses her QA background. Keeping the coffee machine alive is, frankly, a public service. The problem is the total, and what the total is made of.

The research on helping beyond your role, what organisational psychologists call **citizenship behaviour**, has a reassuring half and a worrying half, and [[ch10.sec.reciprocity]] gave a first look at both. The reassuring half: across 168 samples and more than 51,000 people, citizenship behaviour went with better performance ratings from managers, more rewards, and lower turnover and absence [@podsakoff2009]. (Some of that may be halo: managers who like you rate both your helpfulness and your performance highly.) The worrying half: in a study of 98 couples, people whose spouses rated them high on individual initiative reported more role overload, job stress and work-family conflict, the last of these more strongly for women [@bolino2005]. And a theory paper points out the paradox: time spent helping comes out of time for your core work, and since reviews and promotions mostly reward the core work, helping too much can quietly hurt a career [@bergeron2007].

Not all helping is equal, either. In field and laboratory studies, researchers found that women were asked more often than men to do tasks that matter to an organisation but do little for the person's advancement (sitting on committees, writing reports), and accepted such requests more often, driven by a shared expectation that women would say yes [@babcock2017]. They called these **low-promotability tasks**. Many people now call the wider category **glue work**: the coordinating, onboarding, documenting, note-taking and fixing that holds a team together and rarely appears in anyone's promotion case.

::: figure {#ch17.fig.helping src="ch17/helping.svg"}
Alt: A loop of five arrows. "Someone asks for help" leads to "you say yes (it's quicker than explaining)", which leads to "you become 'the person who' does it", which leads to "more people ask", which leads to "less time for the work your review rewards", and back round to "someone asks for help". In the middle of the loop, Dev holds a torch and a wrench beside a small drawing of Old Faithful. A gold exit arrow breaks out of the loop at "you become 'the person who'", labelled "hand it over: write it down, give it an owner".

The helping trap. Each step is reasonable; the loop is not. The best place to break it is at "the person who", before the role hardens: write down what you know, and give the task a real owner.
:::

Dev's week fits the loop exactly. She fixed Old Faithful once, became the person who fixes it, and is now asked several times a week. She ran onboarding once because she had the slides; now it is hers. None of it is in her review, and all of it comes out of the 4 pm block marked *actual work*.

::: coming {ref=ch19}
In [[ch19]], glue work meets the promotion committee. Some of it counts and much of it does not, and the chapter looks at how to tell which is which, and how to make the parts that matter visible.
:::

The way out is not to stop helping, which would make you a worse colleague and, on the evidence above, probably a lower-rated one. It is to choose which help to give and to stop the rest from hardening into a role. And that means saying no more often than feels natural.

### Saying no {#ch17.sec.no}

Saying no costs less than it feels, as [[ch12.sec.no]] showed. People asked to help find it awkward to refuse, which is why requesters underestimate how often others will say yes [@flynn2008]. And people who decline overestimate how much it will hurt: in experiments on social invitations, people who said no expected the person inviting them to be more upset, and less likely to invite them again, than the inviters actually were [@givi2024]. That research was about invitations rather than work requests, so treat it as a strong hint rather than proof. But it points the same way as Dev's experience the first time she tries it.

A good no has three parts, in our experience: a clear answer, a reason tied to priorities rather than feelings, and, when you can, a smaller alternative [!view]. "I can't take the two interviews this week; I'm behind on the reconciliation work Kai asked me to finish. I can do one next week if that helps" is a no that leaves the relationship intact. "I'm so sorry, I'm just really swamped, maybe?" is not a no at all, and the person asking will hear a yes.

:::: move {#ch17.move.boundary} Your boundary sentence
Write the sentence you will use, in your own words, for each of these. Keep each to one or two sentences.

1. A colleague asks you to "quickly" review something large, for the third time this month.
2. Your manager messages at 9:30 pm with a question that is not urgent.
3. You have become the unofficial owner of something that is not your job (a machine, a spreadsheet, a ritual) and want to hand it back.
4. Someone asks you to join a committee that sounds worthwhile and will take two hours a week.

::: answer
Sample sentences, and why they work:

1. "I can't do a full review of 900 lines this week. If you split out the risky part, I'll look at that properly by Thursday." A no to the size, a yes to the part that matters, with a date.
2. Answer in the morning, or, if it really is quick: "Answering now since it's quick, but I'm offline in the evenings from next week." The research suggests that what hurts is the *expectation* of after-hours monitoring [@becker2021], so the useful move is to reset the expectation, kindly, once.
3. "I've written up how to reset it and pinned it in the channel. From next month I'd like facilities to own it; can we agree that?" Writing it down is what breaks the "person who" step of the helping trap; naming an owner is what makes it stick.
4. "It sounds worthwhile. Before I say yes, can I check with my manager how it fits with my priorities?" This is not stalling. Committees are the textbook example of a low-promotability task [@babcock2017], so it is worth deciding deliberately, and asking whether the work will be recognised, rather than saying yes because it is hard to say no.

The common thread: each sentence is specific, ties the boundary to something real (time, priorities, ownership) and, where possible, offers something smaller.
:::
::::

On Monday, Dev writes a one-page guide to E-47 (hold for eleven seconds, kick gently, call the service number if it happens twice in a day), pins it in the channel, and asks the facilities manager to own the machine from the first of the month. He says yes in four minutes. Two people react to the post with a crying emoji. Nobody dies.

## The first small step {#ch17.sec.slope}

Workload is one kind of pressure. There is another, which is rarer and more dangerous: pressure to do something you would not normally do.

::: scene {slug="INT. LANTERNFISH, THE RELEASE ROOM — WEDNESDAY, 2 PM"}
A large warehouse client goes live on the new reconciliation system on Friday. The product director has promised the date to the client's chief executive. The release pipeline is green except for one test, which fails about one run in five.

@rex: That one's flaky. It's been flaky for weeks. Mark it as a known issue and we ship.

Dev has spent three years of her life telling flaky tests from real failures, and she is fairly sure this one is real: when it fails, a small number of shipments appear twice in the client's ledger. She also notices something she would rather not. Two weeks ago, the squad skipped a performance test "just this once". Last week, the status report to the client said green for an item that was amber, because it was "basically fine". Each step was small. Each made the next one feel normal.

@dev: I don't think it's flaky. I think it's duplicating shipments under load. If we ship it, the client's numbers will be wrong, and they won't know.

The room goes quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has made Friday more complicated.
:::

Very few people set out to do something wrong at work. The research on how they end up doing it anyway is sobering, because it describes ordinary people in ordinary situations.

In four multi-round experiments, David Welsh and colleagues found that people who were led into dishonesty through small transgressions that grew gradually were more likely to commit a large transgression later than people faced with the large one straight away, an effect explained by **moral disengagement**, the gradual rationalising that lets people stop seeing an act as wrong [@welsh2015]. That is the **slippery slope**, and it explains how a skipped test becomes a misreported status, which becomes a shipped defect, which becomes a client's wrong numbers. A related theory paper describes **ethical fading**: self-deception that lets the ethical side of a decision fade from view, so that people act unethically without experiencing it as an ethical choice at all [@tenbrunsel2004]. Nobody in the release room thinks they are discussing ethics. They think they are discussing a flaky test.

The situation matters as much as the people. The meta-analysis from [[ch16.sec.wrong]] found that unethical choices come from the person, the issue and the environment together [@kishgephart2010], and a contested critique of goal-setting argues that goals which are too specific or too hard, like a fixed date promised to a client's chief executive, can narrow attention and increase unethical behaviour among their other side effects [@ordonez2009]. Pressure rarely announces itself as an ethics test. It arrives as a deadline.

::: figure {#ch17.fig.slope src="ch17/slope.svg"}
Alt: A staircase running down from left to right. At the top, a flat landing labelled "your line, set in advance", with a gold gate drawn across it. The steps going down are numbered 1 to 4, with a key underneath: "skip one test, just this once", "report amber as green: it's basically fine", "call a real failure flaky", and, at the bottom in coral, "the client's numbers are wrong, and they don't know". Each step down is only a little lower than the one before. Dev stands at the gate. A note beside the steps says "each step makes the next one feel normal".

The first small step. No single step on this staircase feels like a big decision, which is exactly why it works. The defence is a line you drew before you were standing on the stairs.
:::

The defence the research points to is unglamorous: notice the first small step, and decide your lines in advance, in calm conditions, rather than in the release room on a Wednesday afternoon [!view]. People who have thought in advance about what they will not do are not immune to pressure, but they recognise the staircase when they see it.

What Dev does next is the same pattern as a good no: a clear answer, a reason tied to shared goals and an alternative. She does not accuse anyone. She says what she believes is happening, what it would do to the client, and what she suggests instead: ship on Friday with the reconciliation feature switched off for the duplicate-prone shipment type, fix it by Tuesday, and tell the client today, before Friday, exactly what is happening and why.

Tomás, who has been listening from the doorway, asks one question.

@tomas: How sure are you it's real?

@dev: Sure enough that I can show you in twenty minutes.

She shows him in fifteen. He calls the product director. The client's chief executive, told on a Wednesday instead of discovering it in a month, is irritated for an afternoon and grateful by Monday. Tomás, it turns out, hates Friday surprises a great deal more than he hates Wednesday ones, a preference Dev first learned about the hard way ([[ch12]]).

Not every story ends this well. Sometimes the person who says no is overruled; sometimes they pay for it. The research on voice in [[ch16.sec.voice]] is honest that challenging the people above you carries risk [@burris2012]. But the rungs of the escalation ladder exist for exactly this reason, and so does the pre-commitment below. And in a repeated game, being known as the person who will not sign a false number is worth a great deal, including to the people who were annoyed with you on the Wednesday.

:::: move {#ch17.move.precommit} What would you refuse to do?
In calm conditions (now is good), write three short lines that start "I will not...", about your own work. They should be specific enough that you would recognise the first small step towards each one. Then, for each, write the first small step.

::: answer
There is no right list; strong ones are concrete and tied to your actual job. For example:

- "I will not tell a customer something is tested when it isn't." First small step: agreeing to describe a skipped test as "covered".
- "I will not change a number in a report to make it look better than it is." First small step: rounding an awkward figure in the direction that helps, "just for the slide".
- "I will not ship something I believe will harm users without telling someone who can stop it." First small step: deciding not to mention a doubt because the release is close.

What makes these work is the second line: naming the first small step turns an abstract principle into something you can notice in the moment, which is exactly where the slippery-slope research says people go wrong [@welsh2015]. Keep the list somewhere you will see it. If you never need it, nothing is lost.
:::
::::

## When the job is the problem {#ch17.sec.leave}

Sometimes boundaries, conversations and a better no are not enough, because the problem is the job itself: the demands are too high and are not going to fall, the resources are missing and are not going to arrive, or the culture keeps asking for the first small step.

When people do leave, they usually leave for concrete reasons. The Pew survey from [[ch02.sec.why-leave]] found that US workers who quit a job in 2021 most often cited low pay, no opportunities for advancement and feeling disrespected at work, followed by childcare problems, inflexible hours and poor benefits [@pew2022]. Those are self-reported reasons, from one country, but they are a useful checklist: if three or four of them describe your job, and they are not changing, the problem is probably not your resilience.

Some signals that it might be time to go, which are our synthesis rather than a validated test [!view]:

- You have raised the workload or the problem clearly, at least twice, with the person who can change it, and nothing has changed.
- You are regularly asked to take the first small step, and the people asking do not seem to notice.
- The three burnout dimensions (exhaustion, distance or cynicism, and a sense that you are no longer good at the job) have lasted for months, not weeks, and are spreading into the rest of your life.
- You no longer recognise the person you are at work, and you do not like them.

If those describe you, it is worth remembering where this book started. Leaving a job is not failure; it is the loop from [[ch01.sec.loop]]. [[ch02]] is about making that decision well, and [[ch22]] comes back to the same decision years later, made from strength. If any of it is affecting your health, a doctor or other clinician is the right person to talk to, not a book.

::: field {regions="UK, EU, US, India"} Working hours and switching off
**United Kingdom.** You cannot work more than 48 hours a week on average, normally averaged over 17 weeks, unless you choose to opt out. The opt-out must be voluntary and in writing, you cannot be dismissed or treated unfairly for refusing to sign it, and you can cancel it at any time with at least seven days' notice (or up to three months, if your agreement says so). Some jobs are exceptions [@govuk-working-hours].

**European Union.** The Working Time Directive requires member states to limit average working time to 48 hours a week *including overtime*, to guarantee at least 11 consecutive hours of rest in every 24, and to give at least four weeks of paid annual leave; national law sets the details and some states allow individual opt-outs under strict conditions. There is no EU-wide "right to disconnect" law yet: as of our check, the European Commission had consulted employers' and workers' representatives on one (that consultation ended in October 2025) and planned a proposal for a broader Quality Jobs Act by the end of 2026. Some member states have their own rules, which we did not check [@eu-wtd2003].

**United States.** Federal law sets no limit on the number of hours employees aged 16 and over may work. Covered employees who are not exempt must be paid at least one and a half times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek; many salaried professionals are exempt, and states can add rules [@dol-flsa-hours].

**India.** Under the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, which took effect with the other labour codes on 21 November 2025, the Labour Ministry describes 8 hours a day and 48 hours a week as the standard, with overtime paid at twice the normal rate. We could not verify how far this covers office and IT staff, or how it interacts with state Shops and Establishments Acts [@india-osh-code].

For other countries, and for what applies to your own contract, check an official source.
:::

## Answering the pause {#ch17.sec.pause}

At the start of the chapter you guessed what mostly caused the last stretch of feeling worn out by work: the amount of work, something about the work, or something about you. If you guessed "me", it is worth knowing that this is the answer the evidence supports least. The WHO definition locates burnout in chronic workplace stress [@who2019], the best-studied model puts it in the balance of demands and resources [@bakker2007], and interventions aimed at the organisation beat interventions aimed at the person [@panagioti2017]. Your habits matter at the margin. The job matters more.

::: ask
Q: Isn't saying no just going to make me look less committed?

A: It might, a little, in the moment [!view]. But the evidence suggests people overestimate how much refusals cost [@givi2024], and a theory paper argues that helping beyond your role comes at the cost of your core work, which is what reviews mostly reward [@bergeron2007]. A clear, well-reasoned no, with an alternative, usually reads as someone who knows their priorities.

Q: I can't afford to leave. What then?

A: Then the levers are the ones in this chapter: move demands off the scale where you can, add resources where you can (support, control, recovery), have the workload conversation with the person who can change it, and start rebuilding your options in the background. [[ch02]] covers searching while employed, which is easier than searching after leaving.
:::

Which brings us back to Lanternfish, and to Friday.

::: scene {slug="INT. DEV'S FLAT — FRIDAY, 6:15 PM"}
Release day. The reconciliation system went live at noon, with one feature switched off and a client who knows why. Nothing is on fire. Dev left the office at 5:30, which she has not done on a Friday in months.

Old Faithful, under new management, has been working all week. The facilities manager has posted a photo of himself holding the descale button, captioned *eleven seconds, apparently*.

She opens her spreadsheet and scrolls to the *Wins* tab. She adds a line: *Caught a duplicate-shipment defect before go-live; proposed the flag-off release; client informed Wednesday.* She looks at the tab for a while. There is nothing on it about coffee, and nothing about the 900-line review, or the onboarding sessions, or the interviews she covered. There are a lot of weeks on it with nothing at all.

Her first annual review with Tomás is in six weeks.

She closes the laptop, puts her phone in a drawer, and goes out.
:::

That is the end of Part II, and of Dev's first fourteen months at Lanternfish. She arrived as Corporate Dev, stiff and afraid to ask. She leaves Part II as someone with a register, a friend with rules, a record of what she did, the habit of fighting about the work and not the people, and a few lines she will not cross. That is the long game played well: not a performance, but a set of defaults that hold under pressure.

It is also, she is about to find out, only half of what it takes for good work to count. The long game builds the film. The career game decides who sees it, and what they write about it. Part III begins with the most ordinary and most consequential piece of that game, the annual review, in [[ch18]].

::: rewind
- **Burnout is an occupational phenomenon, not a weakness:** chronic workplace stress that wasn't managed, with exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy. Distrust headline percentages; definitions vary wildly.
- **Strain comes from demands, motivation from resources, and resources buffer demands.** Long hours, always-on expectations and faking feelings are demands with strong evidence behind them; switching off is a resource.
- **Fix the job, not only yourself.** Interventions aimed at the organisation beat interventions aimed at the individual, so the most useful step is often a workload conversation with the person who can change it.
- **The helping trap is real.** Helping is rewarded up to a point, then eats the work your review rewards, and low-promotability tasks fall unequally on women. Break the loop at "the person who": write it down and give it an owner.
- **Saying no costs less than you fear.** A good no is clear, tied to priorities, and offers something smaller.
- **Integrity fails one small step at a time.** Gradual transgressions lead to larger ones, and pressure arrives as a deadline, not an ethics test. Set your lines in advance and learn to recognise the first step.
- **Sometimes the job is the problem.** Pay, progression and respect are the commonest reasons people leave. Leaving well is part of the loop, and [[ch02]] is where to start.
- **Myths:** willpower is not a tank that runs dry, and there is no magic positivity ratio.
:::
