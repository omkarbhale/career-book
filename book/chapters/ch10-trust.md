Four minutes, six reactions, and Dev has already started forming opinions about people she will work with for years. So have they, about her.

That is the subject of Part II. In [[ch01]] we called it the long game: the stretch after "I accept", when the people judging you are no longer strangers with an hour but colleagues with years. Part I taught a skill you can learn like a craft. Part II is different. You cannot memorise your way through five hundred mornings in the same kitchen. You need something underneath the rules that tells you what to do when no rule applies.

This chapter builds that something. It is the model every later chapter in Part II leans on: why work is a repeated game, what trust is actually made of, why bad moments outweigh good ones, why generosity pays and when it doesn't, and why acting your way through a job is expensive while adjusting your style is not. It ends with a tool, a small set of personal defaults, that replaces a thousand rules of etiquette.

## Why first principles, not etiquette {#ch10.sec.why}

Most advice about behaviour at work arrives as rules. Reply within a day. Don't discuss salary. Never cry in the office. Always bring a notebook. Some rules are good, some are local superstition, and all of them run out the moment something happens that the rule-writer did not foresee, such as a coffee machine flooding the kitchen on your first morning.

Rules also have a deeper problem: followed mechanically, they produce exactly the stiff, performed version of you that Part II is trying to retire. A person following etiquette is visibly following etiquette. People notice.

A first-principles approach works the other way round. Instead of memorising what to do, you understand *why* certain behaviour works, and then you generate the right behaviour yourself, in your own voice, for situations nobody wrote a rule for. Physics students learn this way: fewer formulas, more understanding of where formulas come from. The principles in this chapter are not new or secret. They come from game theory, from trust research and from the psychology of emotion at work. What they give you is a way to work out, in a kitchen full of water, what you would do and why.

The first principle is about time.

## The shadow of the future {#ch10.sec.shadow}

::: flashback {ref=ch01.sec.long-game}
In [[ch01]], we met Axelrod and Hamilton's finding that cooperation based on reciprocity can start, thrive and hold its ground in a repeated game, because the other players remember [@axelrod1981].
:::

Game theorists have a lovely phrase for what makes a repeated game different from a one-off encounter: **the shadow of the future**. When you expect to meet someone again, tomorrow's consequences fall across today's choices. Grabbing what you can stops looking clever, because you will have to face the same person next week, and the week after, and in the next round of reviews.

This is not only a theory. In a laboratory experiment, players in repeated prisoner's dilemmas behaved less opportunistically when the chance of playing another round was higher, and the size of the change followed the theory's predictions closely [@dalbo2005]. Lengthen the shadow and people cooperate more.

A workplace has a very long shadow. You are not playing one game with one colleague; you are playing hundreds of overlapping games with dozens of people, for years. And there is a second mechanism at work, which makes the shadow longer still. You are not only dealing with the person in front of you. You are dealing with everyone *they* talk to.

Evolutionary biologists call this **indirect reciprocity**: I help you, and someone who saw it, or heard about it, helps me later. Martin Nowak and Karl Sigmund showed in simulations that cooperation can evolve this way through reputation alone, provided the chance of knowing someone's reputation is higher than the cost of helping divided by its benefit [@nowak1998; @nowak2006]. Nowak later listed it as one of five basic mechanisms by which cooperation evolves, alongside direct reciprocity, the "you scratch my back" kind [@nowak2006].

Read that condition again with an office in mind: *the chance of knowing someone's reputation*. In a company of two hundred people, with chat channels, lunch tables, reviews and references, that chance is very high. Kai watched Dev empty the drip tray. By Friday, half of the design team will know that the new backend developer fixes things without making a speech about it.

::: figure {#ch10.fig.repeated src="ch10/repeated.svg"}
Alt: Two panels. The top panel, "One round, between strangers", shows two boxes: "grab the credit, +3 for you, today" shaded coral, and "share the credit, +1 for you, today", with the note "In a single round, grabbing wins." The bottom panel, "Ten rounds, people who remember", is a line chart of running totals over rounds labelled "meetings, favours, deadlines". A coral line labelled "grab, then get frozen out" jumps ahead early and then goes flat. A teal line labelled "keep cooperating: trust compounds" starts lower and climbs steadily, overtaking the coral line by the fourth round. A note says "Illustrative numbers, not data".

The same choice in two games. The numbers are made up; the shape is the argument. In a one-off encounter, grabbing pays. In a game where people remember, and tell each other, it buys one good round and a long, flat line.
:::

There is a third piece, and it explains the person with the sign. In public-goods experiments, many people will pay out of their own pocket to punish free-riders, even when they will never meet them again and gain nothing from it, and the threat of that punishment makes free-riding less common [@fehr2000]. People do not just remember who cooperates. They enforce it, sometimes rather enthusiastically. WHOEVER BROKE THIS, WE KNOW is a punishment, aimed at nobody in particular, which is part of why it lands so badly.

These are game-theory models and laboratory money games, not studies of offices, so treat what follows as an analogy: a good one, but an analogy. With that caveat, three consequences follow for anyone playing the long game.

1. **Every interaction is also a reputation event.** You are never only dealing with the person in front of you.
2. **Wins that cost trust are expensive.** In a one-shot game, defecting pays. At work, almost nothing is one-shot.
3. **"Kind but not a pushover" is a strategy, not just a temperament.** The spirit of Axelrod's winning strategy was simple: start by cooperating, respond to what others actually do, forgive quickly, and be easy to read [@axelrod1981]. Later models found that a slightly *more* forgiving version does better still when players sometimes make mistakes [@nowak2006], which, at work, they do. That maps cleanly onto good conduct at work, though nobody has tested it as a workplace intervention [!view].

The shadow of the future tells you *why* trust matters so much at work. It does not tell you what trust is made of. For that, we need a different literature.

## What trust is made of {#ch10.sec.trust}

Ask people what trust means and you get warm, vague answers. Researchers needed something sharper, and the definition that won is this: trust is **the willingness to be vulnerable to someone else**, based on what you expect them to do [@mayer1995]. You trust a colleague when you are willing to let your success depend on them: to share a half-finished idea, take their estimate at face value, or hand them the part of the project you cannot check.

Roger Mayer, James Davis and David Schoorman proposed that this willingness rests on three judgements about the other person [@mayer1995]:

- **Ability**: can they actually do the thing?
- **Benevolence**: do they care about my interests, not just their own?
- **Integrity**: do they stick to principles I find acceptable, such as honesty and fairness, consistently?

::: figure {#ch10.fig.trust src="ch10/trust.svg"}
Alt: Three gauge dials side by side, each with its needle pointing high. The first, coral, is "Ability: can you do the job?", with the everyday sign "your estimates come true". The second, teal, is "Benevolence: are you on my side?", with "you warn me before I trip". The third, purple, is "Integrity: do you keep to fair principles?", with "same you when nobody checks". Arrows from all three point down into a gold box labelled "TRUST: being willing to be vulnerable to you".

Three things trust is made of. Each dial is a separate judgement, and a high reading on one does not guarantee the others: a brilliant colleague who is out for themselves can still be hard to trust.
:::

A theory is only a theory, but this one has been tested hard. A meta-analysis of 132 independent samples found that ability, benevolence and integrity each predicted how much people trusted their leaders and coworkers. Trustworthiness, in turn, predicted people's willingness to take risks with that person, their task performance, their helping behaviour, and lower counterproductive behaviour, partly through trust and partly directly [@colquitt2007]. Most of those studies were correlational surveys, so being trusted may help you perform as much as performing well gets you trusted, but the three dials are not a consultant's invention. They are what people actually weigh.

Now go back to the kitchen, and to the ranking you made in the Pause.

The **platform engineer** scores high on confidence and, as far as anyone can see, low on ability. Confidence is not one of the three dials. It can borrow trust for a while, as Rex knows well, but the first puddle calls in the loan.

**Kai** does nothing technical at all, and most people rank them near the top. Why? Benevolence. In four minutes Kai warned the new person, looked after the queue and kept the mood light. Nobody yet knows whether Kai is a good designer, but everybody knows Kai is on their side.

The **sign-writer** is a puzzle for the trust model, which is the point. They might be excellent at their job and scrupulously honest. But the sign says "I will treat people as suspects when something goes wrong", and if you are going to be vulnerable to someone, that is the last thing you want to know about them.

**Tomás** is the interesting case. He asked the right question, and then he accepted "probably" as an answer. That is not a crime; it is a Monday. But integrity, in the everyday sense that matters most at work, includes follow-through: does this person make sure the things they care about actually happen? One "probably" tells you nothing. A pattern of them would. (Nobody, by the way, had reported it. [[ch12]] picks up the story of the ticket.)

And **Dev** scored on two dials at once, ability and benevolence, without saying a word. That is the first deposit in an account we will keep coming back to.

The three dials are easier to feel than to define, so try sorting a few moments of your own.

:::: move {#ch10.move.which-dial} Which dial moved?
For each moment, decide which part of trust it mainly affects: **ability**, **benevolence** or **integrity**. Some touch two. Commit before you open the answer.

1. A colleague's estimate for a feature is off by a factor of three, for the third time.
2. Your manager tells you about a reorganisation before it is announced, so you are not blindsided.
3. A teammate takes credit in a meeting for a fix you made.
4. Someone in another team answers your question at length, even though it is not their job.
5. A senior engineer gives you different explanations of the same decision depending on who else is in the room.
6. A new hire fixes a bug in a system nobody else understands.
7. A colleague says "I'll look into it" in every meeting and never does.

::: answer
1. **Ability.** One bad estimate is noise; three in a row is a pattern people will price in. [[ch12]] is about how to stop this happening to you.
2. **Benevolence**, and some integrity. Your manager took a small risk to protect your interests.
3. **Integrity**, and benevolence. It breaks a principle almost everyone shares, and it shows they put themselves first. This is the kind of withdrawal [[ch16]] deals with.
4. **Benevolence.** Help that costs them something and gains them little is exactly what benevolence means.
5. **Integrity.** Inconsistency across audiences is the clearest signal that the principles move with the room. This is the chameleon problem at its worst.
6. **Ability.** And if they did it quietly and explained what they found, benevolence too.
7. **Integrity**, in its everyday form: follow-through. Each "I'll look into it" is a tiny promise, and each broken one is a tiny withdrawal. Tomás's "probably" is the first step on this road, though only the first.

Notice that most everyday trust is not lost to dramatic betrayals. It is lost to patterns: estimates, follow-through, credit.
:::
::::

If you prefer formulas to dials, consultants have one for you.

::: craft
**A practitioner's version: the Trust Equation.** Consultants David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford offer a formula many people find memorable: trustworthiness equals credibility plus reliability plus intimacy, divided by self-orientation [@maister2000!]. We found no published validation of it as a measure, and it is promoted by the authors' own consulting firm, so treat it as a teaching device, not a finding. Its three top terms map loosely onto ability, integrity and benevolence. Its one genuinely useful addition is the denominator: people discount everything else you offer if they suspect it is mostly about you.
:::

## Warmth first, then competence {#ch10.sec.warmth}

There is a second research tradition that lands in a similar place from a different direction, and it explains a mistake almost every newcomer makes.

Across many studies and cultures, people judging others seem to ask two questions above all: **warmth** (are this person's intentions good towards me?) and **competence** (can they act on those intentions?) [@fiske2007]. The two judgements are often mixed: a person or group seen as warm but not competent tends to attract pity, and one seen as competent but cold tends to attract envy [@fiske2002]. And warmth tends to be judged first [@fiske2007]: before people decide whether you are good at your job, they decide whether you are on their side. Treat that as a tendency, not a law. A careful replication of the classic experiments behind it found no warmth-first effect, and competence mattered at least as much [@nauts2014].

Here is the twist. When people judge *others*, warmth-related traits weigh more; when they judge *themselves*, competence-related traits weigh more. Competence does count for more when your own outcomes depend on the other person [@abele2007], which at work they often do, but warmth is still being weighed. So on your first day, you are anxiously trying to prove you are good, while everyone around you is quietly checking whether you are nice. You are answering a question nobody has asked yet.

That gap explains a lot of stiff first weeks, including the one Dev is about to have in [[ch11]]. It also explains a worry many people have about being "too professional". Overly professional, in practice, often means competent-but-cold: crisp, careful and impossible to read. It feels safe from the inside. From the outside it can look like someone keeping their distance.

None of this means competence doesn't matter; ability is one of the three trust dials, and in the long run it is the one your job depends on. It means that warmth is not a soft extra you add once you have proved yourself. It is part of what people are measuring from the first morning. [[ch14]] turns this into a practical question: how formal to be, with whom, and when.

## Bad is stronger than good {#ch10.sec.asymmetry}

If trust were symmetrical, you could build it and lose it at the same rate. It isn't.

In a wide-ranging review titled, with admirable directness, "Bad Is Stronger than Good", Roy Baumeister and colleagues found that across many areas of psychology, bad events, bad feedback and bad impressions have more impact than equivalent good ones, and that bad impressions form faster and resist change more [@baumeister2001]. The broad asymmetry is widely accepted, though not by everyone: one philosopher of psychology has argued that, as a broad principle, it should be rejected [@corns2018].

At work, small bad moments travel further than you would think. In three experiments, people on the receiving end of rudeness, from an authority figure or a passing stranger, or who even just imagined it, performed worse on both routine and creative tasks and were less helpful afterwards [@porath2007]. A follow-up found much the same for people who *merely witnessed* it [@porath2009]. Lynne Andersson and Christine Pearson's theory of incivility describes how it spirals: each side returns slightly more than it received, until a snide comment becomes a feud [@andersson1999]. The sign on Old Faithful cost the sign-writer something with every person who read it, and it probably cost the office a little too.

Put the shadow of the future together with this asymmetry and you get the picture this book will use for the rest of Part II, and spend in Part III.

### The trust ledger {#ch10.sec.ledger}

Think of every working relationship as keeping a **ledger**. Each time you do what you said, help without being asked, give credit, or own a mistake, you make a small deposit. Each time you let someone down, take credit, or treat them badly, you make a withdrawal. And withdrawals are heavier than deposits.

::: figure {#ch10.fig.ledger src="ch10/ledger.svg"}
Alt: A ledger page titled "The trust ledger", with a column for entries and a column headed "weight". Five deposits, marked with a teal plus sign and a small teal block: "did the boring fix nobody asked for", "kept a small promise, on time", "credited Kai by name in stand-up", "warned early that a task would slip" and "admitted a mistake before anyone found it". One withdrawal, marked with a coral minus sign and a block three times as wide: "snapped at someone in a review". The bottom line reads "balance: what people will vouch for". A note below says "One withdrawal can cancel several deposits. Illustrative weights, not a formula."

The trust ledger. Deposits are small and frequent; withdrawals are rarer and heavier. The weights here are invented to make the asymmetry visible; no study gives an exchange rate.
:::

The ledger is a metaphor, not a measurement. There is no reliable number for how many good moments one bad one costs. A popular "2.9 to one" positivity ratio comes from work that has been debunked, as [[ch17]] explains, and we found no good evidence for any other magic number. But the metaphor gets three things right.

**Deposits are mostly small.** Nobody builds trust with a single grand gesture. You build it with a hundred dull ones: the reply that arrives, the estimate that holds, the meeting you came to prepared.

**Withdrawals are heavier, so avoid the big ones and repair the small ones fast.** [[ch12]] is about the commonest withdrawal at work, the missed commitment, and how to turn it back into a deposit.

**The balance is spent later.** This is the part most people miss. The balance in your ledgers is what colleagues will vouch for when it matters: the reference a hiring manager phones ([[ch09]]), the referral that gets you seen ([[ch06]]), and the senior person who argues for you in a promotion meeting you are not invited to.

Before going further, try the dials and the ledger on a relationship of your own.

:::: move {#ch10.move.audit} A trust audit
Pick one working relationship that matters to you: a manager, a teammate, a client, or someone you are about to start working with.

1. Score how much **they** probably trust **you** on each dial, from 1 (low) to 5 (high): ability, benevolence, integrity. Then write one piece of evidence they have seen for each score.
2. Which dial is lowest? Write down one specific action you could take in the next two weeks that would give them new evidence on that dial.
3. Now score how much you trust *them* on the same three dials. Is there a dial you have been assuming rather than observing?

::: answer
There is no right score, but strong audits share three features.

- **The evidence is behaviour they have seen**, not something you know about yourself. "I'm very reliable" is not evidence; "I delivered the migration a day early and told them two days before that it was on track" is.
- **The action is small and concrete.** "Be more benevolent" is not an action. "Send her the notes from the meeting she missed, with the decision that affects her team highlighted" is.
- **The lowest dial is often benevolence for experienced people and ability for new ones.** Newcomers tend to underrate how much warmth is being measured, and overrate how fast competence can be shown, which is the trap [[ch11]] opens with.

The third step matters more than it looks. Most people hold confident views of colleagues' integrity based on very little evidence. Noticing that is the beginning of fairer judgements, and of less gossip.
:::
::::

Keep that audit in mind, because the balance it describes has a destination.

## Reciprocity: give first, give small, give often {#ch10.sec.reciprocity}

If the ledger is the account, reciprocity is the engine that fills it.

The sociologist Alvin Gouldner argued in 1960 that almost every society runs on a moral norm of reciprocity: help those who have helped you, and do not harm them [@gouldner1960]. The experiments came later, and you met the classic one in [[ch06.sec.give]]: people who received an unsolicited soft drink from a stranger later bought more of his raffle tickets, whether or not they liked him [@regan1971]. Reviews place reciprocity among the core processes of social influence, often operating without people noticing [@cialdini2004].

Two findings make the norm more useful, and more human, than "always repay favours".

**The obligation fades.** As [[ch06]] noted, people returned a small favour five minutes later, but showed no reliable reciprocity a week later [@burger1997]. Reciprocity is not a bank loan with compound interest. That is good news: you do not need to keep score. Keeping score would not work anyway, because the two sides' books drift apart: as time passes, givers value a favour more and receivers value it less [@flynn2003-time]. It also means that favours work best as a *habit*, not a one-off investment you expect to cash in months later.

**Frequency matters as much as size.** In a survey of 161 employees at one large firm, Frank Flynn found that people seen as generous in exchanging favours had higher status, while those who kept their exchanges evenly balanced were more productive. People who exchanged favours *frequently* got some of both [@flynn2003]. The practical reading: give first, give small, give often [!view]. Run between the same two people for long enough, that rhythm of small favours is also how colleagues turn into friends, which brings its own rewards and complications in [[ch15]].

Helping beyond your job description pays in other ways too. A meta-analysis of 168 samples, covering more than 51,000 people, found that this kind of "citizenship behaviour" went with better performance ratings, more rewards, less turnover and better unit performance [@podsakoff2009], though some of that may be managers who like you rating everything about you more highly.

But helping has a cost, and it is not shared evenly. As the management researcher Diane Bergeron has argued, time spent helping can come out of time spent on your own work, which is what most reward systems actually track [@bergeron2007]. In a study of 98 couples, employees whose partners rated them as taking more initiative beyond their role reported more overload, stress and work-family conflict, and the link with work-family conflict was stronger for women [@bolino2005]. That is the first sign of a pattern that [[ch17]] and [[ch19]] take seriously: some helpful work builds trust and careers, and some quietly eats both.

::: coming {ref=ch17}
Dev's quiet fix on Old Faithful was a deposit. Done every morning for a year, it becomes a job nobody gave her. [[ch17]] is about the helping trap, and how to stay generous without becoming the office's unpaid mechanic.
:::

## The chameleon problem {#ch10.sec.chameleons}

By now a reasonable reader has a worry. If trust is built from what other people see, isn't the winning move simply to manage what they see? Shouldn't you become a chameleon: read every room, adjust to every audience, and give each person the version of you they want?

The uncomfortable answer is that chameleons do get ahead, up to a point. *Self-monitoring* is the psychologist's term for the habit of watching social cues and adjusting how you present yourself to fit them [@snyder1974]. A meta-analysis of 136 samples and over 23,000 people found that high self-monitors tend to perform slightly better and are more likely to emerge as leaders [@day2002]. Following 139 MBA graduates for five years, Martin Kilduff and David Day found that high self-monitors changed employers more often and won more promotions, both within their companies and across them [@kilduff1994].

Then the catch. A newer meta-analysis of 55 samples found that the link between self-monitoring and leadership is modest, and usually disappears when the people doing the rating are the leader's own subordinates [@lei2025]. Look closer and it gets better. On how *effective* a leader is, the only raters who saw chameleons as better were the chameleons themselves; neither their bosses nor their teams did [@lei2025]. Chameleons get noticed and get moved. Whether they lead better is, mostly, their own opinion. It is the snapshot game and the long game again, inside a single job.

So is "be yourself" naive? Not if you are precise about what "yourself" means, and here the research has a surprise.

William Fleeson and Joshua Wilt tracked how authentic people felt from moment to moment in daily life. People felt most authentic when they were acting in extraverted, agreeable, conscientious, emotionally stable and intellectually engaged ways, *regardless of their own personality* [@fleeson2010]. Introverts, too, felt most like themselves in their more outgoing moments. Acting "in character" was not what made people feel real. Acting at their best was.

Across 75 studies, felt authenticity correlates with well-being (r = .40) and engagement at work (r = .37) [@sutton2020], though feeling good may make people feel more like themselves as much as the other way round. And the major review of authenticity at work found the research fragmented across ten different ways of defining it, concluding that it "both reinforces and tempers" the popular enthusiasm, with power shaping who can afford to be authentic and how [@cha2019]. Being yourself is cheaper for some people than for others. [[ch14]] deals with that directly, including the costs of code-switching.

The organisational researcher Herminia Ibarra adds a practitioner's warning from the other side: a rigid idea of authenticity ("that's just not me") can stop people growing into new roles, and she recommends borrowing style from many role models and treating it as an experiment [@ibarra2015]. That is an essay, not a study, but it fits the evidence.

Put together, the research suggests a more useful definition than "do whatever comes naturally": **authenticity is keeping your values and intentions constant while your style adapts to the room** [!view]. Adjusting your volume for a client meeting is not fake. Pretending to agree with something you think is wrong is.

## Acting versus adjusting {#ch10.sec.acting}

::: flashback {ref=ch01.sec.acting}
[[ch01]] argued that acting doesn't scale: a trailer can be a performance because it lasts an hour, but a film lasts years.
:::

The research on emotional labour lets us be exact about which kind of performance is expensive.

The sociologist Arlie Hochschild, studying flight attendants and bill collectors, coined the term **emotional labour** for managing your feelings to produce the display a job requires. She distinguished **surface acting**, faking the outward display (the smile over gritted teeth), from **deep acting**, working to actually feel what the situation calls for [@hochschild1983].

The difference turns out to matter a great deal. In a field study, coworkers rated people's emotional delivery as worse when they surface acted and better when they deep acted, and only surface acting was related to stress [@grandey2003]. A meta-analysis of 95 studies found that surface acting, and the felt clash between what you feel and what you are supposed to show, go with substantially impaired well-being (correlations of roughly .39 to .48) and worse job attitudes, while deep acting has only weak links to strain and positive links to customer satisfaction [@hulsheger2011].

::: figure {#ch10.fig.acting src="ch10/acting.svg"}
Alt: Two panels separated by a dashed line. Left, "Surface acting": a frowning face holds a smiling mask in front of itself; labels read "inside: annoyed, outside: a bright smile", a coral box says "felt ≠ shown", and notes read "a gap you have to hold shut, all day" and "goes with worse well-being". Right, "Deep acting": a calm face with a thought bubble saying "she's scared, not hostile"; labels read "you change how you see it, so you feel it", a teal box says "felt ≈ shown", and notes read "nothing to hold shut" and "weak links to strain". A line across the bottom reads "Neither is choosing your words or your volume. That is register, not acting."

Two kinds of performance. Surface acting keeps a gap open between what you feel and what you show; deep acting closes it by changing how you see the situation. Choosing your words and volume is something else again.
:::

Notice what deep acting actually is. It is not pretending harder. It is *reappraisal*: deciding, honestly, that the angry customer is scared rather than hostile, or that the colleague who sent the curt message is overloaded rather than rude, and letting your feelings follow. That is closer to a skill than a lie, and it is the same skill [[ch16]] will need for difficult colleagues.

So when people say "fake it till you make it", they are mixing up two different things. Faking *feelings* all day is the expensive kind of acting, and the evidence links it to strain. Practising a *behaviour* you do not yet find natural, such as speaking up in a meeting or introducing yourself first, is how people grow, and Fleeson's findings suggest it may even feel more like you once you do it [@fleeson2010]. Both these findings are correlational, and the direction of cause is not settled, but the line between them is the line this book draws between acting and adjusting.

One popular claim about authenticity is worth checking before you meet it at a conference.

::: myth {#ch10.myth.feel-immoral verdict=busted grade=strong} Research proves that being inauthentic makes you feel immoral and dirty
This claim comes from a 2015 paper by Francesca Gino, Maryam Kouchaki and Adam Galinsky, "The Moral Virtue of Authenticity", published in *Psychological Science*. The journal retracted it in July 2023 [@psychsci2023-retraction], so it is not evidence of anything, and this book does not cite it as such. What remains well supported is narrower and less dramatic: faking emotions you do not feel goes with worse well-being [@hulsheger2011], and feeling authentic goes with feeling better [@sutton2020]. Neither says that adapting your style makes you feel like a bad person.
:::

## Defaults, not scripts {#ch10.sec.defaults}

Here is where the first principles turn into something you can use on Monday.

Every working day contains dozens of small decisions about how to treat people: whether to reply now or later, whether to mention a problem, whether to help, whether to share credit, whether to say what you think. You can make each one fresh, weighing the politics every time, which is exhausting and tends to produce the performed, calculating version of you. Or you can decide a few things once.

::: craft {by=author} Why defaults beat scripts
We think the most practical thing you can do at the start of Part II is write down a handful of **defaults**: behaviours you will do unless there is a good reason not to. Not traits ("I'm honest") but actions ("I tell people about problems the day I find them").

Defaults work for the reasons this chapter has laid out. They are cheap to keep up, because you decided them once and do not have to perform them; that is the difference between deep and surface acting, applied to conduct. They are easy for others to read, which is half of what made Axelrod's winning strategy win. They make steady deposits in every ledger without you having to keep score. And they are yours: two people with different defaults can both be trustworthy, in their own voices.

A script tells you what to say. A default tells you who to be, and lets you find the words.
:::

What do good defaults look like? Here are Dev's, written on a sticky note that evening and stuck to the bottom of her monitor:

1. Tell people about problems the day I find them.
2. Say who did the work, by name.
3. Do the small fix if it takes under five minutes, and don't make a speech about it.
4. Ask the question instead of guessing.
5. Same Dev in the meeting and in the corridor.

Each one is an action, not a virtue. Each one maps onto at least one trust dial: the first onto integrity, the second onto benevolence, the third onto ability and benevolence, the fourth onto ability (and, as [[ch11]] will show, it makes you look *more* competent, not less), the fifth onto integrity. And none of them is a script.


A few questions tend to come up at this point, usually from the sceptical end of the room.

::: ask
Q: Isn't this just a long way of saying "be nice"?

A: No. Nice is a mood; trust is three separate judgements. Plenty of nice people are hard to trust because their estimates never hold (ability) or their follow-through is patchy (integrity). And plenty of brusque people are trusted deeply because they are excellent and straight. Warmth matters, and it is often judged first, but it is one dial of three.

Q: Thinking of work as a "game" with a "ledger" sounds calculating. Isn't that the opposite of authentic?

A: The models describe why good behaviour pays; they are not a reason to fake it. The whole argument of this chapter is that calculated performances are expensive and fragile, and that the cheapest strategy over years is to actually be trustworthy. You do not need to keep score, and as [[ch10.sec.reciprocity]] showed, score-keeping does not work anyway: the felt obligation fades fast, and givers and receivers remember the same favour differently.

Q: My workplace has high turnover, or I'm a contractor. Is my game really repeated?

A: Probably more than it looks. Industries are small, people move between companies together, and references and referrals reach back across jobs. A short contract is a shorter shadow, not no shadow. But it is fair to say that the shorter the expected relationship, the more people will treat it as a one-off, so expect less cooperation from others and do not let it set your own defaults.

Q: I'm an introvert. Are you telling me to act extraverted to feel authentic?

A: No. Fleeson and Wilt's finding is about moments, not personalities: people felt more authentic in the moments when they acted a little more outgoing, warm, organised, calm and curious, whatever their usual traits [@fleeson2010]. That is not an instruction to become a party host. It suggests that stretching a little, such as asking the question in the meeting or saying hello first, is unlikely to feel fake. Quiet versions count.
:::


The best way to find out whether a default is any good is to write one.

:::: move {#ch10.move.defaults} Your five defaults
Write down five things you will do by default at work, whatever the situation, unless you have a good reason not to. For each one, note which trust dial it feeds.

Rules for a good default:

- It is an **action**, not a trait. ("I reply to direct questions within a working day", not "I'm responsive".)
- You could keep it on your **worst** week, not just your best.
- It would be the **same** whoever is watching.

::: answer
Strong lists tend to include at least one default from each of these families, though the wording should be yours:

- **Follow-through** (integrity, ability): "I tell people the day I know something will slip." "If I say I'll look into it, I write it down."
- **Credit and generosity** (benevolence): "I name who did the work." "I answer questions from other teams even when it's not my job, up to fifteen minutes."
- **Honesty under mild pressure** (integrity): "I say 'I don't know' instead of guessing." "I disagree in the meeting, not in the corridor afterwards."
- **Care for your own limits**, because defaults you cannot sustain become surface acting: "I don't answer non-urgent messages after 7 pm." [[ch17]] returns to this.

Weak defaults are aspirations ("always go the extra mile"), traits ("be authentic") or rules that depend on the audience ("be extra careful with senior people"). If a default would make you behave differently when your manager walks in, it fails the third test. The chameleon research suggests the performance is mostly lost on its audience anyway: chameleons rate their own leadership more highly than either their bosses or their teams do [@lei2025].

Keep the list. You will test it against a missed deadline in [[ch12]], a hard conversation in [[ch13]] and a real temptation in [[ch17]].
:::
::::

Dev's own list got its first review that same evening, from someone who had watched her film for three years.

::: scene {slug="INT. DEV'S FLAT — MONDAY, 9:40 PM"}
Meera calls, as promised, to ask how the first day went. Dev can hear a kettle in the background. Meera's mug, she says, is new: SAME TEAM, a gift from her own team after a bruising quarter.

@dev: Someone put a sign on the coffee machine. "Whoever broke this, we know."

@meera: Ah. The prosecutor. Every office has one. Did it work?

@dev: Nobody owned up, if that's what you mean. Because nobody broke it. The drip tray was full.

@meera: And who emptied it?

@dev: ...Me. I didn't tell anyone.

@meera: Good. Somebody saw.

@dev: How do you know?

@meera: Because somebody always sees. That's the thing about a place you're going to be for years, Dev. It's not an interview any more. You can't control what they see, so the only thing that works is being someone you don't mind them seeing.

@dev: That sounds exhausting.

@meera: It's the opposite. Performing is exhausting. This is just Tuesday, every day. Write down what you'll do by default, and then stop thinking about it.

@dev: I already did. It's on a sticky note.

@meera: Of course it is. What's the first one?

@dev: "Tell people about problems the day I find them."

@meera: Lovely. That one's going to cost you something within a month. Keep it anyway.
:::

Meera is right about the cost, and [[ch12]] collects it. But first, Dev has a more immediate problem: being new. She has a trailer to live up to, a team that does not know her yet, and an instinct, like most newcomers, to show everyone how competent she is before she has shown them anything else. The first ninety days are where the long game opens, and where most of its early mistakes are made.

::: rewind
- At work you are in a **repeated game with an audience**: the shadow of the future makes cooperation pay, and reputation means you are dealing with everyone the other person talks to.
- **Trust is the willingness to be vulnerable to someone**, and it rests on three separate judgements: **ability, benevolence and integrity**. A high reading on one does not guarantee the others.
- People often judge **warmth first and competence second**, while judging themselves the other way round. Newcomers who only try to look competent are answering a question nobody has asked yet.
- **Bad is stronger than good**, and rudeness costs even the people who only witness it. Think of every relationship as a **trust ledger**: small, frequent deposits; heavier withdrawals; and a balance that is spent later, in references, referrals and promotions.
- **Reciprocity** runs office life, but the felt obligation fades within a week. Give first, give small, give often, and watch how much invisible helping you take on.
- Chameleons get noticed and promoted, but neither their bosses nor their teams rate them as better leaders; mostly, they do. **Authenticity is keeping your values constant while your style adapts.**
- **Faking feelings is expensive; adjusting style is not.** Surface acting goes with worse well-being; deep acting, honestly reappraising a situation, much less so.
- Replace scripts with **defaults**: a few actions you decide once, keep on your worst week, and do the same whoever is watching.
:::
