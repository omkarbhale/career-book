::: scene {slug="INT. LANTERNFISH, THE QUIET DESK BY THE WINDOW — DEV'S LAST FRIDAY, 3:10 PM"}
Dev's laptop is wiped, her badge is in an envelope for Tomás, and she has one browser tab open on a borrowed machine: the internal referral form for the senior engineer seat she is leaving on the reliability squad.

She has filled in the name already. *Beatriz Santos.*

The box below it asks: *How do you know this person, and why should we talk to them?* Four years ago Dev could not have written a good answer to that question about herself. Now she writes one about somebody else, in four sentences, the way [[ch06.sec.asking]] taught her:

> *I worked next to Bea at Corvid for three years. She rewrote their billing engine in a fortnight, and since then she has become one of the few people anywhere who can change a live payments database without downtime; the open-source tool she maintains to do it is linked below. She is the best engineer I have worked with, and the worst at saying so, which is why I'm saying it. Ask her about the billing rewrite.*

Below that she pastes two links. Two years ago Bea had none to give. Now there is the tool itself, with two years of history in which every bug anyone reported is fixed in public, and a résumé whose bullets say what changed because of her.

She texts Bea.

@dev: Done. You'll hear from Tomás. Don't undersell the billing engine.

@bea: I've practised the billing engine story eleven times. Meera made me time it.

@dev: How long?

@bea: Two minutes. With a follow-up question I'm actually hoping they ask.
:::

That is what a trailer looks like when it finally matches the film. Bea has not become a different person, louder or more polished or more like Rex. She has made what she already was easy to see: specific, checkable and costly to fake, in the language of [[ch01.sec.signals]]. And the referral that opens the door is worth so much for the reason [[ch06.sec.why]] gave: someone who has watched her film for years is putting their own reputation behind it.

At four o'clock Rex comes by with two coffees from the new machine and the expression of a man about to ask a favour.

Six months ago Rex asked to step back from managing. He did it well, which surprised everyone except Kai, who had been having lunch with him every fortnight: he told Tomás what he had learned, recommended the engineer on his team who should take over, and asked to go back to building things. His profile headline, which has been "Builder. Dreamer. Disruptor.", "Chief Possibility Officer", "Visionary-in-Residence" and "Head of Engineering Excellence", now reads, in full: **Engineer.**

@rex: I'm going for a senior role at a company I actually want to stay at. Would you be a reference?

@dev: Yes. But I'm going to tell you what I'll say first.

@rex: That sounds ominous.

@dev: It's the opposite. I'll say you're the best person I know at getting a room behind an idea, that your team liked you even when it was hard, and that you stepped back from managing because you'd worked out it wasn't the right job, which is rarer than it should be. And I'll say your follow-through has got a lot better this year, and that if they give you a clear owner for every promise, you'll keep it.

Rex is quiet for a second.

@rex: That's better than anything I'd have written about myself.

@dev: That's because it's true. Yours were always a bit better than true.

He laughs, because he knows, and goes off to tell someone in sales about the new coffee machine.

A good reference is just a trailer someone else cuts from your film, and an honest one is the only kind worth giving [!view]. Dev's reference for Rex does not hide the weak scenes; it puts them in context and says what has changed. It will probably help him more than a glowing one would, because the person reading it will believe every word.

## Your scorecards, again {#epi.sec.scorecards}

At the start of the book you scored yourself on fourteen statements, in [[ch01.move.scorecards]]. Here they are again.

:::: move {#epi.move.scorecards} Your three scorecards, revisited
Without looking at your old scores, score each statement again from 1 (not at all) to 5 (completely). Then compare.

**The snapshot game:** I can describe my best piece of work in two minutes, including what changed because of it. My résumé or profile shows results, not just duties. I know at least five people who work somewhere I might like to work. I can talk about my work in an interview without my nerves taking over. I know roughly what people in my role are paid in my market.

**The long game:** People come to me for help, and not only with my own work. When I say I will do something by a certain day, I do it, or I warn people early. I can disagree with someone without it becoming personal. I could name three colleagues who would recommend me without being asked twice. I behave much the same way whether or not my manager is in the room.

**The career game:** I know how my work is judged, by whom, and when. I keep a record of what I have done and what changed because of it. I can name the skill I am deliberately getting better at this year. I know what the next level of my role asks for, and whether I want it.

::: answer
Look for three things.

- **Which score moved most?** That is probably the game you were missing, and the one where reading paid off fastest. Keep the habit that moved it.
- **Which score did not move?** Reading a book cannot raise "three colleagues would recommend me"; only months of the long game can. If a long-game score is unchanged, that is not failure; it is the honest speed of that game. Pick one default from [[ch10]] and give it a year.
- **Where is the biggest gap between two scores?** A high trailer score with a low film score is Rex's position; a high film score with a low trailer score is Bea's. The gap matters more than the totals, because the thread of this book is to keep the trailer a true cut of the film.

Then put a date in your diary, a year from today, and do this again. You will play all three games many more times; the scorecards are a way to notice which one you are neglecting.
:::

## The whole book on one page {#epi.sec.one-page}

**Three games.** Working life is a *snapshot game*, in which strangers judge an edited trailer of you; a *long game*, in which colleagues watch the whole film; and a *career game*, in which the film is reviewed and the review decides what you do next.

**Three rules and a thread.**

1. **Learn the snapshot game without shame.** Making real value legible is translation, not spin.
2. **Play the long game without a mask.** Good defaults beat good performances.
3. **Make the work count, honestly.** Get good at things that matter, and make sure the people who judge your work can see it as it is.

**The thread: one you.** Never let a trailer, or a promotion case, promise what the film cannot deliver; never let a good film go without a trailer.

**One line per chapter.**

- [[ch01]]: The three games reward different skills; play them as one person, and the loop between them does the rest.
- [[ch02]]: Separate push from pull, ask before you leave, search from a job, and treat a layoff as an event, not a verdict.
- [[ch03]]: Most rejections are funnel arithmetic, not verdicts, but the funnel is not neutral either.
- [[ch04]]: Turn duties into evidence, and keep a bank of true stories.
- [[ch05]]: Your résumé is a trailer: specific, checkable, readable in a glance and in a slow read.
- [[ch06]]: People who know your work are the strongest signal you have; ask well, and give before you need.
- [[ch07]]: Interviews measure some of what matters, noisily; tell true stories well, and interview them back.
- [[ch08]]: Work samples show real work; scope them, think aloud, and know when a task is free consulting.
- [[ch09]]: Read the whole offer, negotiate once and calmly, and leave through a door you would walk back through.
- [[ch10]]: Work is a repeated game, and trust is ability, benevolence and integrity, built by defaults rather than performances.
- [[ch11]]: Start by learning, asking and keeping the promises your trailer made.
- [[ch12]]: Reliability is the cheapest trust there is: commit clearly, warn early, apologise well.
- [[ch13]]: Write the point first, and give and take feedback about the work, not the person.
- [[ch14]]: Professionalism is a dial you set to the room, not a costume you put on.
- [[ch15]]: Be friendly with everyone and friends with a few, and keep the rules of each apart.
- [[ch16]]: Fight about the work, not the people; share credit in writing; know when something is actually wrong.
- [[ch17]]: Fix the job before you fix yourself, set your lines in advance, and leave when the job is the problem.
- [[ch18]]: A rating is a noisy measurement with the rater inside it; shape what you can, starting with the evidence.
- [[ch19]]: A promotion case is an internal trailer, and the trust you built is the currency it spends.
- [[ch20]]: Choose skills that compound, and practise with feedback rather than counting hours.
- [[ch21]]: Managing is a different job, not a higher rung; try it before you choose, and say so well either way.
- [[ch22]]: A career is a sequence of loops; craft the job you have, and make the next move from strength.

::: scene {slug="EXT. OUTSIDE LANTERNFISH — DEV'S LAST FRIDAY, 6:05 PM"}
Kai walks Dev to the station, which is in the wrong direction for Kai.

@kai: Thursday still stands, you know. Different companies, same noodles.

@dev: No reviews at the counter.

@kai: You'll be reviewing a dozen teams. I'm not hearing about any of them.

At the corner Dev's phone buzzes: a message from Tomás. *Bea's interview is booked for Tuesday. Your referral was the best one I've read this year. Go and be excellent somewhere else, and call me if it doesn't work out.*

On the train she opens the spreadsheet one last time, out of habit. *Game 1*, *Game 2*, *Game 3*, and twenty-odd tabs she has not opened in years. She does not add a new one. She knows how this goes now: a snapshot, a long game, a career game, and then, one day, another snapshot, played from wherever the last loop left her.

She closes the laptop and looks out of the window, and for once she is not making a list.
:::

That is the loop, and it is yours now. You will play it many times. Play it as one person, and each time round you will start from somewhere a little stronger. If you want to go further than this book does, [[more]] says where.
