INT. A NOODLE BAR NEAR LANTERNFISH — THURSDAY, 9:40 PM

The squad’s dinner was meant to end at nine. Everyone else has gone. Dev and Kai are still at the counter, arguing about whether a reality show in which people renovate houses while blindfolded is good television (Kai) or a cry for help (Dev).

They have not mentioned work for an hour. Dev only notices this when the waiter brings the bill and Kai says, unprompted:

Kai Okay, but how are you actually? You went quiet after the planning meeting.
Dev Is it that obvious?
Kai To me. I’ve been sitting next to you for six months.

Dev tells them: the estimate she is worried about, the flat that is too expensive, her mother asking when she will “finish changing jobs”. Kai listens, then tells her about their sister’s wedding, which is in three weeks and is already a diplomatic incident.

On the walk to the station, Dev’s phone buzzes.

Kai home safe? also the blindfold show is a masterpiece and you’re wrong
Dev home. and I’m right

She looks at the message for a moment longer than it deserves. At Corvid it took her two years to have a friend at work. Here it has taken six months and a bowl of noodles.

A full-time job puts you beside the same people for a large share of your waking week, so it would be strange if some of them did not become friends. When it happens, it is one of the best things about a job. It is also one of the more complicated, because friendship and work run on different rules, and sooner or later the rules collide. For Dev and Kai, the collision is about three weeks away.

This chapter is about the people you like at work: what friendship there does for you and costs you, how close is close enough, gossip, drinks, romance and loneliness, and the hardest case of all, when a friend becomes your manager.

Pause

Before you read on: if your closest friend at work were promoted to lead your team tomorrow, what is the first thing you think would change between you? Write it down. The chapter comes back to it near the end.

A mixed blessing#

Start with the good news, because it is real. In two field studies, one of 168 insurance employees and one of 182 restaurant and retail staff, people with more “multiplex” relationships, colleagues who were also friends, received higher performance ratings from their supervisors [1]. The researchers traced the benefit to trust and positive mood: friends help each other, share information and make the day better.

The same study found the bad news in the same relationships. Having more friends at work also went with more emotional exhaustion, because those friendships take effort to maintain, and because being someone’s colleague and someone’s friend at once is harder than being either. The authors’ title asked whether workplace friendships are “a mixed blessing”, and their answer was yes [1].

Why mixed? A theory paper by Julianna Pillemer and Nancy Rothbard gives the clearest explanation [2]. Friendship has its own defining features: it is informal, voluntary, communal (you help because you care, not because you are owed) and emotional. Work has the opposite features: formal roles, constraints on who you work with, exchange norms (you are paid for what you deliver) and goals that are about the task, not the people. Put the two in one relationship and they will clash at predictable points: when a friend’s work is not good enough, when one of you is promoted, when the team needs something the friendship does not.

None of that is a reason to avoid friends at work. It is a reason to understand where the stress points are, so that when one arrives, neither of you is surprised.

Ask Anyway

QGallup says having a “best friend at work” matters. Do I need one?

AGallup reports that the item “I have a best friend at work” became more strongly linked to outcomes like intent to leave after the pandemic began [3 weak]. But Gallup sells the survey that contains that item, and the page shows no methods or effect sizes, so treat it as a claim, not evidence. The better-studied version is less catchy: close, trusting relationships at work go with better performance, at some cost in effort [1]. You need people you can trust and lean on. Whether you call any of them your best friend is up to you.

QI’m quite private. Is it a problem if I don’t make friends at work?

ANo. Plenty of people do excellent work with warm, friendly relationships that never become friendships, and “How much of you to share” in Chapter 14 showed that people genuinely differ in how much they want work and home to mix. The one thing worth watching for is isolation, which is a different thing from privacy, and is covered later in this chapter.

Friendly is not the same as friends#

The first step towards handling work friendships well is noticing that “friend” covers several quite different relationships. A useful map has four stops.

Being friendly with nearly everyone is part of being good at the long game. It costs little, and it builds the warmth that, alongside competence, shapes how people judge you (Chapter 10). Being friends with a few people is a different commitment, with different obligations: you will hear things, you will be asked for things, and your loyalty will occasionally be tested.

The mistake is to treat these as the same thing, in either direction. Some people treat every friendly colleague as a friend and are hurt when the friendliness turns out to have limits. Others keep everyone at the friendly stop for fear of the complications, and miss the trust and help that a few real friendships bring our view.

Closeness takes time, and the right kind of time#

How do friendly colleagues become friends? Mostly through time together, but not all time counts equally.

In two studies, one of 355 adults who had recently moved and one of 112 first-year university students, Jeffrey Hall tracked how closeness grew with hours spent together [4]. His university’s summary puts rough numbers on it: about 50 hours to move from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 to friend, and more than 200 to close friend. Those figures are approximate, and Hall noted something important for this chapter: hours spent working together counted for less than hours spent in leisure and everyday conversation.

That explains Dev’s two speeds. At Corvid, she and Bea spent thousands of hours side by side, almost all of them working, and it took two years. With Kai, a handful of lunches on the roof and one long dinner did more than months of stand-ups.

The findings from “Connection: you are more liked than you think” in Chapter 11 explain why many people never make the move at all. People underestimate how much others liked them after a conversation, the liking gap, which shows up in work teams too and is largest between peers [5; 6]. And people underestimate how much others want to connect [7].

Put together, these say that the colleague you think is merely tolerating you probably likes you more than you think, and would probably say yes if you suggested lunch. People underestimate by as much as half how likely others are to agree to a direct request for help [8], the finding that made Dev’s message to Ana work in Chapter 6. The same arithmetic applies to “do you want to get lunch?”

There is even a small, old idea that asking someone for a favour makes them like you more, the so-called Ben Franklin effect. The original 1969 study was small, and we found no direct replication of it [9 weak], but a modern conceptual replication with Japanese and American participants found that people did like a requester more after she asked for their help, and not when they helped unasked [10]. Treat it as a decent bet with thin evidence: asking for small help is unlikely to hurt a budding friendship, and it might help.

Myth CheckYou can only maintain about 150 relationshipsBusted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

“Dunbar’s number” comes from extrapolating a relationship between brain size and group size in primates to humans. When researchers re-ran that kind of analysis with modern statistical methods, the estimates for humans ranged widely, with confidence intervals running from about 2 to about 520 relationships: no single cognitive limit can be derived that way [11]. That is one reanalysis, and the argument is not over, but the claim as usually stated, a fixed limit of about 150, is not supported by the method used to derive it. Time and energy really do limit how many close friendships you can sustain (see the hours above). A magic number does not.

Loneliness, the quiet cost#

The opposite of a bad work friendship is not no friendship. Sometimes it is loneliness, and loneliness has costs that are easy to underrate because lonely people tend not to announce it.

Gallup’s global workplace data found that about one in five employees reported feeling a lot of loneliness on the previous day: 25% of fully remote workers, 21% of hybrid workers and 16% of those fully on-site [12]. That is a large survey from a commercial publisher, using a single question, and it shows an association rather than a cause. But it fits a careful field study in two organisations, of 672 employees and their 114 supervisors, which found that lonelier employees received lower performance ratings from their supervisors, partly because colleagues saw them as less approachable and partly because they felt less committed [13]. Loneliness, in other words, is not only painful; lonelier people are also seen as less approachable, which is hardly a way out of it.

This connects to the remote-work evidence in “Remote and hybrid: being present without being in the room” in Chapter 14. Working from home does not make most people lonely (three-quarters of fully remote workers in Gallup’s data did not report it [12]), but it removes the accidental hours that friendship is built from, so remote and hybrid workers have to create those hours on purpose.

Your Move 15.1Who would you call?

Write down the name of the person at work you would contact in each situation. One name per line; if you have nobody, write “nobody”, which is useful information too.

  1. You have made a serious mistake at 9 pm and need to think it through before telling your manager.
  2. You need honest feedback on a document before it goes to senior people.
  3. You are wondering whether to leave the company.
  4. You want to celebrate something good that happened at work.
  5. You need to know how something really works here, as opposed to how the wiki says it works.
Reveal one way to play it

Look at the pattern, not the individual names.

  • The same name on every line? You have a close work friend, which is good, and a single point of failure, which is risky. If that person leaves, is promoted over you or falls out with you, you lose all five supports at once. It is worth building one or two more relationships at the “work friend” stop.
  • “Nobody” on two or more lines? That is common, especially in the first year of a job or in remote work, and the research above suggests it is worth fixing deliberately rather than waiting. Start with line 5: asking someone how things really work is a small, flattering request that people say yes to more often than you expect [8].
  • Your manager on line 3? That takes unusual trust, and it is worth checking you have it before you test it.
  • A mix of names, including people on other teams? That is the healthiest pattern: several ties, some close and some weaker, which is also what makes a future job search easier (Chapter 6).

There is no target number. The question is whether, when something goes wrong or right, you have someone to turn to.

Gossip: the office’s reputation system#

Friendship comes with information. Friends tell each other who is struggling, who is leaving, who is impossible to work with. That is gossip, and a lot of advice says simply: don’t.

That advice ignores what gossip is. A systematic review of 324 published definitions settled on a broad one: gossip is a sender talking to a receiver about a target who is absent or unaware, and it can be positive, neutral or negative [14]. When researchers actually recorded people’s everyday conversations, using small devices that sampled the sounds of 467 people’s days, they found that most gossip was neutral, mostly about social information rather than scandal; extraverts gossiped more; and the stereotype that women gossip more maliciously than men largely failed [15].

Some gossip is positively useful. In lab experiments, people who saw someone behave selfishly felt frustrated, and warning others about that person relieved the frustration and deterred future selfishness [16]. Researchers call this prosocial gossip, and in groups it works together with the ability to exclude people to keep cooperation going [17]. This is the repeated game from Chapter 10 in action: reputation is how a group remembers who cooperates, and gossip is how reputation travels.

So “never gossip” is unrealistic advice. Better norms, which are our view rather than tested rules our view:

  • Share what protects people, such as a warning about someone who takes credit, or who is unsafe to be alone with.
  • Don’t share what you would be ashamed to have repeated, and assume that anything you say will be repeated, because it usually is.
  • Don’t trade in other people’s private lives. Someone’s health, relationship or job search is theirs to tell.
  • Be the kind of person the neutral gossip is good about. You cannot stop people talking about you; you can give them true and decent things to say.
Trapdoor

Bonding over a common enemy. The fastest way to feel close to a new colleague is to complain together about a third person. It works quickly, and it sours. Friendships built on shared grievance need a steady supply of grievances, and venting while dwelling on the person who annoyed you tends to increase anger rather than release it [18], as Chapter 13 showed. Complain occasionally, the way friends do; if complaining is most of what you share, you have a grievance partnership, not a friendship.

Drinks, events and the people who don’t drink#

A lot of workplace friendship starts at events with alcohol, and there is a reason. In a lab study of 720 social drinkers who met in groups of three strangers, a moderate dose of alcohol increased smiling, positive displays and how bonded people felt [19]. Drinks lower the threshold for the non-work conversation that friendship is built from.

That study, though, measured strangers having a drink, not what non-drinkers miss out on, and alcohol at work has a less cheerful side. In a US national survey of 2,805 employed adults from the early 2000s, about 15% reported drinking or being impaired at work in the previous year: 9.2% had worked with a hangover and 7.1% had drunk during the workday, with managers reporting higher rates than others [20]. And a much-shared finding that drinkers earn 10 to 14% more than non-drinkers comes from correlational survey data with heavy confounding, promoted by a policy brief linked to one of its authors [21 weak]; it does not show that drinking causes anything.

Our reading of all this is that the active ingredient in after-work drinks is not the alcohol. It is showing up, staying for a while and being socially available, and non-drinkers can get most of the benefit without the drink our view.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

For people who don’t drink, or don’t want to tonight: go, stay for the first hour, which is when most of the conversation happens anyway, and hold a drink of any kind so nobody asks. “I’m not drinking tonight” is a complete answer and needs no reason. For people who organise events: put food at the centre rather than the bar, start some events at lunchtime, and make sure the non-alcoholic option is something an adult would choose. Who comes to your events decides who gets the friendships that start there.

Romance at work#

Some work friendships become something else, and it is common enough to deserve a straight paragraph rather than a nervous joke. In a 2023 survey by the Society for Human Resource Management, 27% of US workers said they were in, or had been in, a romance with a colleague, and 57% of them said love was the motivation [22 weak]. That is a professional association’s survey reported in a press release, so treat the number as a rough guide.

The best-studied risk is not romance itself but power. In an experiment with 212 working adults, people who read about a colleague dating a superior judged that colleague as more likely to be motivated by their job, as getting unfair advantages, and as less trustworthy, and said they would be less honest with them, compared with a colleague dating a peer [23]. A theory model of workplace romance makes the same point more broadly: the effects reach coworkers and the organisation, not just the couple [24].

So the practical rules are mostly about the reporting line our view. Between peers, a relationship is largely your own business, with some gossip risk and a need to keep work decisions separate. Across a reporting line, it creates a conflict of interest for the manager and a trust problem for everyone else, which is why a sensible policy asks for such relationships to be disclosed and for one person to move out of the reporting line. Policies differ, and we found no reliable data on how common particular policies are, so read yours. And anything that is unwanted, or where one person has power over the other’s job, is not a romance question at all; Chapter 16 covers what to do when something is actually wrong.

Boundaries that keep both#

The friendships that last at work are usually the ones with clear, mostly unspoken boundaries: what you talk about, where, and when. Boundaries are not coldness. They are what lets a friendship survive the clashes between friendship’s rules and work’s rules that Pillemer and Rothbard described [2].

Two pieces of evidence help. First, mentally switching off from work in your own time goes with better life satisfaction and less strain, and it does not make people less engaged at work [25]. A friendship that only ever talks about work keeps both of you at work, so the noodle-bar conversations about terrible television are doing a job. Second, people who turn down invitations overestimate how hurt or angry the person inviting them will be, and how much it will cost them future invitations [26]. That study was about social invitations rather than work requests, but it is directly relevant to the colleague who wants more of your time than you want to give: saying no to the third after-work drinks this week costs less than you fear.

Boundaries are easier to hold when you have the words ready, so draft some now.

Your Move 15.2Boundary scripts

Write one or two sentences you could actually say in each situation. Aim for warm and clear; no apologising for having a life.

  1. A friendly colleague invites you to after-work drinks for the third time this week. You like them; you want to go home.
  2. A work friend starts telling you, in detail, about a teammate’s divorce, which the teammate has not mentioned to you.
  3. A colleague you are friendly with, but not close to, keeps messaging you late in the evening about personal problems.
  4. Your work friend asks you to tell them what your manager said about them in your one-to-one.
Reveal one way to play it

Sample scripts, with the reasoning:

  1. “I’m going to skip tonight, I’m saving my social battery for the weekend. Lunch on Thursday?” Saying no costs less than you expect [26], and offering a smaller alternative keeps the relationship warm. You don’t owe a reason; one is just friendly.
  2. “I think that’s for her to tell me, if she wants to. How are you doing?” This declines the gossip about someone’s private life without lecturing your friend, and turns the conversation back to the relationship you actually share.
  3. “I want to be helpful, but I’m not much use late at night: I switch off after eight. Can we grab a coffee tomorrow?” This sets a time boundary, which protects your evenings [25], without rejecting the person. If the problems sound serious, it is kind to suggest someone better placed to help than a colleague.
  4. “I’d rather not pass on what’s said in one-to-ones, the same way I wouldn’t pass on what you tell me. If something’s worrying you, I’ll happily help you work out how to ask her yourself.” This protects your manager’s trust and your friend’s: someone who carries messages from one-to-ones will eventually carry yours.

Notice that each script says no to a behaviour and yes to the person. That is the shape of most good boundaries.

When your friend becomes your manager#

INT. LANTERNFISH, TOMÁS'S OFFICE — MONDAY, 10 AM

Tomás has asked Dev to come in before the squad meeting.

Tomás I wanted you to hear it from me first. Kai is going to lead the design and test squad from next month. I’m still your manager, but Kai will set the squad’s priorities, run its one-to-ones and write a section of your review.
Dev That’s great. They’ll be great.
Tomás They will. I also know you two are close, so I wanted to give you a week’s notice to think about what changes. Both of you.

Dev says the right things and goes back to her desk, where Kai is pretending not to watch her come out of the office. She is genuinely pleased for them. She is also, she notices, slightly worried about Thursday nights.

This is the case where the clash between friendship and work stops being theoretical. A lead has to evaluate, prioritise and sometimes disappoint people; a friend is supposed to be on your side. The same person is now supposed to do both.

The evidence here is thin, and we should say so. The only direct study the researchers found is a small qualitative one, in which employees described how their workplace friendships had deteriorated; promotion was one of the five main causes they described, and people mostly pulled back indirectly, by avoiding personal topics and socialising less, rather than by talking about it [27 weak]. Pillemer and Rothbard’s theory explains why: a manager’s formal role and evaluative duties clash with friendship’s communal norms [2]. And the research on leader-member exchange shows that leaders form relationships of different quality with each person they lead, and that those differences matter for how people are treated and how they perform [28; 29]. A lead with one close friend on the team has an obvious, visible version of that problem, whether or not they are actually unfair.

What should two friends actually do? With so little evidence, the rest of this section is our view, built from the theory above and from what experienced managers tend to report our view. Read it as a reasoned argument, not a finding.

Author’s ViewNaming the change

Our opinion: argued, not proven

The worst way through a friend’s promotion is the one the narratives describe: saying nothing, and letting the friendship cool by avoidance [27]. So name the change out loud, early, together, and agree a few rules:

  • Separate the hats. Work conversations about performance, priorities and pay happen in the one-to-one, with notes; the friendship happens elsewhere. Either of you can say “which hat is this?” at any time.
  • Be visibly fair. The lead holds the friend to exactly the standards of everyone else, and writes their feedback down the same way. The friend does not ask for, or accept, inside information about colleagues.
  • Expect it to change shape. Some things you used to share now belong in a different relationship: the friend should complain about the lead’s decisions to someone else, and the lead should complain about the team to someone else.
  • Agree the exit. If it stops working, either of you can say so, and the answer might be that one of you moves teams. That is a better outcome than a friendship and a working relationship that both quietly rot.

None of this is tested. But every rule follows from the same diagnosis: the trouble comes when one relationship tries to run on two sets of rules at once, so the cure is to keep the rule sets apart.

The same logic applies in reverse, when you are the one promoted over a friend. You will feel the pull to be the friend first. The fairest thing you can do for them is be a good lead to everyone, including them, and the kindest is to say out loud that things have changed.

Your Move 15.3The friend-becomes-lead scenarios

Kai is now Dev’s squad lead. For each situation, decide what each of them should do, and why.

  1. Kai has to tell Dev that her last piece of work missed something important, and the client noticed.
  2. Another squad member tells Dev, over coffee, that they think Kai is favouring her.
  3. At Thursday dinner, Kai asks Dev what the squad really thinks of a new designer who is struggling.
  4. Dev disagrees with a priority call Kai made in the squad meeting.
Reveal one way to play it
  1. Kai should say it in the one-to-one, promptly and specifically, the way they would for anyone. Softening it because Dev is a friend would be unfair to Dev, who needs the information, and to the squad, who would notice. Dev’s job is to hear it as the lead speaking, not the friend judging. Chapter 13 covers what makes feedback useful rather than wounding.
  2. Dev should take it seriously rather than defensively, and tell Kai, without naming the colleague. The perception matters even if it is wrong. Kai can then check the facts (who got which projects, and why) and make the fairness visible. Leaders really do form relationships of different quality with different people [29], so the question is a fair one to ask.
  3. Dev should decline, kindly: “That feels like a work question. Ask me on Tuesday, or better, ask her.” Answering would put Dev in the position of informing on a colleague to her lead over dinner, which is the hats problem in its purest form. Kai, on reflection, should not have asked.
  4. Dev should disagree with the lead in the place she would disagree with any lead: in the meeting if that is the team’s norm, or privately and soon, framed around the squad’s goals. Not at dinner, and not with an extra “as your friend”. Disagreeing well with a manager carries real risk, even with friends, and Chapter 16 covers how to do it so it lands.

The common thread: each person keeps doing their role properly, and the friendship survives because it is not asked to carry the role.

Answering the pause#

What did you write down as the first thing that would change? Many people write something like “we couldn’t be honest any more” or “they’d stop telling me things”. The evidence, thin as it is, suggests the real danger is quieter: not a dramatic break, but a gradual pulling-back that nobody names, as personal topics fade and invitations dry up [27]. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple, and it is the opposite of the danger: talk about it.

INT. THE NOODLE BAR — THURSDAY, THREE WEEKS LATER

Kai’s first week as squad lead is done. They have survived three one-to-ones, a planning meeting and their sister’s wedding, in that order.

On the counter between their bowls is a sticky note in Kai’s handwriting. It says TUESDAYS = LEAD. THURSDAYS = NOODLES.

Kai Rule one: if I ever have to give you bad news, it happens on a Tuesday. In the room. With notes.
Dev Rule two: if I ever think you’re being a bad lead, I tell you on a Tuesday. Not here.
Kai Rule three: nobody’s review gets mentioned at this counter. Including yours.
Dev Rule four: you’re still wrong about the blindfold show.
Kai That’s not a rule, that’s a lie.

They shake on it. On the way to the station Dev realises that this is the first time she has ever negotiated the terms of a friendship out loud, and that it made the friendship feel more solid, not less.

Dev now has a register (Chapter 14) and a friend with rules. She is going to need both. Next month, Lanternfish hires a new engineer whose profile headline reads “Visionary-in-Residence”, a colleague presents one of Dev’s fixes as his own, and the coffee machine becomes the subject of a committee. Chapter 16 is about conflict, credit and politics, and about the people who make work difficult.

Rewind
  • Work friendships are a mixed blessing. Friends at work go with higher performance ratings, through trust and mood, and with more exhaustion, because friendship’s rules (voluntary, communal, emotional) clash with work’s (formal, constrained, exchange-based).
  • Friendly is not the same as friends. Be friendly with nearly everyone; be friends with a few, knowing that this is where the rules collide.
  • Closeness takes time, and work time counts less. Roughly 50, 90 and 200-plus hours to casual, ordinary and close friendship, mostly from non-work time. People underestimate how much others like them and how willing others are to connect.
  • Loneliness is a quiet cost. About one in five employees reported loneliness for much of the previous day, more among remote workers, and lonelier people are seen as less approachable.
  • Gossip is a reputation system. Most of it is neutral; some of it protects people. Share what protects, assume everything is repeated, and leave other people’s private lives alone.
  • The active ingredient of drinks is showing up. Romance is mostly about power: peers are largely their own business; reporting lines are not.
  • When a friend becomes your lead, name the change. Separate the hats, be visibly fair, expect the friendship to change shape, and agree an exit. The evidence is thin; the alternative, silent drift, is what the evidence shows going wrong.

    Strong evidence experiments + longitudinal opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: After conversations, people underestimate how much their partners liked them ("liking gap"), and the gap persisted for months among new dorm-mates.

    Caveats: Replicated across samples and in mastroianni2021.

  6. Mastroianni, A. M., Cooney, G., Boothby, E. J., & Reece, A. G. (2021). The liking gap in groups and teams. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 162, 109–122. doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.10.013 ↩

    Moderate evidence studies of groups and teams opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The liking gap appears in groups and work teams, is largest between peers, and relates to how well teams can work together.

    Caveats: Abstract read via Semantic Scholar.

  7. Epley, N., & Schroeder, J. (2014). Mistakenly seeking solitude. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143(5), 1980–1999. doi.org/10.1037/a0037323 ↩

    Strong evidence field and lab experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Commuters told to talk to a stranger had a more pleasant (and no less productive) ride, though others predicted the opposite; people underestimate others' interest in connecting.

    Caveats: Not workplace, but the mechanism generalises to talking to new colleagues.

  8. Flynn, F. J., & Lake, V. K. B. (2008). If you need help, just ask: Underestimating compliance with direct requests for help. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 95(1), 128–143. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128 ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence experiments + field studies opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People underestimate by as much as 50% how likely others are to agree to a direct request for help, because they overlook how awkward it is to say no.

    Caveats: Replicated conceptually by later work (e.g., givi2024 on the other side of the request).

  9. Jecker, J., & Landy, D. (1969). Liking a Person as a Function of Doing Him a Favour. Human Relations, 22(4), 371–378. doi.org/10.1177/001872676902200407 ↩

    Weak evidence lab experiment opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The original "Ben Franklin effect" test - people asked personally by the experimenter to return prize money (a favour to him) later liked him more than those not asked.

    Caveats: Single small 1969 study; web search found no direct replication. Abstract not machine-readable; design described from secondary sources.

  10. Niiya, Y. (2016). Does a Favor Request Increase Liking Toward the Requester?. The Journal of Social Psychology, 156(2), 211–221. doi.org/10.1080/00224545.2015.1095706 ↩

    Moderate evidence experiment (two countries) opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Japanese and American participants who were asked for help by a confederate liked her more than baseline; helping without being asked did not raise liking.

    Caveats: A modern conceptual replication of the Ben Franklin idea, small samples.

  11. Lindenfors, P., Wartel, A., & Lind, J. (2021). ‘Dunbar's number’ deconstructed. Biology Letters, 17(5), 20210158. doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2021.0158 ↩

    Strong evidence statistical reanalysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Re-running the primate brain-size analysis behind "Dunbar's number" (150) gives very different averages with confidence intervals from about 2 to 520 - no single cognitive limit can be derived this way.

    Caveats: Dunbar disputes it; at minimum "150" is not settled.

  12. Pendell, R. (2024). 1 in 5 Employees Worldwide Feel Lonely. Gallup Workplace, 11 June 2024. www.gallup.com/workplace/645566/employees-worldwide-feel-lonely.aspx ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence global survey commercial source opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: About 1 in 5 employees worldwide reported feeling lonely "a lot of the day yesterday"; fully remote 25%, hybrid 21%, fully on-site 16%.

    Caveats: Large probability-based survey but commercial publisher; single-item loneliness measure; associations not causal.

  13. Ozcelik, H., & Barsade, S. G. (2018). No Employee an Island: Workplace Loneliness and Job Performance. Academy of Management Journal, 61(6), 2343–2366. doi.org/10.5465/amj.2015.1066 ↩

    Moderate evidence time-lagged field study opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Lonelier employees had lower supervisor-rated performance, explained by being seen as less approachable and feeling less committed.

    Caveats: Field correlational but with time lags and supervisor ratings.

  14. Dores Cruz, T. D., Nieper, A. S., Testori, M., Martinescu, E., & Beersma, B. (2021). An Integrative Definition and Framework to Study Gossip. Group &amp; Organization Management, 46(2), 252–285. doi.org/10.1177/1059601121992887 ↩

    Strong evidence systematic review of definitions opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Defines gossip broadly as a sender talking to a receiver about an absent target; valence can be positive, neutral or negative.

    Caveats: Definitional review.

  15. Robbins, M. L., & Karan, A. (2020). Who Gossips and How in Everyday Life?. Social Psychological and Personality Science, 11(2), 185–195. doi.org/10.1177/1948550619837000 ↩

    Moderate evidence naturalistic audio sampling opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Most gossip is neutral, not malicious, and is mostly about social information; extraverts gossip more; the gender stereotype mostly fails (women only did more neutral gossip).

    Caveats: Everyday life, not only workplaces. A widely quoted "52 minutes a day" figure was not in the abstract checked here - do not use it unless verified.

  16. Feinberg, M., Willer, R., Stellar, J., & Keltner, D. (2012). The virtues of gossip: Reputational information sharing as prosocial behavior. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 102(5), 1015–1030. doi.org/10.1037/a0026650 ↩

    Moderate evidence experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: "Prosocial gossip" - warning others about someone who behaved exploitatively - is driven by frustration at unfairness, relieves it, and deters selfishness.

    Caveats: Lab economic games.

  17. Feinberg, M., Willer, R., & Schultz, M. (2014). Gossip and Ostracism Promote Cooperation in Groups. Psychological Science, 25(3), 656–664. doi.org/10.1177/0956797613510184 ↩

    Moderate evidence experiment opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Gossip plus the ability to exclude lets groups sustain cooperation; excluded people then cooperate more.

    Caveats: Lab setting.

  18. Bushman, B. J. (2002). Does Venting Anger Feed or Extinguish the Flame? Catharsis, Rumination, Distraction, Anger, and Aggressive Responding. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 28(6), 724–731. doi.org/10.1177/0146167202289002 ↩

    Moderate evidence experiment opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Venting anger by hitting a punching bag while thinking about the provoker increased anger and aggression; doing nothing worked better.

    Caveats: Lab; consistent with later meta-analyses on venting.

  19. Sayette, M. A., Creswell, K. G., Dimoff, J. D., Fairbairn, C. E., Cohn, J. F., Heckman, B. W., et al. (2012). Alcohol and Group Formation. Psychological Science, 23(8), 869–878. doi.org/10.1177/0956797611435134 ↩

    Strong evidence randomised lab experiment opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: A moderate dose of alcohol increased positive emotional displays and self-reported bonding among groups of strangers.

    Caveats: Moderate dose, strangers, lab. Explains why drinks build rapport - it says nothing about excluding non-drinkers, which is the workplace cost.

  20. Frone, M. R. (2006). Prevalence and distribution of alcohol use and impairment in the workplace: a U.S. national survey. Journal of Studies on Alcohol, 67(1), 147–156. doi.org/10.15288/jsa.2006.67.147 ↩

    Strong evidence national probability phone survey opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: About 15% of the US workforce reported workplace alcohol use or impairment in the past year (e.g. 9.2% worked with a hangover; 7.1% drank during the workday); managers reported elevated rates.

    Caveats: US data, now dated (collected early 2000s).

  21. Peters, B. L., & Stringham, E. (2006). No booze? You may lose: Why drinkers earn more money than nondrinkers. Journal of Labor Research, 27(3), 411–421. doi.org/10.1007/s12122-006-1031-y ↩

    Weak evidence observational (survey data) self-interested source opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: In General Social Survey data, self-reported drinkers earned 10-14% more than abstainers; men who went to bars monthly earned about 7% more again. Authors argue social capital is the mechanism.

    Caveats: Correlational, heavy confounding; promoted by a policy brief from Reason Foundation where one author is an adjunct scholar. Abstract read on reason.org.

  22. Society for Human Resource Management (2023). New SHRM Survey: Workplace Romance 2023. SHRM press release, 7 February 2023. www.shrm.org/about/press-room/new-shrm-survey-workplace-romance-2023 ↩

    Weak evidence online survey (press release) self-interested source opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: 27% of US workers say they are or have been in a workplace romance; of these, 57% cite love as the motivation.

    Caveats: Professional-association survey; press-release detail only; self-report.

  23. Malachowski, C. C., Chory, R. M., & Claus, C. J. (2012). Mixing Pleasure with Work: Employee Perceptions of and Responses to Workplace Romance. Western Journal of Communication, 76(4), 358–379. doi.org/10.1080/10570314.2012.656215 ↩

    Moderate evidence experiment (vignette) opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Coworkers see a peer dating a superior as job-motivated, as getting unfair advantages, and trust them less (and deceive them more) than a peer dating someone of another status.

    Caveats: Hypothetical scenarios.

  24. Pierce, C. A., Byrne, D., & Aguinis, H. (1996). Attraction in organizations: A model of workplace romance. Journal of Organizational Behavior, 17(1), 5–32. doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-1379(199601)17:1<5::AID-JOB734>3.0.CO;2-E ↩

    Moderate evidence conceptual model opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Model of how workplace romances form and their good and bad effects on the couple, coworkers and the organisation.

    Caveats: Theory.

  25. Sonnentag, S. (2012). Psychological Detachment From Work During Leisure Time. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 21(2), 114–118. doi.org/10.1177/0963721411434979 ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Mentally switching off from work during leisure relates to better life satisfaction and less strain without lower engagement at work.

    Caveats: Short review of a robust literature.

  26. Givi, J., & Kirk, C. P. (2024). Saying no: The negative ramifications from invitation declines are less severe than we think. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 126(6), 1103–1115. doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000443 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People who decline invitations overestimate how angry or hurt the inviter will be and how much it will cost future invitations.

    Caveats: Social invitations, not work requests; recent (2024).

  27. Sias, P. M., Heath, R. G., Perry, T., Silva, D., & Fix, B. (2004). Narratives of Workplace Friendship Deterioration. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 21(3), 321–340. doi.org/10.1177/0265407504042835 ↩ ↩ ↩

    Weak evidence qualitative narratives opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Workplace friendships deteriorate through five main causes, one of which is promotion; people mostly withdraw indirectly (avoiding personal topics, socialising less).

    Caveats: Small qualitative study; but the only direct evidence found on promotion damaging workplace friendships.

  28. Graen, G. B., & Uhl-Bien, M. (1995). Relationship-based approach to leadership: Development of leader-member exchange (LMX) theory of leadership over 25 years: Applying a multi-level multi-domain perspective. The Leadership Quarterly, 6(2), 219–247. doi.org/10.1016/1048-9843(95)90036-5 ↩

    Strong evidence theory and review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Leader-member exchange (LMX) - leaders form relationships of different quality with each team member, and these relationships matter for outcomes.

    Caveats: No machine-readable abstract; widely known model.

  29. Dulebohn, J. H., Bommer, W. H., Liden, R. C., Brouer, R. L., & Ferris, G. R. (2012). A Meta-Analysis of Antecedents and Consequences of Leader-Member Exchange. Journal of Management, 38(6), 1715–1759. doi.org/10.1177/0149206311415280 ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: LMX quality is driven most by leader behaviours and perceptions, also by follower traits and the relationship itself; LMX mediates many links to outcomes.

    Caveats: Published online 2011, volume 38 (2012).
