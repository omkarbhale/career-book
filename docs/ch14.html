INT. LANTERNFISH, THE BIG MEETING ROOM — FRIDAY, 4:10 PM

The monthly all-hands. Two hundred people, half of them in the room and half of them in little squares on the screen behind it. Dev has four minutes to demo the new release dashboard, and it is going well. The charts load. Nobody’s laptop dies. Tomás nods at the back.

She reaches the last slide, a chart showing that releases now ship within a day of the date the team promised.

Dev So the short version is: our release dates are now more accurate than the sales forecast.

The engineers laugh. The squares on the screen laugh. The front two rows, which is where the sales team sits, do not. Lanternfish’s new head of sales, three weeks into the job and two days after a quarter that missed its number, smiles the smile of someone who will remember this.

Dev says “Thanks, everyone,” and sits down. Her face is hot for the next twenty minutes.

In the kitchen afterwards, Kai finds her staring at Old Faithful, which is displaying an error code nobody has seen before.

Kai It was a good demo.
Dev It was a good demo with a joke at the end that I would like to delete from history.
Kai Want to know the secret? Look at this.

Kai holds up their phone. It is the #old-faithful chat channel, which started as a place to report the machine’s moods and has become something else: three hundred messages of haiku, mock incident reports and a running poll on the machine’s star sign.

Kai Notice anything?
Dev People here are funny.
Kai People here joke at the machine. Never at each other. It’s the one colleague who can’t be hurt.

Dev’s joke was not a disaster. Nobody was fired, and by Monday most people had forgotten it. But it was a small, public lesson in something every working person has to learn, usually by getting it wrong: how much of yourself to turn up, and when.

This chapter is about that setting. When to be formal and when to be casual, which jokes help, how much of your life to share, why “professional” costs some people more than others, how culture changes the rules, and how to be present at work when you are not in the room. Underneath all of it is one idea from “Why acting doesn’t scale” in Chapter 1: being yourself does not mean being unfiltered. It means choosing your volume without changing who you are.

Pause

Before you read on: what exactly did Dev get wrong? Was it making a joke at all, the joke itself, or something else? Write down your answer; the chapter comes back to it.

Professionalism is a dial, not a costume#

Most people carry a picture of “being professional” as a costume: a set of clothes, words and manners you put on at the door and take off at home. The costume picture causes two opposite problems. Some people wear it all day and become stiff, distant and tiring to be around, which is what happened to “Corporate Dev” in her first weeks (Chapter 11). Others reject the costume altogether (“I’m just going to be myself”) and then wonder why a joke that works in the pub dies in the boardroom.

A better picture is a dial. At one end is the register you would use when presenting to a client’s board; at the other, the one you would use at lunch with a work friend. Most moments sit somewhere in between, and the skill is turning the dial to fit the room. Not every part of you moves when you turn it, though. Your words, volume, jokes and how much you share all change. Your values, your honesty and how you treat people do not. That is our argument, not a finding our view, but the research on authenticity backs the outline of it, as the next section shows.

What sets the dial? Five things, mostly:

  • The audience. Strangers, senior people and clients usually call for a more formal setting than teammates you have worked with for a year.
  • The stakes. Bad news, conflict and anything involving money or someone’s job deserve a notch more care than a status update.
  • The medium. Writing loses tone. People overestimate how well their sarcasm and humour come across in email, because when they reread their own message they “hear” the tone they meant [1]. Chapter 13 has the full story; for the dial, it means a joke that works out loud should be turned down in writing.
  • The culture. Some workplaces, and some societies, have stronger norms and tolerate less deviation from them than others; researchers call these “tight” cultures and the others “loose” [2]. More on culture later in the chapter.
  • Your standing. What you can get away with depends on how established you are, which is unfair and real, and gets its own section below.

Dev’s all-hands moment fails on three of the five at once. Her audience was mixed, and some of it was senior and new to her. The stakes were high for the people the joke was about, who had just missed a target. And she had no standing with them yet. Hold on to that; the section on humour shows why it mattered more than the joke itself.

Why adjusting isn’t faking#

The dial raises an obvious objection, and it comes straight from Part II’s thesis. If the long game rewards being yourself, isn’t adjusting your behaviour to the room exactly the act this book keeps warning against?

“The chameleon problem” in Chapter 10 has already answered it, so here is the short version. High self-monitors, the chameleons who adjust to every room, tend to perform slightly better and to emerge as leaders more often [3], but on leadership effectiveness the only ratings that favour them are their own [4]. People feel most authentic when they act like their best selves, not their default selves [5]. And what goes with damaged well-being is surface acting, faking feelings you do not have, while deep acting shows only weak links to strain [6].

Turning the dial is none of those things. You are not changing your story for each audience, and you are not faking a feeling. Choosing quieter words in a client meeting is choosing a register, the way you speak differently to a child and to a judge without becoming a different person.

Start one notch up#

If the dial has to be set, where should you start when you do not yet know the room? The evidence points in one direction, and it is the boring one: when in doubt, start slightly more formal, and let the other person turn the dial down.

You have already met the smallest version of this, in “Start a notch more formal, then let the room set the level” in Chapter 11 and again in “Emoji, exclamation marks and the first message” in Chapter 13: in work-related first contacts, a smiley made the sender seem less competent and no warmer, and how much it mattered depended on how formal the context was [7].

The same pattern shows up with bigger signals. Deliberately breaking a dress or formality code, like wearing red trainers to a meeting where everyone else is in suits, can raise how high-status and competent people think you are, but only when the observers know the setting and its norms and believe you broke them on purpose [8]. One of that paper’s co-authors has had other papers retracted; this one has not been, but treat it as one study rather than a law. Our reading, which the paper did not test directly: the same break that reads as confidence from someone established reads as cluelessness from someone new, because nobody yet knows whether you meant it our view.

Swearing follows the same rule, with thinner evidence. In one small study of written conversations, speakers who swore were rated lower on overall impression, intelligence and trustworthiness [9 weak]. Other researchers argue that in some teams swearing builds solidarity and lets off steam [10 weak]. Both can be true, depending on the room. A reasonable rule: mirror the room, never lead with it towards strangers or seniors, and never aim it at a person our view.

So the default for new rooms is: start one notch more formal than you think you need, and match down rather than up. Let a client, a senior colleague or a new team set the casual tone; once they have, follow them. It costs very little to be slightly too formal for a week, and a fair amount to be too casual in the first meeting our view.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

How experienced people read a new room, in practice:

  • Read before you write. Scroll back a few weeks in the team’s chat and see how people greet each other, disagree and joke. Then write like the median message, not the funniest one.
  • Watch the second most senior person. The most senior person can break norms (see the red trainers above); the person just below them shows you the actual rule.
  • Copy the incoming register. If someone writes “Hi Devika, I hope this finds you well,” reply in kind. If they write “hey quick q,” you can relax.
  • Notice how disagreement is done. A team that disagrees bluntly in meetings may still expect softness in writing, or the other way round.

Try the dial on a few real situations before you need it.

Your Move 14.1Read the room

For each situation, choose a dial setting from 1 (most formal) to 5 (most casual), and say in one line what would change your answer.

  1. Your first message to a client’s head of operations, introducing yourself as their new technical contact.
  2. The team chat, on a Friday afternoon, after a release has gone out cleanly.
  3. A message to your manager’s manager, whom you have never spoken to, asking for fifteen minutes of their time.
  4. An incident review after an outage that cost a customer money.
  5. A one-to-one with a teammate you have worked beside for a year, about a mistake they made.
  6. A thank-you note to a colleague in another country who stayed late to help you.
Reveal one way to play it

There is no single right number, but here is a defensible set, with the reasoning that matters more than the number.

  1. 1 or 2. A stranger, a client, a first impression: the smiley research is about exactly this moment [7]. Turn it down once they write back casually.
  2. 4 or 5. The room has set the register already. Celebrate; a joke about the release (or the coffee machine) is fine.
  3. 2. Senior and unknown, so start formal, but keep it short and clear: formality is about respect, not length. A crisp three-line message beats a grovelling ten-line one.
  4. 2, whatever the team’s usual register. The stakes are high and someone may be feeling responsible. Humour here, even gentle humour, can read as not taking the customer’s loss seriously.
  5. 3 or 4. Warm, private and specific. The relationship lets you be informal; the topic means you should be careful. Informal is not the same as flippant.
  6. 3. Warm and specific, but remember that their norms may differ and that tone gets lost in writing [1]. Say exactly what they did and what it made possible; that lands in every culture we know of our view.

Notice that the relationship and the stakes move the dial more than the medium does. And notice item 5: a casual relationship with a serious topic still calls for a few notches of care.

Humour: a high-variance bet#

Back to the all-hands. Dev’s instinct to make a joke was not wrong: humour at work is not frivolous. A meta-analysis of 49 studies with more than 8,500 people found that positive humour from employees and from supervisors goes with better performance, satisfaction, team cohesion and health, and with less burnout and withdrawal [11]. Those are correlations (happier, better-run teams may simply laugh more), and the authors stress that they were studying positive humour, not the derogatory kind, which carries legal and relational risks of its own.

Humour also does something for the person telling the joke. Across eight experiments, a team of researchers found that successful humour raises the teller’s status, because it signals both confidence and competence [12]. Failed or inappropriate humour still signals confidence, but it signals low competence, and the teller’s status drops. That is precisely what happened to Dev: she sounded confident, and to the front two rows she sounded like someone who does not understand the room.

The payoff is not equal for everyone, either. In one experiment with 216 participants, the same humour raised status ratings when it came from a man and lowered them when it came from a woman, with knock-on effects on how participants rated their performance and leadership capability [13]. One study is not the last word, but it is a warning that the “high variance” of humour is higher for some people than for others. The unfairness is not yours to fix by yourself; knowing about it is still useful.

So humour is a bet with an uneven payoff: a moderate upside when it lands and a steeper downside when it does not, with the audience, not the teller, deciding which happened. It helps to know which jokes are low-risk.

The ranking explains the #old-faithful channel. Lanternfish’s in-house comedy is almost entirely on rungs one and two: people joke about their own mistakes and about a machine that cannot be offended. That is not because Lanternfish employs unusually kind people. It is because jokes on the low rungs have no victim, so the only way they can fail is by not being funny, which costs very little.

Dev’s joke sat on rung five. It was about a group, the sales team, in front of that group, delivered by someone with no relationship with them, on a bad week. Every one of those features raised the downside, and none of them raised the upside.

Recovering from a joke that died#

What should Dev do now? The research on repairing trust gives a useful steer. When a lapse looks like a failure of competence rather than of integrity, a straightforward apology repairs trust better than a denial [14], and a misjudged joke is a competence lapse, a failure to read the room, not a moral one. Chapter 12 covers the shape of a good apology. For a joke, the version that works is short, private and specific:

Hi, I owe you a quick apology for the forecast joke at the all-hands. It was a cheap laugh at your team’s expense in a hard week, and I’m sorry. The dashboard is yours to use if it helps with the pipeline review.

Notice what it does not do. It does not explain the joke. It does not say “if anyone was offended”. It does not ask the other person to reassure Dev that it was fine. And it does not happen in public, because a public apology for a small thing makes the small thing bigger.

Trapdoor

“It was just a joke.” When a joke lands badly, the reflex is to defend it, and the defence nearly always moves the problem onto the audience: they are oversensitive, they cannot take a joke. That turns a small competence lapse, misreading a room, into a question about your character and judgement, which is far more expensive. If a joke has died, let it go. Apologise if it hurt someone, and save your next one for a lower rung.

How much of you to share#

INT. LANTERNFISH, SQUAD STAND-UP — MONDAY, 9:30 AM

The squad goes round the circle. Yesterday, today, blockers.

Kai Yesterday, the onboarding flow. Today, more onboarding flow. Blockers: I’m running on four hours of sleep because my ex came round to collect a lamp and stayed three hours to explain my flaws, and my therapist says I need to stop letting people do that. So if I’m weird in the design review, that’s why.

Everyone laughs, and two people offer Kai coffee. Dev, who has not told anyone at work that she moved flat last month, says her update about the test runner and wonders whether she is doing this wrong.

Kai shares a great deal. Dev shares very little. Which of them has it right?

Neither, as a rule, because people genuinely differ in how much they want work and home to mix. Researchers call the two ends of the scale segmentation (keeping work and the rest of life separate) and integration (blending them), and people move between their roles every day, somewhere along that range [15]. What matters is not where you sit but whether your workplace fits. In one study of 325 employees across many occupations, the fit between how much separation people wanted and how much their workplace allowed predicted work-home conflict, stress and satisfaction [16]. In another, of 460 employees, “integrating” perks such as onsite childcare actually lowered satisfaction among people who preferred to keep things separate [17]. Dev is a segmenter; Kai is an integrator; both are normal.

A popular slogan says you should “bring your whole self to work”. The Part II researchers looked for a study that tests the slogan directly and found none. What exists is narrower. Feeling authentic at work goes with well-being and engagement [18]. But disclosure interacts with status. In three experiments, when a higher-status colleague disclosed a personal weakness, they lost status and influence, and task conflict in the group went up; the same disclosure from a peer did no harm [19]. And a theory paper argues that across differences in status and background, sharing personal information can sometimes widen the gap between people rather than close it [20]. That fits a wider review of authenticity at work, which concludes that the evidence “both reinforces and tempers” the popular enthusiasm, and that power shapes who can afford to be authentic [21].

Put together, the defensible version of the slogan is less catchy: bring your whole values, and your chosen self, at a pace and depth that fit the role and the relationship our view. Disclosure has its own dial, separate from formality, with two settings worth thinking about:

  • Depth: how personal the thing is. Your weekend is shallow; your health, your relationships and your doubts about your own competence are deep.
  • Breadth: who hears it. The whole squad, the people you trust, or your manager in private.

Kai turns both up, and it works for Kai, partly because they are a peer sharing with peers: the research above suggests a manager telling the same story to their reports might pay a price that Kai does not. Dev keeps both low, which is fine, as long as “low” does not become “nothing”, because people do need something to know you by. A segmenter can share shallow things broadly (the new flat, the terrible film) and deep things narrowly, with the few people who have earned them. That is still being yourself; it is being yourself on a schedule.

Your Move 14.2The disclosure decision

For each of these, decide who (if anyone) at work should hear it, when, and how deep to go. Assume you are a few months into a job, with a manager you trust reasonably well.

  1. You have taken up rock climbing and are a bit obsessed.
  2. A parent is ill, and you will need to leave early twice a week for the next two months.
  3. You are interviewing for another job.
  4. You think the plan your manager announced on Monday is a mistake.
  5. You were turned down for a promotion at your last company and are worried it will happen again.
  6. You have a health condition that does not affect your work most days but occasionally will.
Reveal one way to play it

These are judgement calls, and the reasoning matters more than the verdict.

  1. Anyone, any time, shallow. Low-depth, positive, and it gives people something to know you by. Climbing stories are a segmenter’s best friend.
  2. Your manager, early, at the depth needed to plan. “A parent is ill and I need to leave at 3 on Tuesdays and Thursdays for about two months; here’s how I’ll cover my work” is enough. Telling people before the schedule bites is what turns a disruption into a plan (Chapter 12). You can share more with friends if you want to; you do not owe it to anyone.
  3. Almost certainly nobody, for now. This one has real risks and few benefits until there is an offer, and Chapter 9 covers how to leave well when there is one. The exception is a manager who has explicitly offered to support your growth, even if it means leaving, and has shown that they mean it.
  4. Your manager, privately, soon, framed around shared goals. This is not self-disclosure but voice, and voice that challenges a manager carries real risk: in one study, managers rated employees who voiced challenging ideas as worse performers than those who voiced supportive ones [22]. Chapter 16 is about how to raise it so it is heard.
  5. Probably nobody yet, or one trusted peer. It is a deep disclosure of a perceived weakness, and it is about the past. Better: ask your manager early how promotion works here and what it would take, which gets you the useful part without the disclosure. Chapter 19 has the detail.
  6. It depends, and it is your call. Many people tell their manager or HR so that adjustments are easy when needed; others prefer not to, and research on disclosing invisible identities describes it as a continuing decision, not a one-off, with real strain in managing different disclosures in different places [23]. What you are entitled to varies by country and is a legal question; “Employment law and workplace disputes” in “Left Out, on Purpose” says where to check.

The pattern: share shallow things widely and early, practical things with the people who need them in time to plan, and deep things narrowly, at your own pace.

Code-switching, and who pays for “professional”#

So far the dial has sounded like a personal skill with personal costs. For some people the costs are much higher, and the honest version of this chapter has to say so.

INT. LANTERNFISH, ROOF TERRACE — WEDNESDAY, 12:45 PM

Kai and Dev are eating lunch in the only patch of sun.

Dev Can I ask you something? You’re so yourself at work. Doesn’t it ever feel risky?
Kai Sure. Every client call, someone says “he” about me in the first five minutes, and I have about a second to decide whether to correct them. Correct them, and the call is about me for a moment. Don’t, and I spend the hour being someone I’m not.
Dev What do you usually do?
Kai Depends on the client, the stakes, whether Tomás is on the call. That’s the thing: you’ve seen me in stand-up. You haven’t seen the forty tiny decisions it took to get to stand-up.
Dev I had a whole work voice at Corvid. Corporate Dev. I thought everyone did.
Kai Everyone does. The difference is what it costs to put down.

Code-switching means adjusting your speech, appearance, behaviour or self-expression to fit the dominant norms of a setting. Researchers have studied one form closely: racial code-switching by Black professionals. In two experiments featuring a fictitious Black lawyer, Courtney McCluney and colleagues found that raters, Black and White alike, judged the lawyer more professional when the lawyer code-switched than when they did not [24]. That is how strong the incentive is. In a summary for practitioners, the same researchers argue that code-switching carries a psychological toll and can contribute to burnout, while noting that the findings they draw on are largely self-reported [25].

The pattern generalises beyond race. Theory papers describe employees, especially those from minority groups, putting up “facades of conformity”, pretending to share values they do not hold, with distress as the likely cost [26], and describe the decision to disclose an invisible identity as one that never stops being made [23]. The evidence here is thinner than for the experiments above, so treat it as a strong argument rather than a measured effect. But it points the same way as the emotional-labour research in Chapter 10: the costly part of an act is keeping it up.

This changes what the dial means. For Dev, dropping Corporate Dev was cheap: nobody penalised her for relaxing. For other people, the “professional” end of the dial is a version of themselves that the workplace rewards and that takes daily effort to maintain. Being yourself is easier for some people than for others, which is what Chapter 1 promised the book would deal with honestly (“Why acting doesn’t scale” in Chapter 1).

Author’s ViewIf the dial costs you more

Our opinion: argued, not proven

If you are someone for whom “professional” means hiding more, we do not think you owe anyone more authenticity than is safe. Adjusting to survive is not a moral failure, and a book that told you to “just be yourself” without counting the cost would be selling something. Choose where you spend the effort: many people find a few relationships where the dial can go all the way down, and that is often enough.

If you are someone for whom the dial is cheap, you have a different job. Status lets you widen the range for others: the red-trainers research suggests that a broken norm reads as confidence when people can see it is deliberate [8], and that is far easier to show once you are established. Use the name people ask you to use. Laugh at the low-rung jokes and don’t laugh at the high-rung ones. Ask how people like to work instead of assuming. The cost of being yourself at work is partly set by the people around you, and you are one of them.

Culture: a hypothesis, not a passport#

Lanternfish sells to warehouses in several countries, and Dev’s squad works daily with an integrations team abroad. Her first written review of one of their designs went down badly: she listed eleven problems as bullet points, with no greeting and no sign of what she liked. Her colleague there replied, politely, that he would “consider the feedback”. Dev took this as agreement. It was not.

National culture does shape workplace norms, especially around directness and hierarchy. The best-known framework, Geert Hofstede’s, describes national cultures along six dimensions, among them power distance, uncertainty avoidance and individualism versus collectivism, and Hofstede himself warns against using national scores to describe individuals [27]. A practitioner framework, Erin Meyer’s Culture Map, gives useful vocabulary for the daily friction: one of its eight scales, “evaluating”, describes how directly negative feedback is given, which explains why one culture’s polite feedback sounds harsh, or vague, to another [28 weak]. The Culture Map is a teaching and consulting tool, not a tested model, and its country placements are generalisations.

How much do these national differences actually predict? Less than the airport books suggest. A meta-analysis of 598 studies with more than 200,000 people found that Hofstede’s original dimensions predict work outcomes with a modest average effect (ρ = .18), more for emotions and attitudes than for job performance, and more strongly in “tight” cultures [29]. And a later meta-analysis by the same team found that about 80% of the variation in cultural values lies within countries rather than between them [30]. Your colleague’s passport tells you something about the average person in their country, and rather little about them.

So use culture frameworks as hypotheses about a colleague, never as conclusions, and test the hypothesis the cheapest way: ask. “How do you like to get feedback on designs: written, on a call, all at once or in pieces?” takes ten seconds and beats any book about any country. The team’s own culture, the company’s and the individual’s often matter as much as the national one our view.

Dev’s repair was simple. She called her colleague, said that she had been too terse, walked through the three problems that really mattered, said what she thought was strong in the design, and asked how he preferred to get reviews. He preferred a short call first and the list afterwards. It turned out that he had grown up in a family of very blunt engineers and found her bullet points perfectly normal; what bothered him was getting them from someone he had never spoken to. Country was not the variable at all. Relationship was.

Field NoteDirectness and hierarchy

Across countries

The frameworks agree that countries differ, on average, in how directly people give negative feedback, how much they defer to rank, and how strongly they enforce norms [27; 2; 28 weak]. We have deliberately not printed a ranking of countries: the placements in these frameworks are averages, the effects they predict are modest [29], and most of the variation lies within countries rather than between them [30]. A ranking would invite exactly the passport-reading this section warns against.

What travels well: ask people how they like feedback; start a notch more formal with people senior to you until they signal otherwise; put the most important point in writing and say it aloud; and when a reply seems vague, check what it meant before acting on it. What we could not verify: any reliable data on how these norms differ inside a single multinational company by office, which is the comparison most readers will meet.

Generations get the same passport treatment, with even less justification.

Myth CheckGen Z and millennials are fundamentally different at workBusted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

A meta-analysis of 20 studies covering 19,961 workers found that generational differences in job satisfaction, organisational commitment and intention to quit were small to near zero [31]. That analysis predates Gen Z’s arrival at work, but in 2020 a US National Academies committee reviewed the research and concluded that sorting workers into generations to define their needs is not supported by the evidence; differences between workers more likely reflect age, career stage, experience and wider changes in work [32]. Young people do differ from older colleagues in some ways, mostly because they are young and new, which every generation has been. Treat “what Gen Z wants” articles like country rankings: as averages about large groups, with little to say about the person in front of you.

Remote and hybrid: being present without being in the room#

Half of the all-hands audience was on a screen. For many people, the professionalism dial now has a second control beside it: how much of their working week happens in the same room as their colleagues. The evidence on this is unusually strong, and it shows real trade-offs rather than a winner.

An older meta-analysis of 46 studies of telecommuting found small, mostly good effects on autonomy, work-family conflict, satisfaction and performance, but also that working from home more than about two and a half days a week harmed relationships with co-workers [39].

Three lessons fall out of the table. Hybrid looks safer than fully remote for careers: the two-day hybrid trial found no promotion penalty [35], while the fully remote one did [33]. Proximity is a mentoring subsidy: being near colleagues mostly helps the people who are still learning, and it costs the experienced people who do the teaching some of their own output [37]. And being seen counts, whether or not it should. In one study, simply being seen at work led observers to infer traits automatically: “dependable” for people seen during normal hours, “committed” for people seen outside them [40]. Distributed workers in another study compensated with visible signals of commitment, which won them better work but escalated into a sense of sacrifice that headquarters never noticed [41].

For Dev, this settles a question she had been dodging. She is new to development work, which is exactly the stage at which sitting near people pays most. So she comes in on the squad’s three office days, sits with the engineers rather than at the quiet desk by the window, and keeps her remote day for the work that needs silence our view. Kai, who has several years of design behind them, makes a different choice, and that is fine: the evidence says the right setting depends on your stage, your work and your team, not on a slogan.

Being in the room, though, is not the same as being with people.

Myth CheckOpen-plan offices increase collaborationBusted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

When two corporate headquarters moved from cubicles to open-plan offices, researchers measured interaction with sensors and digital records before and after. Face-to-face interaction fell by about 70%, and email and instant messaging rose [42]. It is two companies, but the design is strong for the question, and the result runs opposite to the reason most open-plan offices are built. Proximity helps when it creates real conversations, like the feedback in the engineering study above [37]; it does not help when people put on headphones to get some privacy.

Wherever you work, presence is something you can build deliberately. Visibility through information (clear updates, written decisions, demos of finished work) does more and costs less than visibility through hours our view, and it travels to the people who were not in the room. That distinction becomes important in Part III, when the question is no longer whether people see you working but whether the people who decide on your career can see what your work achieved.

For now, make your own presence concrete.

Your Move 14.3Your remote-presence plan

Draft a one-paragraph plan for how you will be present at work for the next three months. Answer these four questions in it:

  1. Stage. How much are you still learning from the people around you? Be honest.
  2. Days. If you have a choice, which days will you be in the same place as your team, and why those days?
  3. Signals. What will people who never see you in person see of your work each week? Name the actual artefact: an update, a demo, a document.
  4. Connection. Who will you talk to, live, at least once a fortnight, who is not on your immediate team?
Reveal one way to play it

A strong plan is specific about all four. For example: “I’m six months into a new kind of role, so I’m still learning a lot from the people around me: I’ll be in on the squad’s three office days and sit with the engineers. I’ll post a five-line Friday update in the team channel with what shipped, what I learned and what’s blocked, and demo the test dashboard at the monthly review. I’ll have a fortnightly call with someone on the integrations team abroad.”

Common weaknesses: planning only for days (“I’ll go in on Tuesdays”) with no plan for what people will see; planning only for visibility of effort (“I’ll stay online late”) rather than of results, which is exactly the escalating signal the distributed-work research warns about [41]; and leaving out connection beyond the team, when remote work tends to shrink precisely those ties [36].

If you are experienced and working mostly remotely, your plan might reasonably spend fewer days in the office, but it should include time to give feedback to someone more junior, since that is the part of proximity that is hardest to replace [37].

Answering the pause#

So what did Dev get wrong at the all-hands? Not making a joke: humour at work is, on average, a good thing [11]. Not even the joke’s content, which aimed at the machine would have been fine in the #old-faithful channel. She got the setting wrong: every feature of that room was a reason to turn the dial up a notch, and she turned it down.

That is the whole chapter in one example. The question is never “should I be formal or casual?” in general. It is “what does this room, this relationship and these stakes call for, from the same me?”

Ask Anyway

QIsn’t “professionalism” just a polite word for “act like the people in charge”?

ASometimes, yes, and the code-switching research shows the cost falls unevenly [24]. But part of professionalism is genuinely about other people: being clear, being on time, not making your colleagues carry your mood. Keep that part. Question the rest, and if you have the standing to widen the range for others, use it.

QI’m naturally quite formal. Do I need to loosen up?

ANot necessarily. Formal and warm is a perfectly good setting; what people find hard is formal and distant. If you want to turn the dial down a notch, do it with small, low-risk things: a sentence about your weekend, a thank-you that names what someone did, a joke about the coffee machine.

QMy manager swears constantly. Should I?

AYou don’t have to, and the little evidence there is suggests swearing can cost you in how people judge you, at least on paper [9 weak]. Mirroring your manager’s occasional swear word in the team room is unlikely to hurt. Swearing in front of clients or senior people because your manager does, or ever aiming it at a person, is a different matter.

Rewind
  • Professionalism is a dial, not a costume. Words, volume, jokes and how much you share change with the room; values, honesty and kindness don’t. If those move, it has become an act.
  • Adjusting isn’t faking. Adapting your self-presentation helps you get noticed and, modestly, get ahead, and people feel most authentic when acting like their best selves, not their default selves. What costs you is faking feelings, not choosing a register.
  • Start one notch up and match down. In first contact, a smiley lowered perceived competence; breaking a norm reads as confidence only when people can tell it is deliberate, which is easier once you are established.
  • Humour is a high-variance bet. Successful humour raises status; failed humour signals low competence. The payoff is not equal for everyone. Joke about yourself and the situation; from “a person in the room” upwards, someone else pays.
  • Share shallow things widely, practical things in time, deep things narrowly. People differ in how much they want work and home to blend, and disclosure costs more for higher-status people and across status gaps.
  • “Professional” costs some people more. Code-switching is rewarded and tiring; adjusting to survive is not a failure, and people with standing can widen the range for others.
  • Culture is a hypothesis, not a passport. National differences are real but modest, and about 80% of the variation in values lies within countries. Ask how people like to work. Generational differences are smaller still.
  • Hybrid has trade-offs, not a winner. Proximity helps juniors most; hybrid showed no promotion penalty where fully remote did; being seen counts. Make results visible, not hours.

  41. Cristea, I. C., & Leonardi, P. M. (2019). Get Noticed and Die Trying: Signals, Sacrifice, and the Production of Face Time in Distributed Work. Organization Science, 30(3), 552–572. doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2018.1265 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence qualitative field study opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Distributed employees compensate for missing face time with commitment signals, which win better assignments but escalate into a feeling of sacrifice that headquarters doesn't notice.

    Caveats: Qualitative/inductive.

  42. Bernstein, E. S., & Turban, S. (2018). The impact of the ‘open’ workspace on human collaboration. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences, 373(1753), 20170239. doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2017.0239 ↩

    Moderate evidence field studies with wearables opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: After two headquarters moved to open-plan offices, face-to-face interaction fell about 70% and email/IM rose.

    Caveats: Two firms; strong design for the question.
