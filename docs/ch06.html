INT. DEV'S FLAT — TUESDAY, 11:15 PM

The job ad is perfect, which is the problem. Backend Developer, Lanternfish. Help us build the software that keeps 4,000 lorries on time. Testing background welcome. Python. Queues. Dev has a story in her story bank for every line of it, and on Monday morning she sent it a résumé that finally says what she did instead of what she was for.

Tonight she has the ad open again, and she notices something she missed on Monday. Under the button that says Apply there is a small grey line: Over 200 applicants.

She has read Chapter 3. She knows what 200 applicants means. She can feel her new, honest, carefully evidenced résumé sliding into a pile the size of a small town.

Her phone buzzes. It is Rex, a few weeks into his new job, with news about his profile.

Rex New headline. Be honest. “Chief Possibility Officer.”
Dev What does it mean?
Rex That’s the beauty of it. Nobody knows. They have to message me to find out.

Dev puts the phone face down. Then she opens another tab, the one she found on Sunday while researching the company: Lanternfish’s people page. Third row, a face she knows. Ana, from her second-year databases course, who once lent her a charger during an exam and whom she has not spoken to in five years.

Dev I can’t message her. That would be using her.

Dev has just met the most over-performing channel in hiring, and her first instinct is to treat it as cheating. Most decent people have the same instinct. This chapter is about why it is wrong, and about the part of it that is right.

The short version: a large share of jobs are filled through people, not portals, and the evidence for that is strong. But the reason has little to do with schmoozing and a lot to do with what Chapter 1 called signals. A referral is a signal sent by someone who has watched part of your film. Your job is not to “network”. It is to let the people who already know your work say so, and to ask them well.

Pause

Before you read on: think of ten people who know your work, however slightly. If you sent each of them a short, polite message asking for a small, specific piece of help, how many do you think would say yes? Write down a number. You will check it at the end of the chapter.

The portal and the person#

Start with what Dev did on Monday: press Apply and join the 200.

You have met the headline number twice already, in “The loop” in Chapter 1 and “Not every route through the funnel is the same” in Chapter 3: in one large US corporation, people referred by a current employee were 6.1% of applicants but 29.1% of hires. Now look at the whole pool that firm tracked, all 62,127 applicants, split by how they arrived. Applicants from internet job boards were the mirror image of the referrals: 60.1% of applicants and 23.5% of hires [1].

That is one company, so check it against others. In a recruiting-software vendor’s customer data for 2024, referrals were 1.64% of applications and 17.09% of hires [2]. In data from more than 60,000 small businesses, referrals were 2% of applicants and 11.3% of hires [3]. Both vendors sell recruiting software, and their customers are not a random sample of employers, so treat the exact numbers loosely. The direction is the same in all three: a referred candidate is worth several cold ones.

There is a second detail in those numbers that matters as much as the first. Job boards still produced roughly a quarter of hires in the large firm and in the vendor data, and more than two-fifths in the small-business data [1; 2; 3]. Portals work. They just work at a much lower yield per application. You do not have to choose between applying and asking; you have to stop treating applying as the only move.

Why a referral works#

It is tempting to think a referral works because hiring is corrupt: friends hire friends, and the rest of us queue. Some of that happens, and we will come back to it. But the best evidence says the main mechanism is more boring and more useful. A referral carries information that a résumé cannot.

A study of nine large firms in call centres, trucking and high-tech found that referred workers were 10% to 30% less likely to quit than other hires, and did markedly better on rare but important measures such as patents and avoiding truck accidents, while being about the same on ordinary productivity [4]. When the researchers tested why, the evidence pointed to matching: referrers sent people who fitted the particular job, and people like themselves [4]. Field experiments in an online labour market reached a similar conclusion: referrals carried information about how well and how persistently someone would work that the employer could not get from the worker’s profile [5].

Now connect that to “Signals, not secrets” in Chapter 1. A strong signal is specific, checkable and costly to fake. A referral from someone who has worked with you scores on all three. It is specific (“she rebuilt our regression suite”), it is checkable (the hiring manager can ask the referrer), and it is expensive, because the referrer is spending their own reputation. If you turn out to be a disaster, it is their judgement that looks bad. That is why employers weight it so heavily, and why Your Move 1.1 rated “a former manager who vouches for you” as the strongest signal on the list.

Two consequences follow, and both shape how you should ask.

  • A referral is only as good as what the referrer knows. “I don’t really know her, but sure, put my name down” carries almost none of that information. The best referral comes from someone who has seen your work our view.
  • A referral gets you looked at, not hired. The biggest lift in the firm data is at the door: referred candidates were a far larger share of interviewees than of applicants [1]. After that you still have to interview, which is the subject of Chapter 7.
Myth Check“It’s not what you know, it’s who you know”Partly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

Half true, and the half that is false is the important half. Who you know clearly matters: referred candidates were 6.1% of applicants and 29.1% of hires in one firm [1]. But referrals mostly work by carrying information about what you know and how you work, which is why referred hires fit the job better and quit less [4; 5]. A contact who cannot vouch for your work opens a door that your work then has to walk through. The better version: it’s who knows what you can do.

Weak ties, strong ties#

So who should you ask? The classic answer comes from a 1973 paper with one of the best titles in social science: “The strength of weak ties”.

Mark Granovetter studied professional, technical and managerial workers in a Boston suburb who had recently changed jobs. Among the 54 who had found their job through a personal contact, only 16.7% saw that contact often; 55.6% saw them occasionally and 27.8% rarely, once a year or less [6]. His explanation was structural. Your close friends know the same people and hear the same news you do. Your acquaintances move in other circles, so they are the ones who hear about things you have not heard about [6].

That was 54 people, in one suburb, in the early 1970s. Nearly fifty years later the idea got a much bigger test. LinkedIn randomly varied the “People You May Know” suggestions shown to more than 20 million users, which changed how many weak and strong ties people formed, and then tracked who later moved to a connection’s company [7]. Weak ties did help, but not in a straight line. The relationship was an inverted U: moderately weak ties (people with whom you share a few mutual connections) were the most useful, and both the very weakest and the strongest were less so. Weak ties mattered more in digital industries; strong ties mattered more in less digital ones [7].

A study of 6 million Facebook users adds the last piece. Most people who got help finding a job got it through a weak tie, simply because they had so many of them, but any single strong tie was worth more than any single weak one [8]. Both things are true at once: you have dozens of acquaintances for every close friend, so most help arrives through acquaintances, even though each friend is more likely to help.

Put the three studies together and the advice gets specific. The people most likely to know about a job you have not heard of are the ones on the edge of your network: the ex-colleague from two jobs ago, the person from your course, the client you got on well with, the organiser of the meetup you went to twice. Ana, in other words. Your best friend may be more willing, but they hear the same news as you.

Myth Check“Weak ties are always better than strong ties”Partly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

Overstated. Granovetter’s insight holds up: acquaintances carry new information [6]. But the large LinkedIn experiment found an inverted U, with moderately weak ties most useful and the weakest ties less so, and strong ties more useful in less digital industries [7]. On Facebook, each strong tie was worth more than each weak tie, even though most help came through weak ties because people had so many [8]. Ask widely, but do not skip your friends.

One honest caveat about all of this. In the LinkedIn experiment, the chance that any single new connection led to a job was a fraction of one per cent [7]. Ties matter in aggregate, over years. Adding 300 strangers on a Tuesday night is not a job search; it is a Tuesday night.

Before going further, it is worth seeing what your own rings look like.

Your Move 6.1Map your ties

Draw three rings on a page, like Figure 6.2, with you at the centre.

  1. In the inner ring, write up to five people who know you well: close colleagues, friends in your field, a mentor.
  2. In the middle ring, write as many people as you can who know you a little and have seen some of your work: former teammates, classmates, clients, people from a community you take part in. Aim for twenty.
  3. Next to each middle-ring name, write one specific thing they have seen you do.
  4. Circle the three middle-ring people most likely to hear about roles you want.
Reveal one way to play it

Most people find the inner ring easy and the middle ring surprisingly long once they start: former colleagues from two jobs ago, the person who reviewed your code once, the classmate who went into your target industry. If your middle ring has fewer than ten names, that is useful information, not a verdict. It tells you where the long-game work is: picking one community and showing up in it regularly.

The third step is the important one. A name you cannot attach to a piece of work is an outer-ring tie wearing a middle-ring label. Those people may still pass on news of a job, but they cannot vouch for you, so ask them for information (“Is the team hiring? Who should I talk to?”) rather than a referral.

The “hidden job market”#

If you have read any careers advice at all, you have met a number that sounds like a conspiracy: 70%, or 80%, or 85% of jobs are never advertised. It is usually followed by a course you can buy.

The mechanism behind the claim is real. Surveys of job seekers have consistently found that 50% to 60% of workers report using a referral or contact to find their job [9]. In most European countries, between a quarter and two-fifths of people heard about their current job through informal contacts [10]. Referrals and internal moves together are a large share of hires in employer data [2].

But “found through a contact” is not the same as “never advertised”. Plenty of jobs are posted publicly and filled by someone a current employee recommended. And the percentage itself has no traceable origin: one review of the claim concluded that it “likely originated from anecdotal evidence and informal surveys rather than rigorous, peer-reviewed research” [11 weak].

Myth Check“80% of jobs are never advertised”Partly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

The percentage is unsupported; the mechanism is real. Nobody has traced the 70–80% figure to a study [11]. What the evidence does show is that half or more of workers use a contact to find a job [9], and that referred candidates are hugely over-represented among hires [1]. Many of those jobs were advertised too. So keep applying, and also ask: the two are not alternatives. (The grade is for the evidence on the mechanism; the percentage itself has none.)

Asking for help, and why you are wrong about the answer#

Back to Dev, staring at Ana’s photo. Her objection was not really “this is unfair”. It was “this is awkward, and she’ll say no, or she’ll say yes and resent it”.

On the first part, the evidence is clear that she is probably wrong. In a series of studies, with requests made both in the lab and out in the field, people who needed help underestimated how likely others were to agree to a direct request, by as much as half. The reason was a blind spot: they thought about what saying yes would cost the helper, and forgot what saying no would cost them [12]. And after conversations, people tend to underestimate how much the other person liked them, a “liking gap” that lasted for months among new university room-mates [13]. Put together: you are probably more liked than you think, and people are more willing to help than you think.

There is an ethical edge in that same finding, and it is worth taking seriously. If people say yes partly because refusing is costly, then a good ask makes refusing easy. That is not just politeness. It means the yeses you get are real ones, from people who actually want to help, which is the only kind of referral worth having our view.

On the second part, resentment, the answer is in how you ask. A good ask is short, specific and easy to act on, and it leaves a clean exit.

Each of the four parts earns its place.

  1. The bridge reminds them who you are and why you are writing to them rather than to anyone else. Specific beats warm: the shared course, the project, the conference talk.
  2. The specific ask says exactly what you want. “Would you refer me for this role?” can be answered. “Can I pick your brain?” is a request for an unspecified amount of an unspecified thing, and the honest answer to it is usually “not this month”.
  3. Make it easy puts the work on you. A referrer has to fill in a form or forward an email; give them the link, your résumé and two true sentences they can paste. Those sentences are their reputation on the line, so make them specific and checkable (“Signals, not secrets” in Chapter 1 again).
  4. The clean exit gives them a dignified way to say no, or to say “I don’t know your work well enough to refer you, but here’s who’s hiring”. That second answer is often more useful than a reluctant referral.

The structure is craft rather than science: nobody has run a trial of four-sentence messages against five-sentence ones craft. But each part follows from evidence you have now seen: referrals carry information, people are more willing than you expect, and a reluctant yes helps nobody.

The opposite of a good ask is one that costs the asker nothing and the helper everything: “Do you know anyone hiring?”, a referral request for a role you have not read, or the same message sent to 40 people with the name changed. Each hands the other person a job (working out what you want, whether you fit and what to say), and most people quietly decline by not replying. Do that work yourself, before you send anything.

Your Move 6.2Write a four-sentence ask

Pick one real person from your middle ring and one real role. Write a message to them using the four parts in Figure 6.3: the bridge, the specific ask, make it easy, and the clean exit. Four sentences, no apologies.

Then check it against these questions. Could they answer it with “yes” or “no”? Could they act on it in two minutes? Would a “no” feel easy to send?

Reveal one way to play it

Here is one Dev might send to the organiser of the testing meetup she went to twice, someone she knows less well than Ana:

Hi Jo, I’m Dev; I came to the testing meetup in March and asked about flaky tests after your talk. I’ve seen your company is hiring a backend developer and I’m planning to apply; would you be open to a 15-minute call about what the team needs, or to pointing me to the right person? I’ve put the role and a short summary of my background below so you don’t have to look anything up. And if you’re swamped, no worries at all.

Notice that it asks for information, not a referral, because Jo has heard Dev ask a question at a meetup, not seen her work. A referral request would put Jo in an awkward spot. The most common weaknesses in first drafts are an ask that is too vague (“any advice?”), too big (“could you get me an interview?”), or buried in the third paragraph. If yours runs to more than five sentences, the extra ones are probably apologies. Cut them.

Informational conversations#

Not every ask is for a referral. Often the most useful thing you can ask someone in the middle ring is twenty minutes of their time: what is the team like, what does the job really involve, what would make an application stand out? This is sometimes called an informational interview, and it works for the same reasons a referral does. You learn things that are not on the job ad, and the other person gets to see a little of how you think.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

Running an informational conversation. Ask for a specific, short slot (“twenty minutes, any day next week”), come with three real questions you could not answer from the website, and stop on time. Ask about them and their work before you ask about openings. End with two questions: “Is there anyone else you think I should talk to?” and “Would it be all right if I followed up when I apply?” Then send a two-line thank-you that mentions something specific they said. None of this has been tested in a study; it is what experienced people on both sides of these conversations tend to recommend craft.

Give before you need#

The best time to ask someone for help is after you have spent years being someone they would want to help. This is where the snapshot game borrows from the long game, and it is the first place the loop becomes something you can act on.

Reciprocity is one of the oldest findings in social psychology. In a classic study, people who were given an unsolicited soft drink by a stranger later bought more of the stranger’s raffle tickets, whether or not they liked the stranger [14]. But the pull fades: in another set of studies, people repaid a small favour five minutes later, but showed no reliable pull to repay it a week later [15]. So reciprocity is not a bank account you can fill once and draw on for a decade. It is closer to a habit: small, frequent, honest exchanges that keep relationships warm.

That suggests a different picture of “networking” from the one most people dread. Not working a room of strangers with a stack of business cards, but a few cheap habits, kept up over years our view:

  • Help in small ways, often. Answer the question in the team chat. Review the pull request. Send the article you read that made you think of them. Introduce two people who should know each other.
  • Keep the middle ring warm. When a former colleague changes jobs, congratulate them. When you read something by a classmate, say so. A message every year or so is enough to keep someone in the middle ring instead of the outer one.
  • Refer other people. When you see a role that fits someone you have worked with, tell them, and offer to refer them if you can honestly vouch for their work. It is the most direct way to be the kind of tie you hope to have.
  • Do work worth vouching for. This is the big one, and it is the whole of Part II. Nobody can refer you warmly for work they never saw or did not rate.
Author’s ViewWhy we think “networking” is the wrong word

Our opinion: argued, not proven

“Networking” makes it sound like a separate activity, done in a separate place, by a separate, more extrovert version of you. We think that is why so many people hate it and so few do it well. Almost everything that makes a referral valuable happens in the long game: the work people saw, the help you gave, the way you behaved when things went wrong. The snapshot-game part is small and learnable: noticing a role, asking clearly, making it easy. So if the word makes you wince, drop it. You are not networking. You are letting people who know your work say so.

The fairness problem#

Bea has a different objection to all of this, and it is a serious one.

INT. CORVID SYSTEMS, KITCHEN — WEDNESDAY, 12:40 PM

Dev tells Bea about Ana while the kettle boils.

Bea So the trick to getting hired is already knowing someone who works there.
Dev The trick is someone who’s seen your work saying so.
Bea Which is easier if your parents’ friends are engineers, you went to the right university and you play five-a-side with the hiring manager. I didn’t, and I don’t.
Dev You have me.
Bea I have you. Lucky me. What about everyone who doesn’t have a you?

Bea is right, and the book should say so plainly. People tend to refer people like themselves, in characteristics and even in behaviour [4]. In two firms’ data, referral hiring brought more women in at every job level, but less so at higher levels, the pattern you would expect where an established male network is in place [16]. And the kind of contact matters: in US survey data, referrals from business contacts were used more for high-skill jobs and came with higher starting wages, while referrals from family and friends were used more for low-skill jobs and came with lower starting wages [9].

So a referral-heavy labour market rewards people who already have professional networks, and those networks are not handed out at random. That is a real unfairness, and no amount of good advice makes it disappear.

What advice can do is shift who has those networks. If you are starting with few professional ties, the evidence points in a hopeful direction: the most useful ties are not intimate ones but moderately weak, professional ones [7; 9], and those can be built. Communities of practice (meetups, open-source projects, professional groups, alumni networks, online forums in your field) are places where strangers watch a little of your work and become the middle ring. A year of answering questions well in one of them builds exactly the kind of tie that can later say “I’ve seen what she can do” our view.

And if you are on the inside, Bea’s question is also addressed to you. When you refer, refer for evidence, not for familiarity, and look for good people outside your own circle. The same question comes back in Chapter 19, where the people vouching for you are sponsors and the prize is a promotion.

A few more questions tend to come up at this point, usually from the people who most need the answers.

Ask Anyway

QIsn’t asking for a referral just using people?

ANot if what you ask them to say is true and you make it easy to decline. Employers run referral schemes because referrals help them hire people who fit and stay [4]. You are giving your contact a chance to help their own team. The line is crossed when you ask someone to vouch for work they have not seen, or pressure them when they hesitate.

QI’m an introvert. Do I have to go to networking events?

ANo. The evidence is about ties, not events, and the most useful ties are people who have seen your work [7; 4]. Writing, code, answering questions in a forum and doing good work alongside people all build them, quietly.

QWhat if someone refers me and I don’t get the job?

AThat is normal. A referral gets you looked at; it does not get you hired. Thank them either way, tell them how it went, and you have kept a tie warm for next time.

QShould I connect with the hiring manager before I apply?

AIf you have a genuine reason (a mutual contact, a shared community, something they wrote that you can respond to), a short, specific message is fine. A cold “I’ve applied, please look at my application” mostly adds to their inbox craft.

Recruiters and agencies#

Sometimes the person who reaches out is not someone you know but someone paid to find you. Recruiters come in two kinds, and it helps to know which is which.

In-house recruiters work for the employer. When one of them contacts you, you have been “sourced”: someone looked at your profile and decided you were worth a message. In one vendor’s 2024 data, sourced candidates were hired at five times the rate of people who applied cold [2], as “Not every route through the funnel is the same” in Chapter 3 showed. Most of that is selection (the recruiter picked you because you looked like a fit), but it means a findable, accurate profile is a channel in its own right. “Profiles, portfolios and code” in Chapter 5 covers what makes a profile worth finding.

Agency recruiters work for a separate firm that the employer pays when it hires someone they introduced. The fee is usually a percentage of the new hire’s first-year pay: “contingent” agencies are paid only if a hire happens, while “retained” search firms are paid in instalments and usually cost more in total. One fee guide written for agencies gives examples in the range of 18% to 25% [17 weak]. That is illustrative rather than survey data, but it tells you whose money is on the table.

That incentive shapes the relationship. An agency recruiter wants you placed, and quickly, which is often what you want too. It is not always aligned with your interests on which job is right for you, or on how hard to push on salary when a quick yes earns the fee our view. Treat a good agency recruiter as a useful, knowledgeable ally with a commission, which is exactly what they are.

Field NoteWho pays the recruiter

UKUS

In Great Britain the rule is explicit: “Recruitment agencies cannot charge you a fee for finding or trying to find you work.” They can charge for optional extras such as CV writing or training, and entertainment and modelling agencies have separate rules [18]. In the US, agency fees are normally paid by the employer, typically as a percentage of first-year pay [17]. We did not verify the rules for India or the rest of the EU. Wherever you are, treat a request for an upfront “placement fee” as a reason to stop and check our view.

Dev’s experience is an anecdote, but it is exactly the pattern the research predicts: a moderately weak tie, a specific ask, a quick yes from someone happier to help than Dev expected. Notice what made it work: not Dev’s charm, but the fact that the two sentences she gave Ana were true. In the next chapter, Chapter 7, she has to live up to them in a room with Tomás.

First, though, return to the number you wrote down at the start of the chapter.

Your Move 6.3Guess, then check

At the start of this chapter you guessed how many of ten people would say yes to a short, specific request for help.

This week, send three asks from your People list: one for information, one for an introduction and, if there is someone who genuinely knows your work, one for a referral. Write down your prediction for each before you send it. Record what happens.

Reveal one way to play it

If you are like the people in Flynn and Lake’s experiments, your guess was too low: they underestimated how likely others were to agree to a direct request by as much as half [12]. Your setting differs from theirs, so your numbers will too: a message is easier to leave unanswered than a request made to someone’s face, and some people will simply not see yours. Silence is not a no; one polite follow-up after a week is normal.

The point of the exercise is calibration. Most people who avoid asking are not avoiding rejection; they are avoiding a rejection that is much less likely than they think. The only way to update that belief is to collect your own data. Dev’s first datapoint took 47 minutes.

Dev’s People tab is not finished, and it never will be. That is the point. The snapshot game is about to get much more intense for her, but the habit she just started will matter long after it ends. In Chapter 19, people who have seen her work vouching for her turns out to be how promotions work too; and in “Epilogue: The Loop” it is Dev’s turn to be someone’s Ana.

Rewind
  • Referred candidates are a sliver of applicants and a large share of hires: 6.1% of applicants and 29.1% of hires in one large firm, with the same direction in vendor data.
  • Referrals work mainly because they carry information about fit from someone who has seen your work; referred hires fit better and quit less. A referral is a strong signal because the referrer’s reputation is on the line.
  • Moderately weak ties (former colleagues, classmates, people who know you a little) help most; strong ties still matter, especially outside digital industries.
  • The “80% of jobs are never advertised” figure has no traceable source; what is true is that half or more of workers find jobs through contacts. Apply and ask; they are not alternatives.
  • People underestimate how willing others are to help. A good ask has four parts: the bridge, the specific ask, make it easy, and a clean exit.
  • Reciprocity fades, so give small and often. Networking is mostly the long game: being someone people can honestly vouch for.
  • Referral hiring favours people who already have networks. Build professional ties in communities of practice, and when you refer, refer for evidence.
  • In-house recruiters work for the employer; agency recruiters are paid by the employer, usually a share of first-year pay. In Great Britain they may not charge you for finding work.

    Caveats: Vendor data with a product to sell (sourcing/CRM); customer base not representative; definitions are Gem's.

  3. CareerPlug (2025). 2025 Recruiting Metrics Report. CareerPlug (applicant tracking vendor for small businesses). www.careerplug.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Recruiting-Metrics-Report.pdf ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence industry-report commercial source opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: 2024 data from 60,000+ small businesses and 10M+ applications - 180 applicants per hire (up from 93); 3% of applicants invited to interview; 27% of interviews become hires; click-to-apply 4.7%; referrals 2% of applicants but 11.3% of hires; job boards 61.3% of applicants, 43.3% of hires.

    Caveats: Vendor data; SMB and hourly skew - not representative of professional or tech hiring.

  4. Burks, S. V., Cowgill, B., Hoffman, M., & Housman, M. (2015). The value of hiring through employee referrals. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 130(2), 805-839. doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv010 (copy read) ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Nine large firms (call centres, trucking, high-tech); referred workers 10-30% less likely to quit, better on rare high-impact metrics (patents, fewer truck accidents), similar on ordinary metrics; referred applicants substantially more likely to be hired; workers refer people like themselves.

    Caveats: Industries are specific; effects on the firm, not a guarantee to the candidate. Working-paper wording may differ from published version.

  5. Pallais, A., & Sands, E. G. (2016). Why the referential treatment? Evidence from field experiments on referrals. Journal of Political Economy (2016); NBER working paper w21357, doi 10.3386/w21357. www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21357/w21357.pdf ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Three field experiments in an online labour market (oDesk) - referrals carry positive information about performance and persistence beyond observables; referred workers do especially well working directly with their referrer; no evidence of extra effort to protect the referrer.

    Caveats: Online micro-task context; generalisation to salaried jobs is an inference.

  6. Granovetter, M. S. (1973). The strength of weak ties. American Journal of Sociology, 78(6), 1360-1380. doi.org/10.1086/225469 (copy read) ↩ ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Theory that weak ties bridge to new information; in a sample of professional, technical and managerial job changers in a Boston suburb who found jobs through contacts, 16.7% saw the contact often, 55.6% occasionally, 27.8% rarely (N=54).

    Caveats: Foundational theory but tiny, local, 1970s sample; frequency of contact is only one dimension of tie strength. Causal support came only in rajkumar2022.

  7. Rajkumar, K., Saint-Jacques, G., Bojinov, I., Brynjolfsson, E., & Aral, S. (2022). A causal test of the strength of weak ties. Science, 377(6612), 1304-1310. doi.org/10.1126/science.abl4476 (copy read) ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Randomised LinkedIn "People You May Know" experiments (2015, 2019) on 20M+ people; inverted-U between tie strength and job transmission; moderately weak ties (by mutual connections) most useful; weak ties matter more in digital industries, strong ties in less digital ones.

    Caveats: Job transmission = later working at the connection's company; per-tie probabilities are tiny (fractions of a percent). LinkedIn users only; users were not told they were in the experiments (see acs2022-linkedin).

    Conflicts: Two authors were LinkedIn employees.

  8. Gee, L. K., Jones, J., & Burke, M. (2017). Social networks and labor markets: How strong ties relate to job finding on Facebook's social network. Journal of Labor Economics, 35(2), 485-518. doi.org/10.1086/686225 (copy read) ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: 6 million Facebook users; most people are helped through one of their numerous weak ties, but a single stronger tie is significantly more valuable at the margin.

    Caveats: Correlational; Facebook-measured ties only; data came from Facebook (author affiliations not verified here).

  9. Lester, B., Rivers, D. A., & Topa, G. (2021). The heterogeneous impact of referrals on labor market outcomes. Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Reports, no. 987. www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr987.pdf ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence working-paper opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Survey literature - most surveys of job seekers find 50-60% report using a referral to find employment; firm surveys range from just under 40% to over 50% of hires via referral. In the NY Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations (2013-2018), business-contact referrals associate with higher starting wages and are used more for high-skill jobs; family/friend referrals with lower starting wages, used more for low-skill jobs.

    Caveats: Working paper; "using a referral" is broad (any contact help).

  10. n/a (2016). Pellizzari (2004) as cited in brown2016. cited in brown2016 footnote 2. resources.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr568.pdf ↩

    Moderate evidence note opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Between 25% and 40% of respondents in most European countries heard about their current job through informal contacts (European Community Household Panel).

    Caveats: Secondary citation; Pellizzari's original was not opened.

  11. Recruiter, C., & republished by California State University Fresno Career Development Center (2024). Is it true that 80% of job openings are not advertised?.     Shows: The 80%-unadvertised claim is hard to trace to any single source and "likely originated from anecdotal evidence and informal surveys rather than rigorous, peer-reviewed research."

    Caveats: Originally from College Recruiter (a job board); commentary only.

  12. Flynn, F. J., & Lake, V. K. B. (2008). If you need help, just ask: Underestimating compliance with direct requests for help. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 95(1), 128–143. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128 ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence experiments + field studies opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People underestimate by as much as 50% how likely others are to agree to a direct request for help, because they overlook how awkward it is to say no.

    Caveats: Replicated conceptually by later work (e.g., givi2024 on the other side of the request).

  13. Boothby, E. J., Cooney, G., Sandstrom, G. M., & Clark, M. S. (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think?. Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714 ↩

    Strong evidence experiments + longitudinal opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: After conversations, people underestimate how much their partners liked them ("liking gap"), and the gap persisted for months among new dorm-mates.

    Caveats: Replicated across samples and in mastroianni2021.

  14. Regan, D. T. (1971). Effects of a favor and liking on compliance. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 7(6), 627–639. doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031(71)90025-4 ↩

    Moderate evidence lab experiment opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Participants given an unsolicited small favour (a soft drink) by a confederate later bought more raffle tickets from him; the favour's effect did not depend on how much they liked him.

    Caveats: Abstract not machine-readable; details checked through secondary accounts surfaced by web search, which disagree on exact numbers ("about twice as many tickets"). Cite the direction, not a multiplier. Old single study, but the reciprocity effect it shows is well replicated in the wider literature.

  15. Burger, J. M., Horita, M., Kinoshita, L., Roberts, K., & Vera, C. (1997). Effects on Time on the Norm of Reciprocity. Basic and Applied Social Psychology, 19(1), 91–100. doi.org/10.1207/s15324834basp1901_7 ↩

    Moderate evidence field experiment + scenario study opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The pull to return a favour fades with time - people reciprocated a small favour after 5 minutes but showed no reliable reciprocity after a week.

    Caveats: Small studies; useful as a nuance (reciprocity is not an open-ended debt).

  16. Fernandez, R. M., & Rubineau, B. (2019). Network recruitment and the glass ceiling: Evidence from two firms. RSF: The Russell Sage Foundation Journal of the Social Sciences, 5(3), 88-102. doi.org/10.7758/rsf.2019.5.3.05 (copy read) ↩

    Moderate evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: With gender homophily, referral hiring can disadvantage women where an old-boys network exists, more so at higher levels; but overall network recruitment was desegregating and promoted women's representation at all levels.

    Caveats: Two firms; abstract-level reading.

  17. Echelon, T. (2026). Recruitment agency fee structure types. Top Echelon blog (software and services vendor to recruitment agencies). topechelon.com/blog/recruitment-agency-fee-structures/ ↩ ↩

    Caveats: Vendor to agencies; examples, not survey data.
