INT. MEERA'S HOME OFFICE / DEV'S FLAT — VIDEO CALL, THURSDAY, 8 PM

Meera’s camera shows a bookshelf, a sleeping cat and a mug that says ASK ME ABOUT MY FUNNEL. Dev asks about the funnel.

Meera I was hoping you would. Look. This is one of my open roles. Backend engineer, mid-level, remote within the country.

She shares her screen. It is a dashboard from the company’s hiring software, with names blurred out. At the top: 412 applicants. Below that: 38 reviewed by recruiter. 14 phone screens. 5 final interviews. 1 offer.

Dev Four hundred and twelve. For one job.
Meera In three weeks. And I will hire one of them. Which means four hundred and eleven people will get an email very like the one you got last month.
Dev The “careful consideration” email.
Meera It was careful. It just wasn’t about them. Most of those four hundred never reached a human who had time to think about them properly. That’s not a judgement. That’s arithmetic.

In Chapter 2, Dev decided to search properly, from her job. She has 46 rejections in a spreadsheet already, and until now she has read each one as a verdict on her. This chapter is about reading them as data instead.

That shift matters for two reasons. The first is your morale: a job search is long, and if every no lands as a judgement on your worth, you will stop before the search is done. The second is your strategy: you cannot play a game well if you do not know how it is scored. So we are going to sit on Meera’s side of the table for a chapter and watch hiring as the employer experiences it: who wants what, how the funnel narrows, what the software does and does not do, how well the people choosing can actually choose, and where the process is unfair.

Pause

Make a guess before you read on. Out of every 1,000 people who apply for a job by sending an application through a website, how many do you think get hired? Write a number down. The answer is in the next few pages.

A job starts as a requisition#

From the outside, a job advert looks like an invitation. From the inside, it is the visible end of a long internal process, and it helps to know how that process goes.

Most organisations of any size do not let managers hire whenever they like. A manager makes a case that they need someone, and the organisation approves a requisition: permission to hire one person, into a particular role, at a particular level, within a particular budget, usually by a target date. The job description is often adapted from an older one, sometimes by someone who has never done the job, and it frequently lists more requirements than any one hire will meet. Then the requisition opens, the advert goes up, and the clock starts.

This is how hiring is commonly organised rather than a research finding craft, and it varies with the size of the company. In a small firm the founder may be the requisition, the recruiter and the panel. But three things follow from it almost everywhere, and they explain a lot of candidate misery.

First, the employer is under time pressure, but not the kind you hope for. They want the role filled, which is good for you, but they also have day jobs, which is why replies are slow. Second, the budget is set before you apply. You can negotiate within it and sometimes stretch it, but the range was decided by people you will never meet (Chapter 9). Third, requisitions change. Roles are frozen, filled internally, merged or rewritten mid-process, and the candidates are often the last to hear. Some of your silences will be about you. Many will be about a spreadsheet you cannot see.

Who’s in the room#

Several people touch a hire, and they want different things. Knowing who wants what tells you whom to ask which question, and why the same process can feel friendly at one stage and bureaucratic at the next.

The hiring manager owns the need and, in most organisations, the decision. They care most about whether you can do the work and how soon. The recruiter, either in-house or from an agency, runs the process: finding candidates, first screens, scheduling, keeping everyone moving, and the logistics of the offer. A recruiting consultant quoted in a professional-association write-up put the division bluntly: hiring decisions “are not ours. We’re the project manager” [1 weak]. That is a practitioner’s view rather than research, but it matches how most hiring is organised. The interview panel gathers evidence, ideally against a scoring sheet (Chapter 7). Somewhere behind them, someone holds the budget and signs off the offer.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

Ask the right person the right question. Questions about the process, the timeline and the salary range usually go to the recruiter, who often knows the range and wants the process to succeed. Questions about the work, the team and how success is measured go to the hiring manager. Questions about what the job is really like go to the people who would be your peers. A recruiter is not your opponent: their incentive is to fill the role, which, for most of the process, is the same as yours.

And then there is you: the only person in the process who does not know how it works. This chapter is an attempt to fix that.

The funnel, with real numbers#

Every hiring process is a funnel: many people enter at the top, fewer survive each stage, and one comes out of the bottom. The useful questions are how steep it is, and whether some routes through it are steeper than others.

There is no public census of hiring funnels, so the best numbers come from companies that sell hiring software and publish their customers’ data. That makes them a useful but imperfect source, and we say so each time. The largest set we could check comes from Gem, a recruiting-software vendor, describing its customers’ hiring in 2024. Of people who applied through the front door (“inbound” applicants), 6% got as far as a first screen; 20% of those reached the final round of interviews; 35% of those got an offer; overall, 0.4% of inbound applicants were hired [2].

That is the answer to the pause question. Of 1,000 people who apply through a website, roughly four are hired. If you guessed much higher, you have probably been taking the other 996 personally.

Other data agree on the shape, if not the decimals. CareerPlug, which sells hiring software to small businesses, reports that its customers received 180 applicants per hire in 2024, up from 93 the year before; 3% of applicants were invited to interview, and 27% of interviews ended in a hire [3]. Those are small businesses in sectors such as restaurants, retail, cleaning and personal care, not professional or tech employers, so the numbers will differ from Dev’s world. But the pattern is the same: the first cut is brutal, and the later stages are much kinder.

Not every route through the funnel is the same#

The front door is the steepest way in. In Gem’s data, candidates whom a recruiter went looking for (“sourced”) were hired at 2.0%, five times the inbound rate [2]. Referred candidates were 1.64% of applications but 17.09% of hires [2]. The best peer-reviewed evidence comes from one large US company that tracked its applicants by source: referred candidates were 6.1% of applicants but 21.4% of those interviewed, 27.3% of those offered a job and 29.1% of those hired, while job-board applicants were 60.1% of applicants and only 23.5% of hires [4].

Some of that gap is selection rather than magic: a recruiter who sources you has already decided you look promising, and an employee who refers you has already vouched for you. That is exactly the point. A referral is evidence that arrives before you do, from someone the employer already trusts. It is the long game reaching into the snapshot game, the loop from Chapter 1, and Chapter 6 is about using it honestly.

The funnel has got steeper#

If your search feels harder than it did a few years ago, the vendor data suggest you are not imagining it. In Gem’s customer data, the share of all applicants who were hired fell from 1.6% in 2021 to 0.5% in 2024; the number of interviews per hire rose from 14 to 20; and the average time to hire rose from 33 to 41 days [2]. Offer acceptance rose slightly, from 81% to 84% [2], which fits a market where candidates have fewer options our view. One vendor’s customers are not the whole economy, but it is a reasonable warning: budget for a longer search than the last one.

Myth CheckEvery job gets 250 applicationsBusted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Weak evidence

The earliest source we found is a 2013 article by a recruiting consultant, which hedged the number in the original (“Although it varies with the company and the job, on average 250 resumes are received”) and gave no method or source for it [5]. Current vendor data show applicant numbers varying widely by role and market: 180 applicants per hire among small businesses in 2024, nearly double the year before [3]. The honest version is “it depends on the role and the market”. No single number survives.

What the software actually does#

Between you and the hiring manager sits an applicant tracking system (ATS): software that collects applications, stores them, lets recruiters search and sort them, and moves candidates from stage to stage. It has a fearsome reputation, most of which it has not earned.

What it does do is filter, because people tell it to. In a Harvard Business School and Accenture survey of 2,275 executives in the US, UK and Germany, about two-thirds of employers (63%) used a recruiting-management system, and the report says more than 90% of employers used theirs to initially filter or rank candidates [6]. Those filters are set by people: required degrees, exact credential names, location, work authorisation, and sometimes gaps in employment. The same survey found that 88% of employers agreed that qualified high-skills candidates were being screened out because they did not match the exact criteria in the job description (94% for middle-skills roles), and 48% filtered out middle-skills candidates with a gap of more than six months [6]. Accenture sells services in this space, and these are executives describing their own processes, but the admission is striking: the employers themselves think their filters throw away good people.

What the software mostly does not do is read your résumé and decide you are unworthy. In a small set of interviews with 25 US recruiters, run by a résumé-tool company, 23 said their systems did not auto-reject anyone for formatting; two used match-score thresholds; all used knockout questions on things like work authorisation and location [7 weak]. That is a tiny, commercial sample, useful only because it agrees with how these systems are built.

Myth CheckATS software rejects 75% of résumés before a human sees themBusted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Weak evidence

Nobody has ever published the study behind this number. Secondary sources consistently trace it to a 2012 sales claim by Preptel, a résumé-optimisation company that closed in 2013, and no sample or method was ever released [8]. An HR consultant who went looking for the research found none, as recounted by an ATS vendor that obviously has an interest in the answer [9]. What is real: employers configure knockout filters, on degrees, exact credentials and gaps, and admit that these screen out qualified people [6]. The robot is not rejecting you. The rules someone typed into it might be.

That distinction tells you what to do. The real risks are knockout questions you answer carelessly, and a human doing a fast skim. So answer screening questions accurately and completely, use the job ad’s own words for skills you genuinely have, and keep your formatting simple enough for both software and tired eyes craft. Chapter 5 turns that into a résumé.

Trapdoor

Gaming the bot. Pasting the whole job description in white text, stuffing in keywords you cannot back up, or claiming a degree “in progress” that is not: these tricks are aimed at a robot that is mostly not there, and they are seen by the humans who are. Even where a keyword search finds you, the next reader is a person checking whether the words are true. The cost is the interview you get and then lose, and your name in a recruiter’s memory. Mirror the ad’s language for things you have really done; leave the rest out.

How well can they choose?#

Suppose the funnel works as designed and you reach the final round. How good is the employer at picking the right person from the five or so who are left?

Flashback“Why the snapshot game is so noisy” in Chapter 1

Chapter 1 showed what a correlation of .42 looks like: a real trend inside a lot of scatter. That number was the best single method. Here is the rest of the table.

A major recent re-analysis of how well selection methods predict job performance, by Paul Sackett and colleagues, produced the estimates below [10]. A correlation of 1 would be perfect prediction; 0 would be none.

MethodHow well it predicts performance
Structured interviews (same questions, same scoring).42
Job-knowledge tests.40
Work-sample tests.33
Cognitive-ability tests.31
Unstructured interviews (a conversation).19
Years of job experience.07

These figures are lower than the ones you may have seen quoted. A famous 1998 summary put cognitive-ability tests and structured interviews both at .51 and unstructured interviews at .38 [11]; the 2022 re-analysis argued that earlier statistical corrections had been too generous and cut most estimates by .10 to .20 [10]. Researchers are still arguing about the exact sizes, especially for cognitive ability, which a newer study of 21st-century data put at .22 [12] and other researchers argue is being underestimated [13]. But the old and new estimates agree on the part of the ranking that matters most here: structured beats unstructured, and job-specific evidence beats general impressions [11; 10].

Two things follow for you. First, even the best-run process makes plenty of mistakes, in both directions, so a rejection from a good company is still weak evidence about you. Second, look at the bottom row. Years of experience barely predict performance at all, at .07 [10]. Requirements like “five years of experience” are a convenient filter for a busy employer, not a law of nature. If you can show the work in other ways, a shortfall in years is a gap to explain, not a wall (Chapter 4).

The funnel is not neutral#

INT. A NOODLE BAR NEAR CORVID — FRIDAY, 1 PM

Dev is explaining the funnel to Bea, with chopsticks for stages.

Dev So it’s arithmetic. Four in a thousand. Most noes aren’t about you.
Bea Most. Not all. Did your director friend mention who the four are, on average?
Dev She said the best ones she could see.
Bea Right. And who gets seen is where it stops being arithmetic. There are studies where they send out identical CVs with different names on them. Guess what happens.

Dev puts the chopsticks down.

Bea is right, and the evidence is some of the strongest in this book.

The method is called an audit or correspondence study: researchers send employers pairs of fictitious applications that are identical except for one signal, such as a name that suggests a particular race or religion, and count who gets called back. In a famous US experiment, résumés with white-sounding names received 50% more callbacks than identical résumés with African-American-sounding names [14]. A meta-analysis of 28 US field experiments, covering 55,842 applications, found that since 1989 white applicants have received on average 36% more callbacks than equally qualified African Americans and 24% more than Latinos, with no change for African Americans over 25 years [15]. A study of more than 83,000 fictitious applications to 108 of the largest US employers found that distinctively Black names reduced the chance of contact by 2.1 percentage points, and that the gap varied a great deal from one firm to another [16].

It is not an American peculiarity. In a correspondence study of graduate applicants to private-sector employers in urban India, as summarised by a research centre, the odds of an interview invitation for an applicant with a Dalit name were about two-thirds, and for an applicant with a Muslim name about one-third, of those for an equally qualified high-caste Hindu applicant [17]. We did not verify a comparable recent study for the UK or the EU, so we make no claim about them here.

Author’s ViewWhat this means for you

Our opinion: argued, not proven

We want to say three things plainly, because books like this one often say none of them.

It is real, and it is not your fault. If you belong to a group that these studies show being passed over, some of your silences are discrimination, and no amount of résumé polish fully removes it. It is not uniform. Discrimination is concentrated in some employers more than others [16], which means where you apply, and how you get in, matters. The route in changes what the employer sees first. A referral or a direct conversation puts a person who vouches for you in front of your name, rather than leaving the name to do the work alone. That does not make the system fair, and referrals carry their own fairness problems (Chapter 6). But it is the part you can choose.

This is our argument, not a measured result: we know of no study that tests whether referrals reduce the specific callback gaps above.

Bias does not stop at the first screen; it can show up in interviews, offers and later in reviews, and the book returns to it in Chapter 5, Chapter 9 and Chapter 18. For now, hold two things at once: most rejections are arithmetic, and some are not, and neither fact is a reason to stop.

Rejection is mostly arithmetic#

Back to Dev’s 46 noes, and the question she really wants answered: is something wrong with her?

Here is a way to think about it. Suppose each application you send has some chance of ending in an offer, and, to keep the maths simple, suppose each application is independent of the others. Then the chance of a long run of noes is easy to work out, and it is surprisingly high.

If Dev’s chance per application were 1 in 100, which is not unreasonable for cold applications through the front door, given that only 0.4% of inbound applicants are hired [2], then the chance of 46 noes in a row would be about 63%. Forty-six rejections would be the likely outcome, not a sign that something is wrong. If her chance were 1 in 20, 46 noes would happen only about 9% of the time, and it would be a hint that something in her approach needed fixing.

This is the reframing that matters, and it has two halves. The first is emotional: a run of noes is often just what a low-probability game looks like. The second is strategic: the number to work on is the chance per application, not just the number of applications. You can raise it by applying where you are a strong fit (Chapter 4), with evidence that survives a fast skim (Chapter 5), through routes that carry trust (Chapter 6).

Volume still matters. A meta-analysis of the job-search research found that how much people search is reliably related to whether they find work [18]. But volume multiplies whatever your per-application chance is, and a better chance is usually cheaper to buy than a hundred more applications.

Your Move 3.1Funnel maths

Use the vendor numbers from this chapter: of inbound applicants, 6% get a first screen, 20% of those reach the final round, and 35% of those get an offer [2].

  1. Roughly how many inbound applications does it take, on average, to get one first screen? One final round? One offer?
  2. Suppose a referral gets you straight to a first screen, and from there you convert like everyone else. How many referrals would you expect to need for one offer?
  3. Why are your own odds probably better than these averages, and how could you check?
Reveal one way to play it
  1. About 17 applications per first screen (1 ÷ 0.06), about 83 per final round (1 ÷ 0.012) and about 240 per offer (1 ÷ 0.0042). That last number is sobering, and it is an average across everyone who applied, including many people who were never a plausible fit.
  2. From a first screen, the chance of an offer is 20% × 35% = 7%, so about 14 referrals per offer, if referrals only skipped the first cut. In practice referred candidates also do better at later stages (Figure 3.2), so it is likely fewer. Compare 14 conversations with 240 cold applications.
  3. The averages include people applying to jobs they are not qualified for, in other locations, without the right to work there. If you apply only where you meet the core of the role, your chance is higher. Check by tracking your own funnel: applications, screens, final rounds, offers. After 20 or 30 applications you will know your own conversion rates, and which stage is leaking. That is exactly what Dev’s Funnel maths tab is for.

Silence, ghosting and morale#

The cruellest part of the funnel is not rejection. It is silence: the application that vanishes, the recruiter who said “next week” a month ago, the final round followed by nothing. Some of it is rudeness. A lot of it is the requisition problem from the start of this chapter: the role was frozen, filled internally or rewritten, and nobody remembered to tell the candidates.

You cannot fix that. You can stop it from running your search. The research on structured job-search programmes is useful here, because it is about what keeps people going. Programmes that worked combined building skills with keeping motivation up, and the components that mattered included setting goals, boosting confidence and enlisting social support [19]. Those findings come from evaluated programmes, not from people searching alone, so treat them as ingredients rather than a promise.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

Keeping your morale out of the funnel.

  • Set goals for inputs, not outcomes. “Five tailored applications and two conversations this week” is in your control. “An offer by March” is not.
  • Check messages in batches, once or twice a day, rather than refreshing your inbox between meetings.
  • Track the funnel, not the feelings. Count applications, screens, finals and offers. A leak at one stage tells you what to fix; a pile of noes tells you nothing.
  • Follow up once. A short, polite note after a promised deadline has passed is normal. After that, assume no and move on, and be pleasantly surprised if they come back.
  • Search with someone. Social support is one of the ingredients that worked [19]. Dev has Bea. Find your Bea.

Reading a rejection as data rather than a verdict is a skill, and like any skill it improves with practice.

Your Move 3.2Rejection reframing

Here are four things that might happen in a search. For each, write one sentence about what it does tell you and one about what it does not.

  1. You sent 30 applications through job boards and heard nothing from 26 of them.
  2. You reached the final round twice and lost both times.
  3. A recruiter called, was enthusiastic, then went silent.
  4. You were rejected within an hour of applying.
Reveal one way to play it
  1. Tells you: your front-door applications are not getting past the first skim, so look at targeting and the top third of your résumé (Chapter 5). Does not tell you: that you are unemployable. At a 6% screen rate, silence from most of 30 inbound applications is normal [2].
  2. Tells you: your paper and your early screens are working, which is the hard part. Ask for feedback; look at how you tell your stories (Chapter 7). Does not tell you: that you were the wrong choice. Final rounds are close calls between strong people, decided by noisy instruments (“How well can they choose?”).
  3. Tells you: probably that the requisition changed, or the recruiter is overloaded. Does not tell you: anything about you. Send one follow-up, then move on.
  4. Tells you: you hit a knockout filter, such as location, work authorisation, a required credential or a screening question. Re-read the application questions. Does not tell you: that a person read your résumé and disliked it. Nobody reads that fast.

A few questions come up in almost every search.

Ask Anyway

QShould I apply in the first day or two after a job is posted?

AEarlier is probably better, but the evidence is thin. One job-search startup’s data on 6,600 hires found that almost half had applied in the first week a job was posted [20 weak], and research on job boards finds that fewer people apply after a posting’s first few days [21]. Neither shows that timing itself causes hires. Apply within a week or so when you can, but never send a rushed, generic application just to be early.

QIf 88% of employers admit their filters throw out good people, why do they keep using them?

ABecause they are cheap and fast, and a funnel of hundreds needs a fast first cut. Our reading is that the cost of the good people screened out is invisible to the employer, while the cost of reading 400 applications is not our view. That is a reason to use routes that bypass the cut where you can, not a reason to give up on the front door.

QShould I write to the hiring manager directly?

AA short, specific note to the hiring manager, alongside a proper application, is common practice and rarely hurts craft. Keep it to three or four sentences about why you fit this role, with one piece of evidence. Chapter 6 covers the anatomy of a good ask.

Reading a job ad from the inside#

Once you know how a requisition gets written, a job ad reads differently. It is part specification, part wish list and part advertising, written by busy people who are trying to attract the right candidates while filtering out the wrong ones.

Your Move 3.3Decode a job ad

Here is an ad for a role Dev might apply to. Sort each line into must-have (likely a knockout filter or a core need), nice-to-have (a wish), or signal (tells you about the team or the process).

Backend Engineer. You’ll build and run the services behind our shipment-tracking platform. Requirements: 3+ years writing production code in Python or Go; experience with relational databases (PostgreSQL preferred); familiarity with message queues; a degree in computer science or equivalent experience; right to work in this country. Nice to have: Kubernetes; logistics domain knowledge. How we hire: a 30-minute call with our recruiter, a take-home exercise (max 3 hours), then a half-day with the team. We’re a team of 14 who value clear writing and boring, reliable software.

Reveal one way to play it
  • Must-have: right to work (almost certainly a knockout question); production code in Python or Go (the core of the job); relational databases. The “3+ years” is probably softer than it looks, given how weakly years predict performance [10], if you can show equivalent work.
  • Nice-to-have: “PostgreSQL preferred”, message queues (“familiarity” is a soft word), Kubernetes, logistics knowledge. The degree line says “or equivalent experience”, which is the employer telling you it is negotiable.
  • Signals: “build and run” means they will expect you to support what you ship. The hiring process is described in advance and the take-home is capped at three hours, which suggests a structured, considerate process (Chapter 8). “Clear writing and boring, reliable software” tells you what to emphasise: Dev’s regression-suite story and her test reports are exactly on target.

The pattern: match the must-haves exactly and honestly, show evidence for as many soft requirements as you can, and use the signals to choose which stories to tell. This is the start of Chapter 4.

Dev has learned what the snapshot game looks like from the other side: a narrow, noisy, slightly unfair funnel, run by busy people, in which most rejections are arithmetic. She cannot change the funnel. But she can change her chance at each stage, starting with the evidence she brings to it, which is the subject of Chapter 4. Later in her career she will see the same machinery from the inside again: a promotion round is a funnel too, with a budget, a panel and a calibration meeting, as Chapter 19 shows.

Rewind
  • A job starts as a requisition: one approved role, a budget and a deadline. Many silences come from requisitions changing, not from you.
  • The hiring manager decides, the recruiter runs the process, the panel gathers evidence and someone holds the budget. Ask each the questions they can answer.
  • The front door is steep: in one vendor’s data, about 0.4% of inbound applicants were hired. Referred and sourced candidates convert far better, because someone has vouched for them already.
  • Software filters because people configure it to. There is no study behind “ATS rejects 75%”; the real risks are knockout questions and a fast human skim.
  • Even the best selection method predicts with a correlation of about .42; years of experience predict almost nothing (.07).
  • The funnel is not neutral. Audit studies show large callback gaps by race in the US, unchanged for decades, and by caste and religion in India. That is not your fault, and how you get in still matters.
  • Rejection is mostly arithmetic. With a low chance per application, long runs of noes are normal. Raise the chance per application, track your own funnel, and set goals for inputs, not outcomes.

    Strong evidence meta-analysis of 47 experimental or quasi-experimental evaluations opened 2026-10-25
