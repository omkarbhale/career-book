EXT. A GUESTHOUSE VERANDA, SOMEWHERE WITH HILLS — VIDEO CALL, 7 PM HER TIME

Meera is three months into a six-month sabbatical, and she looks it, in the good way. The connection is slow. Behind her there are hills, a washing line and a dog that is not hers. In her hand is a new mug: REST IS PRODUCTIVE.

Dev You look ten years younger.
Meera I look like someone who has slept. It’s not the same thing, but I’ll take it.
Dev Do you miss it?
Meera The work? Some of it. The people, yes. My calendar? I had a dream last week that I was double-booked, and I woke up laughing.
Dev Can I ask you something I’ve been wanting to ask for years? If you started again, what would you do differently?

Meera takes long enough to answer that Dev wonders whether the call has frozen.

Meera Three things. I’d have stopped chasing the next box on the chart sooner, and asked what I actually wanted my days to look like. I’d have changed jobs twice when I didn’t, because I was comfortable, and once when I did, because I was angry. And I’d have taken this, the rest, at forty instead of fifty-four. I thought it was a reward for finishing. It’s maintenance.
Dev That’s very wise.
Meera It’s thirty years of hindsight, which is the cheapest kind of wisdom there is. Now tell me about this recruiter.

Meera is talking about the whole of a working life, and the whole is a different object from any of its parts. So far this book has looked at games you play in months: a job search, a first year, a review cycle, a promotion case. This chapter steps back to the scale of decades, where the most useful questions change. Not “how do I get this job?” but “what do I want the next ten years to have been made of?”

The evidence thins out at this scale, and we will say so as we go. Careers are long, people are different, and nobody has run a randomised trial on a working life. What we can do is put the best findings we have next to each other, and use them to make the next decision better than the last.

Pause

Think of someone ten or twenty years further into their working life than you whose career you admire. Draw it as a line. Is it a ladder, straight up? Or does it have flat stretches, turns and a few steps back? Keep the drawing; the chapter comes back to it.

A career is a sequence of loops#

The picture most people carry of a career is a ladder: start at the bottom, climb, stop at the top. It is a tidy picture and a misleading one.

Start with how often people move. In the US, people born between 1957 and 1964 held an average of 12.9 jobs between the ages of 18 and 58 [1]. Moves between whole occupations and industries were common too, and became more common between 1968 and 1997 [2]; “Career capital” in Chapter 20 drew out why that matters: the research those authors build on finds that much of what you learn is specific to a kind of work, so a bigger turn costs more of it. A career, in the data, looks much less like one ladder than like a series of jobs joined by turns.

The loop from Chapter 1 gives you a better picture. Each job is a full turn of it: a snapshot game to get in, a long game once you are there, a career game that turns the work into skills and reputation, and then, eventually, another snapshot, played with what the last turn produced. A career is that loop, repeated. The loops are rarely the same size, and they rarely point in exactly the same direction.

That picture has two practical consequences. The first is that the snapshot game never goes away, so the skills from Part I keep their value for the whole of your working life. The second is that the most important output of each loop is not the title you leave with but the material you carry into the next one: what you can do, what you can show, and who will vouch for you.

Plateaus are normal#

Every ladder-shaped career eventually stops going up. This is not a personal failing; it is arithmetic.

Organisations are pyramids. In the economists’ model of promotion as a tournament, the prize at each level goes to whoever ranks first among the people competing for it, and the number of prizes shrinks as you climb [3]. So most people, at some point, stop being promoted. The idea of a “career plateau” was introduced in the 1970s by researchers who described managerial careers as “early rapid upward mobility followed by inevitable leveling-off” [4].

What matters is how a plateau feels and what you do with it. A review of forty years of plateau research, covering 72 empirical sources, distinguished two kinds [5]:

  • a hierarchical plateau, when promotions stop; and
  • a job-content plateau, when learning stops.

Both went with lower satisfaction, well-being, performance and commitment, and with more intention to quit, largely because plateaued people felt less supported by their organisation. But the effects were weaker for people who cared less about promotion and where the organisation and the person actively worked against the plateau, and proactive behaviour and taking on added responsibilities went with less plateauing [5]. Most of those studies are cross-sectional, so they cannot show what causes what. The direction is still useful.

Put the two kinds together and you get a distinction worth keeping our view. The hierarchical plateau is mostly out of your hands: the pyramid narrows whatever you do. The content plateau is mostly in your hands, because you can usually find something new to learn without anyone’s permission. A plateau in title with steady growth in what you can do is a perfectly good stretch of career, and often a better one than a fast climb that stops you learning.

Trapdoor

Mistaking a flat stretch for a dead end. When promotions stop, it is tempting to read it as a verdict and either quit in a hurry or quietly stop trying. Before you do either, ask which plateau you are on. If you are still learning, you may simply be at the top of a narrow pyramid, and the right move might be sideways, or nowhere at all. If you have stopped learning, that is the problem to fix, and it can often be fixed in place (see job crafting, below) or with a lateral move. Leaving to escape a hierarchical plateau can land you on the same plateau one floor up, with less trust than you had.

Your tools expire; your judgement compounds#

Over decades, some of what you know will become worth less. That is a real effect, and it has a slogan attached that is not real.

The real effect is the one “Career capital” in Chapter 20 described: in fast-changing fields, the earnings premium for a degree in something like computer science falls by more than half over the first decade in the working-paper version of a large study of US job postings, because the skills jobs ask for change underneath it [6]. Seen over a whole career, the same study adds two things: in faster-changing careers the age-earnings profile is flatter, and people tend to move out of fast-changing occupations as they get older [6].

Myth CheckYour skills have a half-life of five yearsPartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

The decay is real. The number is not. The labour-market evidence shows that skills tied to fast-changing work lose market value, fastest in technology [6]. But the “five-year half-life” traces back, as far as our researchers could follow it, to a hedged aside in talk slides by John Seely Brown: the half-life of skills is shrinking, “perhaps as low as 5 years”, with no data or method behind it [7]. By 2010 it was circulating as a talk line [8 weak], and it now appears with confident attributions that we could not trace to any primary report. Say instead: tool-specific skills lose value, sometimes quickly; there is no measured half-life.

So what lasts? The same study suggests an answer: in the working-paper version, the authors’ model explains why ability pays relatively more in slower-changing careers, “because learning gains accumulate” [6]. Our reading, which is a view rather than a finding our view, is that the durable part of expertise is judgement: knowing which problem matters, how systems fail, how to work with people, how to learn the next tool quickly. The specific framework you use this year is a tool. Your ability to choose the right one, and to explain why, compounds. That is why “Career capital” in Chapter 20 argued for skills that compound rather than skills that are fashionable.

Age itself is a smaller factor than many people fear: as Chapter 20 noted, it is largely unrelated to core task performance [9]. Where performance stalls with time, the better explanation is usually that people stop learning, not that they get older our view. A meta-analysis of work experience found that the amount of relevant experience predicted performance better than time served [10], which is the point “Experience is not expertise” in Chapter 20 makes.

The middle of the road#

Somewhere in the middle of a working life, many people hit a stretch when the question “is this it?” gets louder. There is a famous finding about this, and a famous argument about the finding.

In cross-sectional data from 145 countries, the relationship between age and happiness is U-shaped, with the low point around age 50, after controlling for education, marital status and employment [11]. Critics point out that cross-sectional data compare different people at different ages, so they cannot show what happens to any one person over time; that the longitudinal evidence is mixed; that older adults often remember midlife as one of the better periods of their lives; and that a single average curve hides very different paths [12].

The honest summary is that surveys find a midlife dip on average, and that many people do not follow the average our view. If you are in a low stretch, it is not a law of nature and it is not permanent. And it is a reasonable moment to ask the questions in the rest of this chapter, about what keeps you going and whether your job could be shaped to supply more of it.

That is roughly what Meera did. Her mug is a joke and an argument. The evidence on sabbaticals is limited, but one careful study is encouraging: in a quasi-experiment, 129 university academics on sabbatical were compared with 129 matched colleagues who were not. During the sabbatical, the people on leave lost fewer resources, gained more and reported higher well-being, and the comparison group showed no change; the gains were largest for people who felt in control, detached properly from work, and spent the time outside their home country [13]. Academics take sabbaticals routinely, so this does not tell you what a career break would do for a software engineer, and the study does not say how long the benefits lasted. But it is a reason to treat rest as part of the work of a long career rather than a prize at the end of it, and it fits what Chapter 17 found about switching off [14].

What keeps people going#

Over a few months you can run on adrenaline, deadlines and the fear of looking bad. Over decades you can’t. So what does the research say sustains people?

The best-supported framework is self-determination theory. Its founders’ review of the research at work distinguishes autonomous motivation, doing something because you find it interesting or because you have made its value your own, from controlled motivation, doing it because of pressure, from outside or from inside yourself. Work that satisfies three basic needs, competence (getting better at something), autonomy (having some say in how you work) and relatedness (being connected to people), promotes autonomous motivation, better-quality performance and well-being [15].

This does not mean money is irrelevant, and the evidence says the two work together. A meta-analysis of 40 years of studies, covering 183 samples and more than 212,000 people, found that intrinsic motivation predicted performance whether or not incentives were present, that intrinsic motivation predicted the quality of performance better and incentives predicted its quantity better, and that intrinsic motivation mattered less when incentives were tied directly to performance [16]. Pay for the work, and want to do it: both, not either.

Meaningful work#

The strongest pattern in this area is about meaning. A meta-analysis of 44 articles, with more than 23,000 people, found that how meaningful people find their work correlates strongly with how engaged, committed and satisfied they are at work, more weakly with their health and life satisfaction, and only modestly with how well they say they perform [17].

Those are correlations, so they cannot show that meaningful work causes engagement; engaged people may also find their work more meaningful. But the shape is worth noticing. Meaning goes with how work feels far more than with how well it is done, which is a reason to want it for your own sake rather than as a productivity trick.

Meaning has a cost, and the research is clear about that too. Zookeepers with a strong sense of calling found deep meaning in their work, and were also more likely to treat it as a moral duty and to sacrifice pay, personal time and comfort for it [18]. In a series of experiments, observers judged unpaid extra hours and demeaning tasks as more legitimate when the worker was seen as passionate, assuming such workers would volunteer anyway [19]. And people accept lower pay for work they find meaningful [20]. Put plainly: meaningful work is good for you, and it is also the easiest thing for an employer to underpay. Love the work, and still negotiate (“Should you negotiate?” in Chapter 9), and still keep the boundaries from Chapter 17.

Myth CheckFind your passion first, and the rest will followPartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

“Choosing without predicting the future” in Chapter 20 met the evidence on this when Dev was choosing a skill; here it is as a verdict on the slogan. Believing that a passion is something you find, already formed, has costs: in five studies, mostly with students, people who saw interests as fixed lost interest faster when a new interest became difficult, and, as the authors put it, urging people to find their passion may lead them “to put all their eggs in one basket but then to drop that basket when it becomes difficult to carry” [21]. But the opposite slogan is not proven either: people who believe passion comes from finding the right fit and people who believe it is developed over time can both reach well-being and success at work [22]. The popular argument that skill should come before passion [23 weak] is a practitioner’s case, not a finding. The honest version: interest often grows with competence and investment, and you rarely know in advance where it will grow.

Money, over decades#

Money matters, and it matters in a particular way over a long career.

The old claim that happiness stops rising above an income of about $75,000 came from a 2010 US study, in which emotional well-being showed “no further progress” beyond that level [24]. Later work with 1.7 million moment-by-moment reports from more than 33,000 employed US adults found well-being rising with the logarithm of income, with no plateau above $75,000 [25]. When Killingsworth re-analysed the data together with Kahneman, one of the 2010 authors, and a third researcher, they found that happiness flattened at around $100,000 only for the least happy 15 to 20% of people, while for the majority it kept rising [26]. “Rising with the logarithm” means that each doubling of income is worth about the same: going from 50,000 to 100,000 matters about as much as going from 100,000 to 200,000. These are American, correlational findings, so treat the dollar figures as local.

Two other findings are more useful for decisions. People adapt to pay rises: in German panel data, happiness adapted to income gains within about four years, though not to gains in status [27], and people who stayed in the same job became less satisfied over time even as their wages rose [28]. And losses hurt more than gains help: falls in income reduced well-being by more than equal rises increased it, in large German and British samples [29].

The practical rules that follow are our view, built on those findings our view. Do not take a job you would dislike for a raise you will get used to in a few years. Take seriously the parts of a job that do not wear off: security, the status of the work, and how you spend your days. And be wary of moves that trade a stable income for a volatile one unless the upside is large, because the bad years will hurt more than the good ones help.

Shape the job you have#

Before you decide that the problem is the job, it is worth asking how much of the job you could change from inside it.

Amy Wrzesniewski and Jane Dutton called this job crafting: employees reshape their work by changing its task boundaries (what they do, and how much of each thing), its relational boundaries (whom they work with, and how) and its cognitive boundaries (how they think about what the work is for), and in doing so change both what the work means to them and who they are at work [30]. That was a theory. Later intervention studies tested it: across 14 studies, training people to craft their jobs increased crafting (g = 0.26), work engagement (g = 0.31) and contextual performance, the helpful behaviour around the core job (g = 0.39); gains in core task performance appeared only among healthcare workers [31]. Those are small to moderate effects from a small set of studies with wide uncertainty. But they are real, and they come from changes people made themselves.

Even a job title can be crafted. In one study at a health-care system, employees who wrote their own self-reflective job titles reported less emotional exhaustion five weeks later [32].

Dev has been doing this for years without a name for it. At Corvid she turned a testing job into one that touched the payments code. At Lanternfish she turned an old tester’s instinct for how things break into the testability work of Chapter 20, and the relationships she built through it became the squad she was offered in Chapter 21. Crafting is also the cheapest answer to a content plateau: you rarely need permission to start learning something next to your job.

Your Move 22.1Craft your current job

Draw three columns: tasks, people, meaning. In each, write one change you could make in the next month without anyone’s permission and one you would need to ask for.

  • Tasks: something to do more of, less of, or differently.
  • People: someone to work with more, or a different way of working with someone you already do.
  • Meaning: a more accurate answer to “who does this work help, and how?”

Then pick one change from the “no permission” row and put it in your diary.

Reveal one way to play it

Good answers are small and specific. Dev’s, at the start of her last year at Lanternfish:

  • Tasks. No permission: spend Friday mornings turning the most common on-call failure into an automated check. Ask: own the release-safety review for the whole platform team.
  • People. No permission: pair once a fortnight with the newest engineer. Ask: a monthly hour with the head of customer support, who sees the failures first.
  • Meaning. “I make sure warehouses get the right shipments” is truer, and more motivating, than “I write tests”.

Weak answers are vague (“be more strategic”) or are really requests for a different job. If every change you can think of needs permission, or none of them would help, you may have learned something important: the problem is not the shape of the job but the job, and the last section of this chapter is for you.

What success looks like from the inside#

There is one more finding worth having before the next decision, because it explains why people with apparently successful careers can feel stuck, and vice versa.

A meta-analysis of the predictors of career success separated objective success (pay and promotions) from subjective success (how satisfied people are with their careers). Human capital, such as education and experience, and demographics related more strongly to the objective kind; organisational sponsorship and stable personal traits related more strongly to the subjective kind [33]. In plain words: the things that get you paid and promoted are not quite the same things that make you satisfied with how it went. You can be good at one and poor at the other.

That is the career-game version of the thesis of this book. The trailer, the film and the review are different things judged by different audiences; so are your pay slip, your title and your own sense of whether your working life is being well spent. Meera’s three regrets were all about the last of those. None of them would show up on a CV.

The next move, from strength#

Which brings us back to the recruiter.

INT. DEV'S FLAT — WEDNESDAY, 6:40 PM

The call is from a recruiter at Kestrel, a payments company about four times Lanternfish’s size. She has done her homework: she has read Dev’s public write-up on designing services to be testable before they are built, which a friend of a friend shared, and she has spoken to someone who worked with Dev at Corvid.

Dev Can I ask how you found me?

“Your name came up twice in a week, from two different people. That doesn’t happen often.” A pause, and the sound of a screen being scrolled. “Sorry, I have to ask. Dev, as in developer?”

Dev Staff engineer, actually. Or that’s the job you’re calling about, isn’t it?

The recruiter laughs. The role is a staff engineer leading testability and release safety across a dozen teams: exactly the work Dev has spent two years building, at three times the scale. Dev says she is interested, and that she will need a week before deciding whether to start a process.

After the call she does something she has not done for four years. She opens the old spreadsheet and clicks on the first tab, the one she renamed Game 1 on a Sunday night in Chapter 1. Forty-seven rows. Forty-six noes.

She adds row 48: Kestrel, staff engineer. Came to me.

Look at the difference between row 47 and row 48, because it is the whole book. The first time, Dev was a stranger to every employer she applied to, with a résumé of duties and nobody inside to vouch for her. This time the employer came to her, because people who had watched her film talked about it, and because she had made some of it visible in public. That is the loop working: the long game and the career game producing the raw material of the next snapshot.

Playing from strength does not mean skipping the method. It means the method gets easier, and the answers more honest. Dev goes back to Chapter 2 and runs the same questions she ran four years earlier.

Push or pull? Mostly pull: the work at Kestrel is her work at a scale Lanternfish does not have. There is one push: the staff role she wants at Lanternfish depends on a reorganisation that has slipped twice.

Ask before you leave. She asks. Tomás is straight with her, which is why she trusts him: the staff role might come “next cycle”, and he gives her a date and a condition, which is a real maybe in the sense of “Ask before you leave” in Chapter 2. It is also eight months away and depends on a budget neither of them controls.

Does switching pay? In US data, job switchers’ wage growth usually beats stayers’, but by a small margin on average, and the gap shrinks when the market is slack [34]. External hires are typically paid more than internal promotees, around 18% more in one well-known study, but are rated lower for their first two years, because they start the long game from zero [35]. Dev knows exactly what that means: at Kestrel, nobody has seen her film.

Search from strength. She is employed, which helps: employed job seekers get more offers per application and better-paid ones [36]. She can afford to run a real process, ask hard questions, and say no.

The door you leave through. In one large data set, managers rehired by a former employer performed about as well as other new managers at first, but improved less and were more likely to leave again, often for the same reasons [37]. If she goes, she wants the pull to be real, so that she is not back in two years, and she wants to leave well, so that she could be.

Your Move 22.2Write your next trailer from your real film

Imagine a recruiter calls you tomorrow about the job you would most like next. Using only evidence from your actual work, write:

  1. One sentence saying what you do, at what scope, with what result.
  2. Three bullets of evidence, each specific enough to survive five minutes of questions (“Signals, not secrets” in Chapter 1).
  3. Two names of people who would vouch for you without being asked twice.
  4. One honest gap: something the job needs that your film does not yet show.
Reveal one way to play it

Dev’s version:

  1. “I make systems testable before they are built: I run testability design reviews across Lanternfish’s platform, and I lead the reliability squad’s technical work.”
  2. Evidence: reviewed more than forty service designs for testability before they were built, with a log of which suggestions squads adopted; cut failed releases from roughly one a week to one a quarter by adding pre-release checks to every service; wrote the internal guide to testable services that new engineers now start with.
  3. Names: Tomás, and the engineer she named as the squad’s lead in Chapter 21.
  4. Gap: “I’ve never set technical direction across a dozen teams I don’t work in every day.”

If you struggled with item 2, the fix is not a better adjective; it is the Wins habit from Chapter 11, started today. If you struggled with item 3, the fix is the long game (Chapter 10). And item 4 is not a weakness to hide. Saying it plainly is a strong signal in its own right: it shows you know the difference between your trailer and your film.

A trailer is half the preparation. The other half is the decision itself, run properly, as Dev ran it.

Your Move 22.3Stay, go or grow, revisited

Run the decision path from Figure 2.3 on your current job, today, even if you are not thinking of leaving. For each step, write one line:

  1. What is pushing you, and what is pulling you?
  2. For each push: could it be fixed here? Have you asked, with a date?
  3. For each pull: is it on offer here?
  4. Which plateau, if any, are you on: title or learning?
  5. If you went, what would you take with you: skills, stories, people?
Reveal one way to play it

The useful outcome is not a decision; it is knowing what would change your mind. Most people who do this find they are in one of three places. Stay, and craft: the pushes are fixable or small, the pulls are available inside, and the job-crafting exercise above has work to do. Grow: the pull is real and on offer inside, so ask for it, with a date. Go, from strength: the pull is real, it is not on offer, and question 5 has good answers. If question 5 has thin answers, that is information too: the next year’s work is to build them, wherever you are.

Dev’s answers: 1, pull (scale) and one push (a stalled role); 2, asked, with a date, eight months out; 3, not at Lanternfish’s size; 4, neither, yet; 5, plenty. Which is why she says yes to the process.

Kestrel’s process takes five weeks. It is the same game as four years ago, and it feels entirely different: Dev tells stories from her story bank instead of inventing answers in the room, asks the questions on her Questions for them tab (“Interview them back” in Chapter 7), scopes the design exercise before starting it (“The take-home task” in Chapter 8), and negotiates once, calmly, with numbers (“Dev negotiates, once” in Chapter 9). When the offer comes, she tells Tomás first, in person, on a Monday morning rather than a Friday afternoon. He is disappointed and not surprised, and he says the thing good managers say: “If it doesn’t work out, call me.” She hands over the testability reviews with a written guide and two weeks of pairing, because the door she leaves through stays in the building.

INT. LANTERNFISH, KITCHEN — DEV'S LAST FRIDAY, 8:50 AM

There is a crowd in the kitchen, which is unusual at 8:50, and a large cardboard box, which is unprecedented.

Old Faithful has been unplugged. In its place stands a new machine, matt black, with a touchscreen and no personality. The facilities manager has taped a printout to the wall: every repair ticket the machine ever had, starting with the one Dev filed in her first months, and a photo of the descale button.

Kai I want it on record that it waited until you were leaving.
Dev It was never going to let me see it go.
Rex We should say a few words.
Kai You absolutely should not.

The new machine makes a perfect flat white in nineteen seconds. Somebody applauds. Dev drinks it and finds, slightly to her alarm, that she misses the noise.

At ten, she opens her laptop for the last time on the Lanternfish network, scrolls to the Wins tab, and adds one line: Left the testability reviews in better hands than I found them. Then she closes the spreadsheet and, for once, does not add a tab.

That is not quite the end of Dev’s time at Lanternfish. On her last afternoon she has two more things to do: refer someone, and give someone a reference. The “Epilogue: The Loop” closes the loop.

Rewind
  • A career is a sequence of loops, not a ladder. People change jobs often, and much of what they learn is tied to an occupation. Each loop starts from the skills, stories and people the last one produced.
  • Plateaus are normal. Pyramids narrow, so promotions stop. A plateau in title with growth in skill is fine; a plateau in learning is the one to fix.
  • Your tools expire; your judgement compounds. Fast-changing skills lose market value, but the “five-year half-life” is a guess that became a statistic. Age is largely unrelated to core task performance.
  • The midlife dip is an average, and a contested one. Rest is maintenance, not a reward for finishing.
  • What sustains people: competence, autonomy and relatedness. Intrinsic motivation and pay work together. Meaningful work goes strongly with engagement and satisfaction, and is also easy for employers to underpay.
  • “Find your passion” is half right: interest often grows with competence and investment.
  • Money: happiness keeps rising with income for most people, but you adapt to raises within a few years and losses hurt more than gains help. Don’t take a job you dislike for a raise.
  • Craft the job you have (tasks, people, meaning) before deciding it is the wrong job. When you do move, make the move from strength: the same questions as Chapter 2, with better answers.

  5. Yang, W., Niven, K., & Johnson, S. (2019). Career plateau: A review of 40 years of research. Journal of Vocational Behavior, 110, 286–302. doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2018.11.005 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence systematic review of 72 empirical sources (1977-2017) opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Plateaued people (hierarchical or job-content plateau) report poorer satisfaction, well-being, performance and commitment and more turnover intention, largely because they perceive less organisational support; effects are weaker when people care less about promotion and when organisation and individual counteract the plateau; proactivity and added responsibilities are linked to less plateauing.

    Caveats: Narrative review of mostly cross-sectional studies.

  6. Deming, D. J., & Noray, K. (2020). Earnings Dynamics, Changing Job Skills, and STEM Careers*. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 135(4), 1965–2005. doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjaa021 ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence job-postings data plus earnings data and a model opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Returns to experience are a race between on-the-job learning and skill obsolescence; in faster-changing careers age-earnings profiles are flatter. The earnings premium for applied STEM majors (computer science, engineering) is highest at entry and, in the working paper, declines by more than 50% in the first decade; graduates sort out of fast-changing occupations as they age. STEM vacancies requiring machine-learning/AI skills rose 460% between 2007 and 2017 (working paper).

    Caveats: Measures job-skill change, not a "half-life" of an individual's skills.

  7. Brown, J. S. (2026). Presentation slides (Toronto talk), undated. johnseelybrown.com. johnseelybrown.com/toronto6up.pdf ↩

    ✕Myth or unsupported talk slides practitioner writing opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Slide text: "In a world of increasingly rapid change, the half life of many stocks/skills is constantly shrinking. (perhaps as low as 5 years)" - a hedged guess, with no data or method.

    Caveats: Earliest traceable form found of the "skills have a five-year half-life" claim; it is an aside, not a finding.

  8. Campbell, G. (2010). John Seely Brown, Closing Plenary for NMC 2010. Gardner Writes (blog). www.gardnercampbell.net/blog1/john-seely-brown-closing-plenary-for-nmc-2010/ ↩

    Weak evidence live notes of a conference talk practitioner writing opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: An attendee's notes of John Seely Brown's 2010 NMC plenary: "Most skills we teach have a half-life of about five years."

    Caveats: Second-hand notes; shows the figure circulating as a talk line by 2010.

  9. Ng, T. W. H., & Feldman, D. C. (2008). The relationship of age to ten dimensions of job performance. Journal of Applied Psychology, 93(2), 392–423. doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.2.392 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Age was largely unrelated to core task performance, creativity and training performance, but had stronger relationships with the other seven dimensions (citizenship, safety, counterproductive behaviour, aggression, substance use, tardiness, absence); age links with task performance and counterproductive behaviour are curvilinear.

    Caveats: Cross-sectional age comparisons.

  10. Quińones, M. A., Ford, J. K., & Teachout, M. S. (1995). THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN WORK EXPERIENCE AND JOB PERFORMANCE: A CONCEPTUAL AND META‐ANALYTIC REVIEW. Personnel Psychology, 48(4), 887–910. doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.1995.tb01785.x ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Work experience correlates rho = .27 with job performance; experience measured as amount (rho = .43) or at task level (.41) predicts best, more than time-based measures; correlations are higher with hard measures such as work samples (.39) than with ratings (.24).

    Caveats: Contrast sackett2022 (Part I): years of experience predict new-hire performance only .07. What you did repeatedly matters more than how long you were employed.

  11. Blanchflower, D. G. (2021). Is happiness U-shaped everywhere? Age and subjective well-being in 145 countries. Journal of Population Economics, 34(2), 575–624. doi.org/10.1007/s00148-020-00797-z ↩

    Moderate evidence cross-sectional analysis of many national surveys opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: A U-shaped happiness-age curve with a low point around age 50 appears in 145 countries (109 developing), controlling for education, marital and labour-force status.

    Caveats: Cross-sectional; contested (galambos2020).

  12. Galambos, N. L., Krahn, H. J., Johnson, M. D., & Lachman, M. E. (2020). The U Shape of Happiness Across the Life Course: Expanding the Discussion. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 15(4), 898–912. doi.org/10.1177/1745691620902428 ↩

    Moderate evidence critical review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Argues the U-shape is less robust than assumed: cross-sectional data cannot show within-person change, and both cross-sectional and longitudinal evidence are mixed; older adults recall midlife as one of the more positive periods; a single average trajectory hides diverse pathways.

    Caveats: Argument paper; Blanchflower and others have replied (not opened).

  13. Davidson, O. B., Eden, D., Westman, M., Cohen-Charash, Y., Hammer, L. B., Kluger, A. N., et al. (2010). Sabbatical leave: Who gains and how much?. Journal of Applied Psychology, 95(5), 953–964. doi.org/10.1037/a0020068 ↩

    Moderate evidence quasi-experiment with matched controls, measured before, during and after opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Among 129 faculty members on sabbatical, resource loss declined and resource gain and well-being rose during the sabbatical, while 129 matched controls showed no change. Well-being rose more for those with higher respite self-efficacy and more control, who were more detached from work, who had a more positive sabbatical experience, and who spent it outside their home country.

    Caveats: Academics, for whom sabbaticals are routine; the published abstract does not say how long the gains lasted after people returned to work.

  14. Sonnentag, S. (2012). Psychological Detachment From Work During Leisure Time. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 21(2), 114–118. doi.org/10.1177/0963721411434979 ↩

    Strong evidence review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Mentally switching off from work during leisure relates to better life satisfaction and less strain without lower engagement at work.

    Caveats: Short review of a robust literature.

  15. Deci, E. L., Olafsen, A. H., & Ryan, R. M. (2017). Self-Determination Theory in Work Organizations: The State of a Science. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 4(1), 19–43. doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113108 ↩

    Strong evidence Annual Review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Self-determination theory distinguishes autonomous motivation (intrinsic or fully internalised) from controlled motivation; satisfying needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness promotes autonomous motivation, high-quality performance and wellness at work.

    Caveats: Written by the theory's founders.

  16. Cerasoli, C. P., Nicklin, J. M., & Ford, M. T. (2014). Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives jointly predict performance: A 40-year meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin, 140(4), 980–1008. doi.org/10.1037/a0035661 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Intrinsic motivation predicts performance (rho = .21-.45) whether or not incentives are present; intrinsic motivation predicts quality better and incentives predict quantity better; when incentives are tied directly to performance, intrinsic motivation matters less ("crowding out").

    Caveats: Mostly correlational studies.

  17. Allan, B. A., Batz-Barbarich, C., Sterling, H. M., & Tay, L. (2019). Outcomes of Meaningful Work: A Meta-Analysis. Journal of Management Studies, 56(3), 500–528. doi.org/10.1111/joms.12406 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis with meta-analytic structural equation modelling opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Across 44 articles (N = 23,144), meaningful work had large correlations (r = .70 and above) with work engagement, commitment and job satisfaction; moderate to large correlations (r = .44 to -.49) with life satisfaction, life meaning, general health and (negatively) withdrawal intentions; and small to moderate correlations (r = -.19 to .33) with organisational citizenship behaviours, self-rated job performance and negative affect. The best-fitting model had meaningful work predicting engagement, commitment and satisfaction, which in turn predicted self-rated performance, citizenship and withdrawal intentions.

    Caveats: Correlational primary studies; performance is self-rated. The abstract gives ranges, not the correlation for each outcome, so only the ranges are printed.

  18. Bunderson, J. S., & Thompson, J. A. (2009). The Call of the Wild: Zookeepers, Callings, and the Double-edged Sword of Deeply Meaningful Work. Administrative Science Quarterly, 54(1), 32–57. doi.org/10.2189/asqu.2009.54.1.32 ↩

    Moderate evidence qualitative interviews plus survey opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Zookeepers with a strong calling found deep meaning in their work but were also more likely to see it as a moral duty and to sacrifice pay, personal time and comfort for it - meaningful work as a double-edged sword.

    Caveats: One occupation.

  19. Kim, J. Y., Campbell, T. H., Shepherd, S., & Kay, A. C. (2020). Understanding contemporary forms of exploitation: Attributions of passion serve to legitimize the poor treatment of workers. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 118(1), 121–148. doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000190 ↩

    Moderate evidence seven studies plus a meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People judge unpaid extra hours and demeaning off-role tasks as more legitimate when the worker is presumed passionate, because they assume passionate workers would volunteer and that the work is its own reward; people also attribute passion to exploited workers.

    Caveats: Vignette studies of observers' judgments.

  20. Hu, J., & Hirsh, J. B. (2017). Accepting Lower Salaries for Meaningful Work. Frontiers in Psychology, 8. doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01649 ↩

    Moderate evidence four studies (experiments and cross-national surveys) opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People accept lower minimum salaries for jobs they see as meaningful; workers with more meaningful work were more likely to turn down higher-paying offers elsewhere, and the effect strengthened between 2005 and 2015.

    Caveats: Stated willingness, not actual pay paths.

  21. O’Keefe, P. A., Dweck, C. S., & Walton, G. M. (2018). Implicit Theories of Interest: Finding Your Passion or Developing It?. Psychological Science, 29(10), 1653–1664. doi.org/10.1177/0956797618780643 ↩

    Moderate evidence five studies including experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People who believe interests are fixed ("find your passion") show less interest in areas outside their existing interests, expect boundless motivation once a passion is found, and lose interest faster when a new interest becomes difficult, compared with people induced to see interests as developed.

    Caveats: Students and short lab tasks.

  22. Chen, P., Ellsworth, P. C., & Schwarz, N. (2015). Finding a Fit or Developing It. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 41(10), 1411–1424. doi.org/10.1177/0146167215596988 ↩

    Moderate evidence four studies opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: "Fit" theorists (passion comes from finding the right work) and "develop" theorists (passion grows over time) show different priorities and motivational patterns, but both beliefs can lead to vocational well-being and success.

    Caveats: Shows develop-theory is not the only route to satisfaction.

  23. Newport, C. (2012). So Good They Can't Ignore You: Why Skills Trump Passion in the Quest for Work You Love. Author website book listing. calnewport.com/writing/ ↩

    Weak evidence popular book practitioner writing opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Popular argument that skill and mastery come before passion; the book popularised "career capital". Title- and blurb-level only.

    Caveats: Specific phrasings attributed to the book (e.g. "rare and valuable skills") were not verified against the text here.

  24. Kahneman, D., & Deaton, A. (2010). High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional well-being. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 107(38), 16489–16493. doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107 ↩

    Strong evidence large daily survey opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Life evaluation rises steadily with log income; emotional well-being also rises but showed "no further progress beyond an annual income of ~$75,000"; low income worsens the emotional pain of misfortunes.

    Caveats: The $75,000 plateau was later traced to a ceiling effect in yes/no measures (killingsworth2023), with the flattening holding only for the least happy people.

  25. Killingsworth, M. A. (2021). Experienced well-being rises with income, even above $75,000 per year. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 118(4). doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016976118 ↩

    Strong evidence experience sampling opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Both experienced and evaluative well-being rose linearly with log income, as steeply for higher as for lower earners; no plateau above $75,000.

    Caveats: App users; correlational.

  26. Killingsworth, M. A., Kahneman, D., & Mellers, B. (2023). Income and emotional well-being: A conflict resolved. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120(10). doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208661120 ↩

    Strong evidence adversarial collaboration reanalysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: For the least happy ~15-20% of people, happiness rises with income up to about $100,000 then flattens; for the happier majority it keeps rising, and accelerates for the happiest. Kahneman and Deaton's measures could not discriminate degrees of happiness (a ceiling effect).

    Caveats: Correlational. PubMed Central lists published critiques, including "Inappropriate causal assumptions underlie Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers' conclusions", and a 2024 reply (killingsworth2024).

  27. Di Tella, R., Haisken-De New, J., & MacCulloch, R. (2010). Happiness adaptation to income and to status in an individual panel. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 76(3), 834–852. doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2010.09.016 ↩

    Moderate evidence individual panel opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: In German panel data, happiness adapts to income gains within four years but not to status gains; in the long run a one-standard-deviation status rise matched an income rise of 285% of a standard deviation.

    Caveats: Working-paper abstract; "status" is occupational prestige; one country.

  28. Grund, C., & Sliwka, D. (2007). Reference-Dependent Preferences and the Impact of Wage Increases on Job Satisfaction: Theory and Evidence. Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics, 163(2), 313–335. doi.org/10.1628/093245607781261351 ↩

    Moderate evidence model plus panel data opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Job satisfaction depends on wage increases as well as wage levels; staying in the same job, employees become less satisfied over time even though wages rise.

    Caveats: One country.

  29. Boyce, C. J., Wood, A. M., Banks, J., Clark, A. E., & Brown, G. D. A. (2013). Money, Well-Being, and Loss Aversion. Psychological Science, 24(12), 2557–2562. doi.org/10.1177/0956797613496436 ↩

    Strong evidence two national panels opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Income losses hurt well-being more than equal gains help, beyond diminishing returns; suggests small, stable raises may serve well-being better than volatile pay even at some cost to long-run growth.

    Caveats: Observational panels.

  30. Wrzesniewski, A., & Dutton, J. E. (2001). Crafting a Job: Revisioning Employees as Active Crafters of Their Work. The Academy of Management Review, 26(2), 179. doi.org/10.2307/259118 ↩

    Moderate evidence theoretical model opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Employees "craft" jobs by changing task, relational and cognitive boundaries, which changes the meaning of the work and their work identity.

    Caveats: Foundational theory; tested later (oprea2019).

  31. Oprea, B. T., Barzin, L., Vîrgă, D., Iliescu, D., & Rusu, A. (2019). Effectiveness of job crafting interventions: a meta-analysis and utility analysis. European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology, 28(6), 723–741. doi.org/10.1080/1359432x.2019.1646728 ↩

    Moderate evidence meta-analysis of interventions opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Job-crafting interventions raised overall crafting (g = 0.26), work engagement (g = 0.31) and contextual performance (g = 0.39); task-performance gains appeared only among healthcare employees; effects on performance ran through engagement.

    Caveats: Small number of studies; wide confidence intervals.

  32. Grant, A. M., Berg, J. M., & Cable, D. M. (2014). Job Titles as Identity Badges: How Self-Reflective Titles Can Reduce Emotional Exhaustion. Academy of Management Journal, 57(4), 1201–1225. doi.org/10.5465/amj.2012.0338 ↩

    Moderate evidence qualitative study plus field quasi-experiment opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Employees who created their own self-reflective job titles reported less emotional exhaustion five weeks later, via self-verification and psychological safety.

    Caveats: Small field samples; titles as identity, not as pay.

  33. NG, T. W. H., EBY, L. T., SORENSEN, K. L., & FELDMAN, D. C. (2005). PREDICTORS OF OBJECTIVE AND SUBJECTIVE CAREER SUCCESS: A META-ANALYSIS. Personnel Psychology, 58(2), 367–408. doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.2005.00515.x ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Human capital and demographics relate more strongly to objective career success (salary, promotion); organisational sponsorship and stable traits relate more strongly to career satisfaction.

    Caveats: Correlational.

  34. Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta (2026). Wage Growth Tracker. Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta (based on Census Bureau / BLS Current Population Survey). www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/data/wage-growth-tracker ↩

    Strong evidence dataset opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Median 12-month wage growth of matched individuals, with separate series for job switchers vs job stayers; methodology page defines a switcher as in a different occupation or industry than a year ago or changed employer/duties in the past three months.

    Caveats: Median of individuals, not causal; switcher definition is broad and local (movers drop out of the CPS); the gap varies with the cycle.

  35. Bidwell, M. (2011). Paying more to get less: The effects of external hiring versus internal mobility. Administrative Science Quarterly, 56(3), 369-407. doi.org/10.1177/0001839211433562 (copy read) ↩

    Moderate evidence article opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: US investment-banking division, 2003-2009 - external hires paid about 18-20% more than internal promotions, significantly lower performance evaluations in first two years, higher exit rates, faster promotion if they stay beyond two years; similar in another bank and a publisher.

    Caveats: Secondary write-up of a peer-reviewed paper; one industry.

  36. Faberman, R. J., Mueller, A. I., Sahin, A., & Topa, G. (2017). Job search behavior among the employed and non-employed. Working paper (August 2017); later published in Econometrica (2022) per secondary listings, not verified. business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/20320/FMST_WP.pdf ↩

    Strong evidence working-paper opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Employed searchers receive more offers per application and higher wage offers than the non-employed, even after controls; unconditional wage-offer gap 40 log points (49%), 25 log points after controls; employed nearly three times more efficient at converting search effort into offers.

    Caveats: Working-paper figures; the published version may differ. Part of the gap reflects who is unemployed (selection), which the paper models.

  37. Arnold, J. D., Van Iddekinge, C. H., Campion, M. C., Bauer, T. N., & Campion, M. A. (2021). Welcome Back? Job Performance and Turnover of Boomerang Employees Compared to Internal and External Hires. Journal of Management, 47(8), 2198–2225. doi.org/10.1177/0149206320936335 ↩

    Strong evidence longitudinal company data opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Rehired "boomerang" managers performed similarly to other hires in year one but improved less over time and were more likely to leave again, often for the same reasons.

    Caveats: One large organisation; published online 2020, volume 47 (2021).
