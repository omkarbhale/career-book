A book that claims to cover everything about working life is either very long or not telling the truth. This one is long enough already, so here is the truth: there are whole subjects it leaves out on purpose. Some are different games with their own rules. Some change by country and by month, so that anything we printed would mislead someone by the time they read it. And some belong to professionals, such as lawyers, doctors and financial advisers, whom no book can replace.

Leaving a topic out is not the same as pretending it doesn’t exist. So this appendix does three things for each one: it says in a line or two why the topic is outside this book, it points to where the book touches it, and it offers a short list of next steps that we have checked.

How the resources were chosen. Every resource below was opened during the making of this book, and each is a recognised publisher, a university or public body, a peer-reviewed journal, or an author with a track record in the field. Each card is labelled by what it rests on: Evidence-based (research, official statistics, the law, or an official public service), Theory, or Practitioner view (experience that has not been tested, which is sometimes the best guidance available). We prefer free and official sources where they are as good as anything else, and we never prefer them over a clearly better paid one. There are no affiliate links and no tracking parameters: books link to a library catalogue or the publisher. Where we could check official sources only for some regions, we say which, and we say plainly what we could not check. Two good resources beat four adequate ones, so some sections are short.

One more caution: the links were checked in October 2026. Official sites reorganise, and laws, visa rules and benefit schemes change. If a page has moved, search the same organisation’s site rather than trusting a copy elsewhere.

Managing people#

Why it’s out. Chapter 21 is about whether and when to manage, not how. Learning to manage well is a craft with its own large literature, and a chapter-sized summary of it would teach the vocabulary without the skill.

Where the book touches it. Chapter 21 on what the job is and how to try it; “When your friend becomes your manager” in Chapter 15 on a friend becoming your manager; Chapter 13 on feedback; “Working with your manager” in Chapter 12 on working with your own manager.

Evidence-basedBecoming a Manager

Hill, L. A. (2019) [1]

The research-based place to start. Hill followed nineteen new managers through their first year, so the book shows what the transition actually involves rather than what it is supposed to involve. Good for anyone in, or about to start, a first management job, in any industry.

Practitioner viewThe Manager's Path: A Guide for Tech Leaders Navigating Growth and Change

Fournier, C. (2017) [2]

Written by an engineer who became a chief technology officer, and organised by stage, from mentoring an intern to managing managers. The most useful single book for people moving into management in software teams. One practitioner’s experience, clearly told.

Practitioner viewThe Making of a Manager

Zhuo, J. (2019) [3]

A warm, practical guide by someone who became a manager at twenty-five, built around the questions new managers actually ask: hiring, meetings, feedback, looking after people’s careers. Suits readers who want reassurance as well as method; practitioner experience rather than research.

If you read only one study about the job itself, read the one behind “What managing actually is” in Chapter 21: people management skills measurably reduce how many people leave [4].

Freelancing and founding a company#

Why it’s out. This book assumes you are employed. When you work for yourself the games change: you become the employer, the snapshot game is played with clients and investors, and there is no review cycle except the bank balance. That needs its own guide.

Where the book touches it. Chapter 2 on deciding whether to go at all, and Chapter 22 on making a move from strength. Much of Part I (evidence, stories, referrals) transfers directly to winning clients.

Evidence-basedA Scientific Approach to Entrepreneurial Decision Making: Evidence from a Randomized Control Trial

Camuffo, A., Cordova, A., Gambardella, A., & Spina, C. (2020) [5]

A randomised trial with 116 startups: founders taught to treat their business ideas as hypotheses and test them like scientists did better and pivoted more readily than founders given the same general training. Good for anyone tempted to start something; a short, rigorous paper rather than a how-to.

Evidence-basedThe Founder's Dilemmas: Anticipating and Avoiding the Pitfalls That Can Sink a Startup

Wasserman, N. (2013) [6]

The decisions that make or break new ventures (whether to found alone, with whom, how to split equity, when to hand over control), drawn from data on almost ten thousand founders. Suits people thinking about co-founding rather than freelancing; evidence-based, but written for founders, not academics.

Official starting points. For the legal and tax side, start with your government’s own guidance: in the UK, Working for yourself [7]; in the US, the Small Business Administration’s business guide and free counselling [8]; in India, the government’s Startup India portal [9], whose detailed pages we could not read through our tools, so check its schemes directly. We did not check EU-level guidance: rules for self-employment are set mainly by each member state.

Personal finance beyond the pay cheque#

Why it’s out. Chapter 9 covers offers, pay and equity basics, and “Money, over decades” in Chapter 22 covers what the research says about money and happiness over a career. Investing, tax and pensions are country-specific, change every budget, and need qualified sources rather than a careers book.

Where the book touches it. Chapter 9 on total compensation; “Timing and the runway” in Chapter 2 on savings as a runway; “Money, over decades” in Chapter 22.

Evidence-basedThe Economic Importance of Financial Literacy: Theory and Evidence

Lusardi, A., & Mitchell, O. S. (2014) [10]

The best-known review of what research shows about financial literacy: how much people know, who knows least, and how it shows up in their decisions. Good for readers who want to understand why personal finance is hard for almost everyone, before choosing what to learn. Evidence, not advice.

Evidence-basedInvestor.gov

U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2026) [11]

The US Securities and Exchange Commission’s free investor-education site, starting from the basics. For US readers; it explains how investing works and how to avoid fraud, without selling anything.

Evidence-basedWorkplace pensions

GOV.UK (2026) [12]

How workplace pensions work in the UK, including the employer contributions and tax relief that many people leave unexamined. For UK employees; the official, plain-English starting point.

Evidence-basedSEBI Investor website

Securities and Exchange Board of India (2026) [13]

The Securities and Exchange Board of India’s investor-education site, in Hindi and English, with a toll-free investor helpline. For readers in India.

We did not find a single official EU-wide source to recommend; financial guidance is national. We would also have liked to check the UK’s MoneyHelper service, but its site refused our tools, so it is not listed here.

Employment law and workplace disputes#

Why it’s out. The book points at laws wherever they matter (notice, non-competes, pay transparency, working hours, unlawful interview questions) and tells you to check them. But a dispute needs advice on your facts, in your jurisdiction, from someone qualified. A book that tried to give it would be dangerous.

Where the book touches it. “When something is actually wrong” in Chapter 16 on when something is actually wrong and how to escalate; the Field Notes in Chapter 2 and Chapter 17.

Evidence-basedAcas advice

Acas (2026) [14]

Free, impartial advice on employment rights in England, Scotland and Wales, with template letters and a service that helps resolve disputes before a tribunal. If you work in Great Britain and something has gone wrong, this is the first call.

Evidence-basedHow to File a Charge of Employment Discrimination

U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (2026) [15]

How to file a charge of employment discrimination with the US federal agency responsible, including deadlines. For US readers who think they have been treated unlawfully; pair it with the Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division for pay and leave problems [16].

Evidence-basedWork and retirement - Your Europe

European Union (2026) [17]

The EU’s official guide to rights at work for people living and working in EU countries, in every EU language. Good for understanding EU-level rights, especially if you work in a country other than your own; national law sets most details.

Evidence-basedFAQs on Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code, 2020; Additional FAQs on Labour Codes (As on 16.03.2026)

Ministry of Labour and Employment, G. o. I., & Press Information Bureau, G. o. I. (2020) [18]

The Labour Ministry’s own answers to common questions about India’s new Labour Codes, which took effect on 21 November 2025. A starting point for Indian readers; state rules and your contract still matter, and for a dispute you need a lawyer.

In every country, for a real dispute, a qualified employment lawyer, a trade union or an official advisory service beats any website, including these.

Immigration and working abroad#

Why it’s out. Visa rules change by country and, sometimes, by month. Anything specific we printed would be wrong for some readers on the day they read it. The only safe starting point is the official source for the country you want to work in.

Where the book touches it. It mostly doesn’t, which is the honest reason for this section. If a move abroad is part of your stay, go or grow decision (Chapter 2), treat the visa as part of your runway: it can take longer than the job search.

Evidence-basedWorking in the United States

U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (2026) [19]

The official US starting point for working in the United States, covering permanent and temporary work routes and how to avoid immigration scams. Use it to find which route might apply before you spend money on advice.

Evidence-basedWork in the UK

GOV.UK (2026) [20]

The UK government’s index of work visas, including the Skilled Worker, Graduate and Global Talent routes. The official place to check eligibility; rules change, so read the current page.

Evidence-basedEU Immigration Portal

European Commission (2025) [21]

The European Commission’s practical guide to coming to the EU for work, study or family reasons, with pages for each member state and kind of worker. Good for comparing countries; the permits themselves come from national authorities.

Evidence-basedIndian Visa Online (e-Visa)

Government of India (2026) [22]

The Government of India’s official visa portal, for people coming to work in India. For Indian citizens going abroad to work, the Ministry of External Affairs runs an emigration portal, but it would not load for our tools, so start from your destination country’s official site above.

Beware of anyone who promises a visa outcome: the US immigration service’s own site has a whole section on avoiding immigration scams [19].

Mental health beyond burnout#

Why it’s out. Chapter 17 explains burnout as the WHO defines it: an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. Anxiety, depression and other mental health conditions are medical matters, and help with them belongs to clinicians. This section is a set of doors, not a substitute for walking through one.

Where the book touches it. Chapter 17 on burnout, boundaries and when the job is the problem; “Loneliness, the quiet cost” in Chapter 15 on loneliness; “When the decision is made for you” in Chapter 2 on the shock of losing a job.

If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number now, or one of the services below.

Evidence-basedGuidelines on mental health at work

World Health Organization (2022) [23]

The World Health Organization’s evidence-based guidelines on mental health at work. Written mainly for employers and policymakers, so it is the resource to hand your manager or HR team if you want your organisation to do better; it is also a clear picture of what works.

Evidence-basedFind NHS talking therapies for anxiety and depression

NHS (2026) [24]

In England, you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies for anxiety and depression without seeing a doctor first, and without a diagnosis. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run their own services.

Evidence-based988 Lifeline

988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (2026) [25]

The US 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline: free, confidential support by call, text or chat, at any hour.

Evidence-basedUpdate on National Tele Mental Health Programme (NTMHP)

Press Information Bureau, G. o. I. (2025) [26]

Tele-MANAS, India’s national tele-mental-health service: free, 24-hour counselling on the toll-free number 14416, in 20 languages.

We did not check an EU-wide service; mental health support is organised nationally, so start with your doctor or your national health service.

Salary data for your role and market#

Why it’s out. Pay figures date in months and vary by city, company and level, so any number printed here would mislead someone. Chapter 9 teaches how to find and use pay data; this section says where the trustworthy kinds live and how to read them.

Where the book touches it. Chapter 9 on offers and negotiation; “The other road: senior, staff, principal” in Chapter 21 on what managers and engineers earn at the median in the US.

How to read pay data. Official statistics are large and careful but describe whole occupations, not one company’s level. Crowdsourced sites are specific and current but come from whoever chose to report. Use both: the official figure tells you the range, the crowdsourced figure tells you where in it a particular employer sits. Job adverts with pay ranges, now required in some places (Chapter 9), are a third source and often the most current.

Evidence-basedOccupational Employment and Wage Statistics

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026) [27]

US official wage estimates for about 830 occupations, nationally and by state and metropolitan area. The most reliable picture of what an occupation pays in a US location; it cannot tell you what one company pays.

Evidence-basedEmployee earnings in the UK: 2025

Office for National Statistics (2025) [28]

The UK’s official annual survey of earnings, with breakdowns by occupation and industry. For UK readers who want a baseline before they look at advertised ranges.

Evidence-basedEarnings - Labour market

Eurostat (2026) [29]

Eurostat’s Structure of Earnings Survey, the comparable EU-wide source, broken down by occupation, age, experience and more. Updated every four years, so pair it with your national statistics office for current figures.

For technology roles, the most specific source is crowdsourced. Levels.fyi reports pay by company, level and location, weighted by how much proof each submission carries [30]. It is self-reported and commercial, so it gets no evidence card here: treat it as one input, not the truth.

We did not verify an official source of pay by occupation for India, so we have not recommended one.

Technical interview practice#

Why it’s out. Chapter 7 and Chapter 8 teach the skill behind interviews and work samples: what they measure, how to show your thinking, how to scope a task. Question banks and coding drills exist in abundance elsewhere and date quickly, so reproducing them here would add pages without adding value.

Where the book touches it. Chapter 8 on work samples, thinking aloud and take-homes; Chapter 7 on structured and behavioural interviews.

Practitioner viewTech Interview Handbook

Tay, Y. (2026) [31]

Free, well-organised preparation for software engineering interviews: a study plan, curated practice lists, and guidance on behavioural rounds. Good for busy people who want a path rather than a pile of problems. Practitioner material.

Practitioner viewBeyond Cracking the Coding Interview

McDowell, G. L., Mroczka, M., Lerner, A., & Mamano, N. (2025) [32]

The sequel to the best-known coding-interview book, by its original author and three co-authors; several chapters are free to read. Good for engineers preparing for large-company technical interviews. Practitioner material; one co-author runs a mock-interview company.

Practitioner viewDoes stress impact technical interview performance?

Behroozi, M., Shirolkar, S., Barik, T., & Parnin, C. (2020) [33]

For the evidence-minded: the small experiment behind “Being watched” in Chapter 8, in which students solving a coding problem while watched did less than half as well as those working in private. Worth reading before you over-interpret one bad whiteboard round, and before you design one.

Personality tests and types#

Why it’s out. Personality typing is popular at work, and the book does not need it for its argument. Where personality matters to the evidence (self-monitoring in Chapter 10, political skill in Chapter 16), the book cites the research directly. Here is where to read the actual science, and why types and traits are not the same thing.

Evidence-basedThe Big Five Personality Dimensions and Job Performance: A Meta-Analysis

Barrick, M. R., & Mount, M. K. (1991) [34]

The classic meta-analysis linking the Big Five personality traits to job performance: conscientiousness predicted performance across every occupational group studied, and other traits mattered only in some jobs. Good for anyone who wants to know what personality research can and cannot predict at work. Later re-analyses have revised many of the field’s estimates, as Chapter 1 explains.

Evidence-basedCautionary comments regarding the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator

Pittenger, D. J. (2005) [35]

A careful review of the psychometric problems with the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, written for consultants. Read it before your organisation sorts you into a four-letter type, or before you make a decision based on one.

Evidence-basedInternational Personality Item Pool

Pool, I. P. I. (2026) [36]

Thousands of free, public-domain personality items and scales used by researchers, including Big Five markers. For readers who want to see what a research-grade personality measure actually asks, without a paywall or a sales pitch.

Reading the research yourself#

Why it’s out. This book has done the reading for you, and graded every source. But you should not have to take our word for it. This section is for readers who want to check our sources, or anyone else’s.

Where the book touches it. Every reference list, and “How to read the evidence” in “How to Use This Book”, which explains our grades. Three habits carry most of the weight: prefer meta-analyses and large studies to single small ones; read the abstract’s numbers, not the press release’s adjectives; and check whether a famous finding has been replicated or retracted.

Evidence-basedHow to Read a Paper

Greenhalgh, T., & Dijkstra, H. P. (2024) [37]

The classic, readable introduction to critical appraisal: how to search the literature, how to judge different kinds of study, what the statistics mean. Written for health care, but most of it applies to research on work. Good for anyone who wants to read a paper and know whether to believe it.

Evidence-basedEstimating the reproducibility of psychological science

Collaboration, O. S. (2015) [38]

The landmark project that tried to replicate 100 psychology studies and found effects half the size of the originals on average. Essential context for why this book grades its sources, and for why single surprising findings deserve patience.

Evidence-basedNews: Crossref and Retraction Watch

Crossref (2023) [39]

The Retraction Watch database, now open and maintained with Crossref, records papers that have been retracted. Check it before you rely on a famous study; this book refuses to cite retracted work, and the database is how anyone can check.

Evidence-basedCalling Bullshit

Bergstrom, C. T., & West, J. D. (2020) [40]

A university course, with free videos and readings, and a book, on seeing through misleading numbers, charts and claims. The most enjoyable way to build the reflexes this book tries to model. Suits anyone; no statistics background needed.

Finding papers legally. Many papers are free. Search for the title in OpenAlex, the free catalogue our researchers used to read the abstracts behind this book [41]; look for an open-access version on the author’s university page; or ask the author, who is usually delighted that someone wants to read it. Public and university libraries can often get the rest for you.

What is still missing#

Two kinds of gap remain, and we would rather name them than hide them.

The first is regional. The book’s research base is mostly American and Western European, and our official pointers cover the US, the UK, the EU and India because those are the regions we could verify. If you live elsewhere, the shape of the advice in this appendix still holds (start with official sources, prefer evidence to anecdote, get qualified help for law, health and money), but the specific doors are different, and you will need to find your own.

The second is time. Everything here was checked in October 2026, and some of it will have moved by the time you read it. If you find a broken link or a better resource, the book’s repository welcomes corrections: that is one advantage of a book that lives on the web.

  34. Barrick, M. R., & Mount, M. K. (1991). The Big Five Personality Dimensions and Job Performance: A Meta-Analysis. Personnel Psychology, 44(1), 1–26. doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.1991.tb00688.x ↩
