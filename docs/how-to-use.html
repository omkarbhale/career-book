You picked up a book about jobs. Odds are something brought you here: a rejection that made no sense, a job you have outgrown, a first week somewhere new, or a colleague you cannot work out. Good. A book is far easier to learn from when you already have a question it can answer.

This page takes five minutes. It tells you who the book is for, how it is built, and how to get the most out of it, including what every box and badge on these pages means.

Who this book is for#

Read on if most of these are true:

  • You are early or in the middle of your career, or you are about to start one.
  • You want to get a job, change jobs, get better at the job you have, or make sure your good work counts.
  • You would rather be told what the evidence says than be told a confident story.
  • You can enjoy a joke and a cartoon without suspecting they make the content less serious.

You may want a different book if:

  • you need legal advice about a specific dispute (this book points at laws, but it is not a lawyer);
  • you want a reference manual of every interview question ever asked;
  • you are looking for tricks to appear to be someone you are not. We will make the case that this is a bad trade, and we are not subtle about it.

The examples lean towards technology jobs, because that is where our main character works, but almost nothing here depends on writing code. Where practice differs by country (notice periods, pay-transparency laws, what an employer may ask), you will find a Field Note for the regions we could verify.

The idea in one paragraph#

Working life is three different games. In the first, strangers judge a short, edited trailer of you; in the second, colleagues watch the whole film, every day, for years; in the third, the film gets reviewed, and the review decides what you do next. The skills differ, so the book teaches them separately: Part I is the snapshot game of getting or switching a job, Part II is the long game of working with people, and Part III is the career game of being good at the work and having it count. The thread through all three is one person: you. The trailer should be an honest cut of the film, and the film is what earns your next snapshot. Chapter 1 makes that case properly.

How the book is built to teach#

This is not a textbook, but it borrows from the research on how people learn from words and pictures. A few of the design choices, and why:

  • It talks to you. Rewriting instructional text in a conversational, second-person style has improved both recall and the ability to apply what was learned, though in studies longer than about 35 minutes the benefit shrank to almost nothing [1]. So the book talks with you, and it keeps sections short.
  • Pictures carry ideas, not decoration. Every figure explains something, and its labels sit on the picture rather than in a key you have to match up. In lab studies, placing words next to the parts of a picture they describe, and signalling the structure of an explanation, both helped people understand it [2].
  • Jokes have to earn their place. Interesting but irrelevant material (“seductive details”) tends to hurt learning a little [3], and cutting it helped in Mayer and Moreno’s experiments [2]. So the humour here is meant to make a point stick, not to fill a page. If a joke ever gets in the way, that is our failure, not yours.
  • You will be asked to do things. In a major review of study techniques, testing yourself earned the top rating for usefulness, while rereading and highlighting earned the bottom one [4]. That is what the exercises are for.
  • One idea at a time. Working memory holds only a handful of chunks at once (about four, on the best current estimate) [5], so each section tries to land one idea before starting the next.

And one thing we avoid: brain myths. “Learning styles” and “left-brain versus right-brain learners” are popular, and a survey of teachers interested in neuroscience found that they believed about half of such myths [6]. You will not find them here.

How to read the evidence#

Every factual claim in this book points at a numbered source. Click a number and you land in the chapter’s reference list, where every source carries one of four grades:

GradeWhat it means here
StrongMeta-analyses, large randomised or natural experiments, official statistics, the text of a law. Hard to overturn.
ModerateA single well-designed study, a careful review, a large survey with a clear method. Probably right, could shift.
WeakSmall or unreplicated studies, vendor surveys, practitioner frameworks. A hint, not a finding.
Myth or unsupportedContradicted by better evidence, retracted, or with no traceable source. Cited only to show where a myth came from.

Advice that rests on experience rather than research carries a craft label, and our own arguments carry our view. If we could not verify something, we either left it out or labelled it unverified. You will see that last label rarely, because the rule is to cut first.

The full list of sources, with their grades and the chapters that use them, is on the References page.

A few honest limits#

Some topics are deliberately left to others (managing people in depth, founding a company, personal finance, legal specifics, immigration), and “Left Out, on Purpose” says where to go for each. Much of the research on hiring and on behaviour at work comes from the United States and Western Europe. Where that matters, we say so. The rules of the hiring game also change: laws on pay transparency, non-compete clauses and notice periods were moving even while this book was being written, so treat any legal detail as a pointer to check, not as advice.

The style of this book owes a debt to the learner-centred approach Kathy Sierra and Bert Bates championed in technical books, and to Richard Mayer’s research on learning from words and pictures. Every word and every picture here is original.

  1. Ginns, P., Martin, A. J., & Marsh, H. W. (2013). Designing instructional text in a conversational style: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 25(4), 445–472. eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036785 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Conversational-style versions of instructional text improved retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.54); the clearest boundary condition was time, with small, non-significant effects in studies longer than 35 minutes.

    Caveats: Abstract-level reading; most studies are short lab lessons.

  2. Mayer, R. E., & Moreno, R. (2003). Nine ways to reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 43–52. doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_6 (copy read) ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence synthesis of the authors' experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Learners understand an explanation better when interesting but extraneous material is cut (coherence effect, median effect size .90 over 5 experiments), when cues signal the structure (.74), and when printed words sit next to the parts of the graphic they describe (.48).

    Caveats: Mostly short lessons with university students in one lab; later meta-analyses find smaller effects for some principles.

  3. Cheng, C., Wu, Y., Wang, R., & Wang, Z. (2026). Seductive details, cognitive load, and learning outcomes: A multi-level meta-analysis and MASEM. Educational Psychology Review. eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1510171 ↩

    Strong evidence multi-level meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Interesting but irrelevant additions ("seductive details") have a small negative effect on learning (g = -0.16 over 177 effect sizes from 50 studies), mainly by adding extraneous cognitive load.

    Caveats: Small average effect with moderators (sample size, language, learning environment).
