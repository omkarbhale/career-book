INT. CORVID SYSTEMS, A BORROWED MEETING ROOM — FRIDAY, 9:20 AM

Tomás keeps his promise: it is Friday, and it is the morning.

Tomás We’d like to offer you the backend developer role. I’ll have it sent over in writing today, but I wanted to tell you myself. The team liked you, and I liked the bug you showed us.

Dev says thank you three times, which is two more than she planned. Tomás runs through the outline: a base salary at the bottom of the range in the job ad, a small annual bonus, some share options, a start date in five weeks.

Tomás We’d love an answer by Monday, if you can.

The old Dev would have said yes before he finished the sentence. She has, after all, been rejected 46 times. Instead she hears herself say:

Dev I’m really pleased, and I’d like to read it properly. Could I come back to you by Wednesday?
Tomás Wednesday’s fine.

She sits in the borrowed room for a full minute after he hangs up. Then she opens the spreadsheet and adds a tab called Offers. It has one row. That is one more than it has ever had.

The snapshot game ends where most job-search advice stops: at “you got the offer”. But the next few weeks decide a surprising amount: how much you are paid for years, what you have agreed to without noticing, how you leave the people you are leaving, and what they remember about you. This chapter is about reading an offer, negotiating it without drama, the checks and rules that come with it, and resigning well. It is the last chapter of Part I, and it ends at the door of Part II.

Pause

Before you read on: should Dev negotiate at all? She wants the job badly, she has an offer in hand after 46 rejections, and the number is within the range she hoped for. Write down yes or no, and your reason.

What’s in an offer#

The number people talk about is base salary. It is the most visible part of an offer and rarely the whole of it.

Reading an offer properly means pricing each layer craft:

  • Base salary. The fixed part. Most raises, bonuses and future offers are calculated from it, so a little more here compounds.
  • Bonus. Ask what has actually been paid in recent years, not the maximum. A “target” bonus is a hope, not a promise.
  • Equity. Shares or options in the company. Ask for the details in writing: how many units, the strike price for options, the vesting schedule, and what happens to them if you leave. A common schedule for options is four years with a one-year “cliff”, meaning nothing vests until your first anniversary and a quarter vests then, though that description comes from a company that sells equity financing, and schedules vary [1 weak]. In a private company, equity can be worth a lot, or nothing, and you usually cannot sell it until something happens to the company. Value it accordingly. In the US, how options are taxed depends on their type (statutory or nonstatutory) and on when you exercise them [2]; tax rules elsewhere differ, and this book does not cover them.
  • Benefits. Health insurance, retirement contributions, leave, learning budgets. These can be worth a large share of base pay in some countries and almost nothing in others.
  • Not on paper. The manager, the work, what you will learn, flexibility about where and when you work, the notice period you are signing up to, and the start date. Some of the most important parts of an offer are the ones you heard about in Chapter 7, when you interviewed them back.
Field NoteCTC versus in-hand pay

India

Offers in India are usually quoted as CTC, “cost to company”: the total the employer spends on you, which differs from the take-home pay that reaches your account [3 weak]. CTC can include allowances, bonuses, health insurance, gratuity and the employer’s provident-fund contribution, so two offers with the same CTC can mean quite different monthly pay. Ask for the breakdown and the expected in-hand figure. India’s four Labour Codes, which came into force on 21 November 2025, changed parts of how wages are defined for these calculations, and rules were still being notified at the time of writing [4], so check the current position before comparing offers.

Reveal one way to play it

On guaranteed money, the gap is narrower than it looks. Offer A’s base is 8 higher, but its retirement contribution is 2.7 against B’s 6.6, which closes about half the gap. Its bonus, on last year’s evidence, is worth about 4.5, not the 18 its maximum suggests. So the honest comparison is roughly 97 (A, with a realistic bonus) against 89 (B), plus a lottery ticket, against two things not in the money: a three-month notice period that will slow down your next move, and a manager signal that matters more than any line in the table. Many people would take B and sleep well. The point is not the answer; it is that the “8 more” at the top of the letter was never the real comparison.

What to ask before deciding: Offer A’s bonus history over several years, the equity details in writing, and a second conversation with the distracted manager.

Time to decide#

Tomás asked for an answer by Monday, and Dev asked for Wednesday. That small exchange is worth a closer look, because deadlines on offers are a known problem.

Economists who study labour markets call an offer that must be accepted almost immediately an exploding offer. Across a number of real markets, those where deals were struck far in advance tended to allow exploding offers and forbid going back on an acceptance; in laboratory experiments, that combination produced inefficient early matching, and relaxing either rule let people match later, with better information and better results [5]. In plain terms: pressure to decide fast means deciding with less information, and leads to worse matches on average.

For a candidate, the practical move is simple and normal: ask for a specific, reasonable amount of time, with a reason craft. “I’m excited about this. I’d like to read the details properly and finish one other conversation; can I give you a firm answer by Wednesday?” Most employers will agree. One who refuses a few days for a decision that will shape years of your life is telling you something about how decisions get made there, as “Interview them back” in Chapter 7 warned.

Should you negotiate?#

Now the Pause. Should Dev negotiate?

Start with what people actually do. In a representative US survey, only about a third of workers said they had bargained over pay before accepting their current job, while about a third had known the precise pay at their first meeting with the employer [6]. About 40% could have stayed in their previous job when they accepted, which put them in a stronger bargaining position than people who were out of work [6]. After adjusting for differences between the groups, the authors found that wages were higher among those who bargained [6].

How much is it worth? Evidence on that is thinner. In one survey of 149 employees, those who negotiated rather than accepting the first offer reported annual pay about US$5,000 higher [7 weak]. That is a small, self-reported sample, read through secondary coverage, and people who choose to negotiate may differ from those who do not, so treat the figure as a hint. But the direction is consistent: asking, done reasonably, tends to pay.

And there is a quiet reason it matters more than it looks. Your starting salary is the base for every percentage raise that follows, and often the anchor for your next employer’s offer too. A modest difference at the start can echo for years our view.

So the short answer to the Pause is: usually yes, once, calmly, with evidence. The long answer has two important exceptions, which come after the mechanics.

How an anchor pulls#

The most robust finding in negotiation research is about first numbers. In a set of experiments, whichever side made the first offer, buyer or seller, tended to get the better outcome, and first offers strongly predicted where the final price landed [8]. The first number becomes an anchor that the rest of the conversation adjusts from, usually not far enough.

The same experiments found the antidote. When the person receiving the first offer deliberately focused on their own target, or on the other side’s alternatives and limits, the anchoring advantage disappeared [8].

Those were laboratory negotiations between students over business deals, not salary conversations with a future manager you will see every day, where the relationship matters more our view. But they give two practical rules our view:

  1. If you know the market, a well-justified number near the top of it is a fine first move. Increasingly, you will know the market, because the employer has to tell you (see the Field Note on pay transparency below).
  2. If they anchor first, which is usual, write down your target and your alternatives before you respond. What would you need to be pleased? What happens if you walk away: do you stay where you are, take another offer, keep searching? That is exactly the focus that cancelled the anchor in the experiments.
Myth Check“Never name a number first”Partly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

Overstated. In laboratory negotiations, whoever made the first offer usually did better, because first offers anchor the outcome [8]. The advice has a sensible core when you have no idea what the role pays: a number pulled from thin air can anchor you low. But with good market information, opening with a well-justified number can help, and when the other side opens first, focusing on your own target and alternatives removes most of their advantage [8]. The grade is for the lab evidence; salary talks have a relationship the experiments did not.

In some places, you no longer have to guess the market, because the law makes employers tell you.

Field NotePay transparency: when they have to tell you

USEUUKIndia

United States. As of May 2026, 13 states and the District of Columbia had pay-transparency laws in effect requiring salary ranges in job postings, including California, Colorado, Illinois, New York and Washington [9]. New York State, for example, requires employers with four or more employees to list a compensation range for advertised jobs, promotions and transfers [10]. European Union. By late September 2026 only a few had done so fully, and Germany and France had not [13], so whether the rights apply to you yet depends on your country.

UK and India. We did not find a law requiring salary ranges in job postings in either, and did not verify one.

Where a range is posted, it is your anchor information: place yourself within it using your evidence.

Transparency laws are popular, and a popular belief has grown up around them.

Myth Check“Pay transparency raises everyone’s pay”Partly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

It depends on the kind of transparency. A major study of US state laws protecting workers’ right to discuss pay with colleagues found that wages fell by about 2% after the laws took effect, because transparency weakened individual bargaining; the effect was smaller where workers had little individual bargaining power to begin with [14]. But a newer study of the state laws that require salary information in job postings found wages rose by 1.3% to 3.6%, including for people already in their jobs, which its authors read as employers competing more openly for workers [15]. That study is a working paper, not yet peer-reviewed, so the grade is moderate. Our reading is that posted ranges are more likely to help job seekers than to hurt them our view; a guaranteed pay rise for everyone is more than either study shows.

Negotiating is not equally free#

Here are the two exceptions promised above, and the book would be dishonest without them.

A common piece of advice says that negotiating is costless: “the worst they can say is no”. For some people, the evidence says otherwise.

In a series of experiments, evaluators penalised women more than men for initiating salary negotiations; the negative effect on evaluators’ willingness to work with the candidate was 5.5 times greater when the candidate was female [16]. In the video study, male evaluators penalised only the women who negotiated, while female evaluators penalised every negotiator [16]. The paper’s subtitle sums up its reading: “Sometimes it does hurt to ask” [16]. If asking carries a real social cost for some people, reluctance to ask is not a lack of confidence; it is a reasonable response our view.

Race shows a similar pattern. In three studies, evaluators higher in racial bias expected Black job seekers to negotiate less, and when Black candidates did negotiate, those evaluators awarded them lower starting salaries, apparently because they were more resistant to making concessions to them [17].

And yet the claim that women simply “don’t negotiate” does not hold up either. In a field experiment with 2,422 people answering real job ads in nine US cities, men were more likely than women to negotiate when the ad said nothing about pay; when the ad stated that the wage was negotiable, the difference disappeared, with 21.2% of women and 21.4% of men negotiating [18]. Overall, 21% negotiated when the ad said “negotiable” and 7% when it did not [18]. The gap lived in the ambiguity.

Myth Check“Women don’t negotiate”Partly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

Overstated. When job ads made clear that pay was negotiable, women and men negotiated at the same rate, 21.2% and 21.4% [18]. The gap appeared when the rules were unclear, and evaluators have been found to penalise women more than men for asking [16], which makes caution a reasonable choice rather than a failing. The fix is clearer rules, not braver women.

The other half of the folk wisdom, that asking costs nothing, fares worse.

Myth Check“Negotiating is always free; the worst they can say is no”Busted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

False for some candidates. Evaluators penalised women more than men for initiating salary negotiations [16], and more racially biased evaluators gave Black candidates who negotiated lower starting salaries [17]. For many people, a polite, well-founded request carries little risk, but “free for everyone” is not what the evidence shows.

What does this mean for you? Not that some people should never negotiate; the field experiment shows how much ambiguity, rather than the people involved, drives the gap. Several practical moves follow from the evidence, though none of them has itself been tested as a remedy craft:

  • Remove the ambiguity. Ask directly, early: “Is there flexibility on the base salary?” It turns a test of nerve into a normal question with a normal answer.
  • Anchor on evidence, not on wanting. “The posted range is A to B; given my experience with X and Y, I was hoping for nearer the middle” asks the employer to agree with facts, not with you.
  • Frame it as solving a shared problem. “I’m excited about the role, and I want to say yes; is there anything we can do on base?” keeps the relationship in the foreground, which is exactly where the backlash research says the risk lies.

And if you are on the hiring side, the clearest lesson is for you: say in the ad and the offer what is negotiable, and post the range. It costs nothing and removes a gap that has nothing to do with talent our view.

The written offer matches what Tomás said. She says the number. Then she stops talking, which is the hardest part.

On Tuesday afternoon the recruiter calls back. Lanternfish has met her about two-thirds of the way. Dev thanks her, says yes, and asks for the updated letter in writing.

That is what a negotiation usually looks like: one ask, a reason, a number, a pause, and a partial yes. Not drama; a conversation. Dev did not threaten to walk away, did not invent a competing offer and did not apologise. She used the range as her information, her evidence as her reason and her written-down target and alternatives as her counterweight to the anchor. She also took the partial yes, because she had decided in advance what she would be pleased with. She will need the same habits again: a promotion comes with its own conversation about pay, and Chapter 19 shows how the rules from this chapter carry over.

Checks, references and probation#

Between “yes” and the first day, many employers check things: your identity, your right to work, your past employment, sometimes your criminal record, and often your references. Most of this is routine. A little of it is worth understanding, and all of it is the moment when the trailer you cut in Chapter 5 meets someone who checks it.

References are a weak but real signal. In the classic 1998 summary of selection methods, reference checks predicted job performance with a validity of .26, well below structured interviews and work samples [19]. Their value to you is mostly defensive: choose referees who have seen your work, ask them in advance, and tell them what the role is so they can talk about the relevant parts of your film craft. This is the long game paying out in the snapshot game, exactly as “The loop” in Chapter 1 promised.

Field NoteBackground checks

USUK

United States. If an employer uses a background-check company, it must ask for your written permission first. If it might reject you because of something in the report, it must give you a copy of the report and a notice of your rights before deciding, and it must treat applicants equally regardless of protected characteristics [20]. Some jurisdictions, including New Jersey and New York City, have “ban-the-box” laws that stop employers asking about criminal records on applications. In one large field experiment in New Jersey and New York City, employers that asked about records called back applicants without a record 63% more often than applicants with one; after the question was removed, the gap in callbacks between white and Black applicants at those employers grew from about 7% to about 45%, suggesting that some employers assumed a record based on race once they could no longer ask [21].

England and Wales. Criminal-record checks are run by the Disclosure and Barring Service, at basic, standard and enhanced levels; a basic check shows unspent convictions and conditional cautions, and the certificate is issued to you [22]. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own systems.

India and the EU. Third-party background verification is widely described as common in Indian IT and services, but we did not verify its prevalence or the rules that govern it, and we did not verify EU rules.

Probation is a trial period at the start of a job, often with shorter notice on both sides. In Germany, for example, a probation period can last up to six months, with two weeks’ notice during it [23]. Elsewhere it is set by contract or custom; read yours. Probation is, in effect, the first few months of the long game played with the snapshot game’s rules: Chapter 11 is about how to use those months well.

Resigning#

At last, the part people dread. You have a signed offer. Now you have to tell the people you are leaving.

Trapdoor

Resigning before the offer is solid. A verbal offer, an email saying “we’d love to have you”, or a letter that is “subject to checks” is not yet a job. Wait until you have the final written offer, signed, and any conditions (references, background checks, right-to-work documents) have cleared, before you resign craft. The awkwardness of a week’s delay is nothing next to the cost of resigning into a withdrawn offer.

How you resign is not just etiquette. A four-study research programme on resignations found that the way people resign is shaped by how they have been treated by their employer and their boss, and that resignation style affects how managers react emotionally [24]. A report of that research gives the styles names, and their shares among the people studied [25]:

StyleWhat it looks likeShare
By the booka face-to-face meeting with your manager, a standard notice period, a reason31%
Perfunctorythe same, but shorter and with no reason given23.5%
Avoidanttelling colleagues, mentors or HR, rather than your boss12.7%
Grateful goodbyeexpressing thanks and offering to help with the handover10%
Bridge burningtrying to harm the organisation or colleagues on the way out8.6%
In the looptelling your manager you were thinking of leaving before resigning7.9%
Impulsivewalking out without notice6.3%

Managers saw by-the-book, grateful-goodbye and in-the-loop resignations as positive, and avoidant, bridge-burning and impulsive ones as negative; even a perfunctory resignation, with no reason given, landed badly [25]. The researchers’ own reading was that harmful resignations often respond to unfair treatment or disrespectful managers, rather than to bad character [25].

The practical upshot: tell your manager first, in person or on a call; give your notice; give a short, true reason; and thank them for something specific craft. You do not owe anyone a list of grievances, and the resignation meeting is the wrong place for one. If you have feedback that might help the people staying, an exit interview or a later conversation is a better place.

Your Move 9.2Write the resignation, twice

Dev has to resign from Corvid. Her manager there once told her “maybe next year” when she asked about moving into development, and she is still a little annoyed about it.

Write two short resignation messages to follow up her conversation, of four or five sentences each. Make the first the one she is tempted to send at 11 pm. Make the second the one she sends at 9 am. Then decide: which one will Corvid remember in five years, and what will it remember about her?

Reveal one way to play it

The 11 pm version tends to mention “maybe next year”, lists what Corvid failed to do and ends with something cool like “Best of luck”. In the language of the resignation research, it drifts from perfunctory towards bridge burning, the styles managers react to most badly [25].

A 9 am version might be:

Following our conversation this morning, I’m writing to confirm my resignation. My last day will be [date], in line with my notice period. I’ve accepted a backend developer role, which is the move into development I’ve been hoping for. Thank you for three good years and for trusting me with the regression suite; I’ll make sure it’s fully handed over, with documentation, before I go. I’d be glad to stay in touch.

It is short and true. It gives the real reason without an accusation, thanks the manager for something specific, and promises a handover. It is also the one that will be remembered, because in five years, Corvid people will be your references, your referrers and possibly your colleagues again. Leaving is the last move of one repeated game and the first move of the next (“The long game” in Chapter 1).

The counter-offer#

Dev’s manager at Corvid has one more move to make.

INT. CORVID SYSTEMS, MANAGER'S OFFICE — WEDNESDAY, 4:00 PM

Dev’s manager takes the news well, too well. Two hours later, Dev is asked back in.

We don’t want to lose you. We can match the money, and I’ve spoken to the head of delivery: there’s a real chance of a move into development next year.

That evening, Dev calls Meera. Meera’s mug, visible over the video call, says IT DEPENDS.

Dev They matched the money. And they said I could move into development next year.
Meera Did they say that last year?
Dev They said “maybe next year” last year.
Meera So what’s changed is the money and the word “real”. Why did you start looking?
Dev Because I want to build things, and nobody here would let me.
Meera Does the counter-offer fix that?
Dev It promises to fix it. Next year.
Meera Then you know what the mug says. It depends on whether you believe the promise more than you believe the last three years.

A counter-offer is a strange moment: the people who would not give you what you wanted are suddenly offering it, because you are leaving. Popular advice usually answers the question for you, with a statistic: “80% of people who accept a counter-offer are gone within six months.” Before you lean on that number, look at where it comes from.

Myth Check“80% of people who accept a counter-offer leave within six months”Busted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Weak evidence

Unsupported. Two career sites that tried to trace the statistic found no study behind it; the figure circulates as anything from 48% to 93%, depending on who repeats it [26; 27]. Both mention a figure of roughly 50% leaving within a year, attributed to a commercial research firm, which we could not verify. As one of them points out, the most enthusiastic repeaters of the 80% figure are recruiters, who are paid when you move [27]. The debunking comes from commercial career sites, so its grade is weak; but nobody, including us, has found a study that supports the claim.

Without the statistic, you have to think, which is better anyway. Two questions do most of the work craft.

Why did it take a resignation? If the raise or the role was possible, it was possible last month. A counter-offer can be a sincere correction by a manager who did not realise what they were about to lose. It can also be a way to buy time while they find your replacement. You know your manager better than any statistic does.

Are your reasons for leaving fixed by what is on offer? Recall from “Why people actually leave” in Chapter 2 that among US workers who quit a job in 2021, the most common reasons were low pay and no opportunities for advancement (63% each) and feeling disrespected at work (57%) [28]. If your reason was pay, a counter-offer might genuinely solve it. If it was advancement, a promise about next year is worth exactly as much as the last promise. If it was respect, money rarely buys it back our view.

The boomerang study from “Boomerangs, and the door you leave through” in Chapter 2 is worth recalling here too. In one large organisation, rehired “boomerang” managers performed about as well as other hires in their first year but improved less over time, and were more likely to leave again, often for the same reasons as the first time [29]. That study was about returning rather than staying, but the lesson transfers our view: when the original reasons for leaving have not changed, they tend to reassert themselves.

Dev declines the counter-offer, politely and in writing, and thanks her manager for it, because it was a compliment.

Leaving well#

Your notice period is the last scene of one film, and people remember endings. It is also governed by rules that differ sharply by country.

The details behind that figure: in the US, employment is “at will” by default, meaning either side can usually end it at any time and for almost any reason, with exceptions that vary by state [30]; notice there is a matter of custom and contract. In Great Britain, you must give at least a week’s notice once you have been in a job for a month, or more if your contract says so, and your employer may put you on “garden leave”, asking you to stay away from work during your notice period [31]. In Germany the basic notice period is four weeks, to the 15th or the end of a calendar month, and two weeks during a probation period of up to six months [23]. In India notice is set mainly by contract, usually one to two months for non-managerial employees and three to six months for senior roles, and there is little direct case law on very long notice periods [32]; under the Labour Codes, final dues are payable within two days of resignation [4]. We did not verify final-pay rules for the US, where they are set by state law.

Non-compete clauses, which try to stop you working for a competitor after you leave, vary even more. Chapter 2 covers them in a Field Note; the short version is that they are generally void in India [33] and in California [34], grounds for your employer to take you to court in the UK [31], and governed by state law in the rest of the US, since the Federal Trade Commission’s rule banning them lost in court and the agency dropped its appeal in September 2025 [35]. If your new job is anywhere near your old one, read the clause, and if it worries you, get local advice.

Within those rules, leaving well is mostly a matter of craft craft:

  • Hand over as if you were staying. Write down what you know that nobody else does. Document the systems, the passwords’ locations (not the passwords), the clients’ quirks, the jobs that run at 3 am. Introduce your replacement, or your manager, to the people you work with.
  • Finish what you can, and say clearly what you cannot. A half-done project with a clear note is better than a “nearly finished” one nobody understands.
  • Do not coast, and do not gossip about the new place. Your colleagues are still in the film you are leaving.
  • Say goodbye properly. A short, specific thank-you to the people who helped you is cheap, true and remembered.

Why bother, when you are leaving anyway? Because, as “The long game” in Chapter 1 put it, at work people remember. The colleagues you leave become your middle-ring ties (“Weak ties, strong ties” in Chapter 6), your references, your referrers and, sometimes, your colleagues again. Leaving well is not the end of the long game; it is the move that keeps it going. Years from now, Dev will face the stay-or-go decision again, from strength, in Chapter 22, and some of the people she is leaving will be part of how it goes.

A few last questions come up at this stage, often late at night.

Ask Anyway

QShould I tell my current employer that I’m interviewing?

AUsually not until you have a signed offer, unless you have a manager you trust and a reason, such as wanting them to try to keep you. The research on resignation styles found that managers react well to people who keep them “in the loop” [25], but that is about how you resign, not about announcing a search that may come to nothing craft.

QCan an employer withdraw an offer because I tried to negotiate?

AIt can happen, and the backlash research shows why some evaluators react badly to asking [16; 17]. In practice it is rare when the request is polite, specific and grounded in evidence craft. An employer who withdraws an offer over a courteous question has told you something important cheaply.

QWhat if I’ve already accepted, and a better offer arrives?

AWhatever the legal position where you are, which depends on what you signed, reneging is costly to your reputation, and the people you let down will remember. If it happens, be honest, quick and apologetic, and do not do it twice our view.

QDo I have to give a reason for leaving?

AYour contract may not require one. Even so, resignations with no reason given tend to land badly with managers [25], and a short, true reason (“I’ve accepted a role that moves me into development”) costs you nothing.

Dev I’m leaving you with a story bank, a laminated wall and a referral the moment Lanternfish has a role.
Bea I’ll hold you to that.
Dev You should.

Dev takes her mustard hoodie off the back of her chair, where it has hung for three years, and logs out for the last time.

Bea’s search has stalled for now: a final round at the payments company ended in a hiring freeze. She will stay at Corvid, and the book will come back to her. Dev’s snapshot game is over. Her long game is about to begin, at a company where she knows one person from a databases course, one manager who hates Friday afternoons, and nothing else at all. Part II starts on her first morning there, when the coffee machine breaks and everybody’s response tells her something about them. Chapter 10 is about the model under the whole long game: trust, reciprocity and why good defaults beat performances.

Rewind
  • An offer is more than base pay: price the bonus as paid, the equity as uncertain, the benefits, and what is not on paper. In India, ask how much of the CTC lands in your account.
  • Ask for time to decide; exploding offers favour whoever applies the pressure. A few days is a normal request.
  • Only about a third of US workers bargain, and those who do tend to earn more. Negotiate once, calmly, with evidence: a target and alternatives written down first, a number from the range, a reason, a pause.
  • First numbers anchor outcomes; focusing on your own target and alternatives cancels most of the pull. “Never name a number first” is overstated.
  • Negotiating is not equally free: evaluators have penalised women and Black candidates for asking. Ambiguity drives the gender gap, so ask whether pay is negotiable, and if you hire, say so in the ad.
  • Pay-transparency laws are spreading in the US and EU. Laws that let colleagues discuss pay were followed by slightly lower wages, while early evidence on laws requiring salary ranges in job ads points to slightly higher ones.
  • Resign after the offer is final: tell your manager first, give notice and a short, true reason, thank them, and hand over properly. The “80% of counter-offer accepters leave” statistic has no source; ask whether the offer fixes the reasons you started looking.
