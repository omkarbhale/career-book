INT. DEV'S FLAT — SUNDAY, 3 PM

The email arrived on Friday: Annual review cycle: self-assessments due by end of next week. The form has three boxes and a drop-down. What did you accomplish this year? How did you work with others? What do you want to develop next? The drop-down asks Dev to rate herself from 1, Not yet meeting expectations, to 5, Outstanding.

She starts with the first box and types from memory.

This year I worked on the rates service and the reconciliation project. I helped the team with testing and onboarding. I improved my communication.

She reads it back. Every word is true. It is also, almost exactly, the résumé she had at Corvid: a list of duties with her name on top. A year of work, and from memory it comes out as three sentences that anyone on her squad could have written.

So she opens the spreadsheet, scrolls past Game 1, Game 2 and Game 3, and clicks on Wins.

Twenty-eight lines. The Northwind recovery and the early-warning habit that came after it. The invoice-sync race condition, with a link to Marcus’s correction in the demo channel. The duplicate-shipment defect she caught before the reconciliation release. And between them, gaps: a run of seven empty weeks in the spring, five more in the autumn, the weeks when her calendar belonged to other people’s interviews and a coffee machine.

She duplicates the tab and renames the copy Self-review. Then she texts Bea, who has survived three review cycles at Corvid.

Dev How do you write a self-review?
Bea At Corvid? You don’t, really. There’s a curve. Two “exceeds” per team, and they go round like the washing-up rota.
Dev That can’t be true.
Bea It’s not officially true.

Of all the moments in the career game, this is the most ordinary and, for most people, the most consequential. Once or twice a year, someone looks back at months of your work and turns it into a paragraph and a number. The number often decides your pay rise, feeds your next promotion and follows you into the next review. And as “The career game” in Chapter 1 warned, it says a surprising amount about the person who gave it.

This chapter opens the machine up. How a review is actually made; how much of a rating is about you and how much is about the rater, the system and the room; where bias gets in; and the parts you can genuinely influence, which are fewer than you would like and more than you might fear. It ends with Dev’s first review, a rating she did not expect, and what she did about it.

Pause

Before you read on, a guess. When researchers split the variation in performance ratings into its sources, what share do you think reflects the actual performance of the person being rated? Write down a percentage. The answer is a few pages away.

How a review is made#

Review systems differ a great deal between organisations, and some companies have abandoned formal ratings altogether. But most formal systems run some version of the same cycle, and it is worth knowing its parts before you try to influence any of them craft.

It starts, in theory, with expectations: at the beginning of the year, you and your manager agree what good looks like for your role and your work. Then comes the year itself, during which the evidence of your performance piles up, mostly in people’s memories. Near the end, you write a self-assessment. Your manager writes a draft, often with input from others: at Lanternfish, Kai, as Dev’s squad lead, writes a section (“When your friend becomes your manager” in Chapter 15). Then the drafts go to calibration, a meeting where managers compare their proposed ratings across teams and adjust them. Finally there is the conversation, in which you hear the result, and behind it, pay and promotion decisions that use it.

That last link is often direct. At Microsoft until 2013, for example, each employee got a single rating from 1 to 5, built from their manager’s assessment and a calibration process, and that one number drove merit pay, bonuses and stock awards [1]. Calibration meetings are widespread, though they take many forms [2]. And companies that announce they have “got rid of ratings” rarely get rid of evaluation: as one side of a published expert debate put it, performance is always evaluated in some manner, and the alternatives to ratings may be worse [3]. A company with no ratings still decides who gets the raise. You just cannot see the number.

Look at where the stars are. Three of the five stages are yours to shape, and all three come before the meeting you will never attend. By the time you hear the result, most of what could have changed it has already happened. That is the single most useful thing to know about reviews, and the rest of this chapter keeps coming back to it.

How much of a rating is about you?#

Now the uncomfortable part, and the answer to the pause.

In a study of more than 4,000 managers, each rated by seven people (two bosses, two peers, two direct reports and themselves), the researchers split the variation in the ratings into its sources. The raters’ own idiosyncratic tendencies accounted for 62% and 53% of the variance in the two data sets. The managers’ actual performance, general and dimension-specific together, accounted for 21% and 25% [4]. Random error took 11% and 18%, and whether the rater was a boss, a peer or a report added only a little. (A later reanalysis of two other large data sets, with a different statistical model, found that the rater’s role mattered much more, and general performance even less, than this study suggested [5].) If you guessed something like 50% or more for performance, you are in good company, and the data says otherwise.

Read that carefully, because it is easy to misread in both directions. It does not mean that 62% of your particular rating is about your manager. It is a share of the variation across thousands of ratings: if you want to predict how a given rating will come out, knowing who gave it tells you more than knowing how the person performed. Those were also ratings for development, not ratings tied to pay, and researchers still argue about how much of a rater’s “idiosyncrasy” is noise and how much is a genuinely different, legitimate view. But other evidence points the same way. When two supervisors rate the same person’s overall performance, their ratings agree at an average reliability of about .52 [6]: real agreement, far from perfect.

ReceiptsThe rater is part of the measurement

Scullen et al. 2000 [4]

Strong evidence

What they did. Scullen, Mount and Goff took two large sets of 360-degree ratings (2,350 and 2,142 managers), in which each manager was rated on three performance dimensions by seven people, and used statistical modelling to estimate how much of the variation came from five sources: the manager’s general performance, their performance on the specific dimension, each rater’s idiosyncratic tendencies, the rater’s organisational perspective (boss, peer or report), and random error [4].

What they found. Rater idiosyncrasy was the largest source by far, at 62% and 53%. Performance, general and specific combined, was 21% and 25%, less than half as large. Perspective effects were small, and random error was 11% and 18%.

What it cannot tell you. These were developmental ratings of managers, which carry no pay consequences; ratings that decide money may be cleaner, or more political. The model cannot tell a rater’s bias from a rater’s honest, different view of the same person, and a later reanalysis of other data, modelling the rater’s role differently, found much larger role effects [5]. And it describes populations of ratings, not your review.

Why do raters differ so much? Partly because they combine the same facts in different ways. In one experiment, managers given identical employee profiles clustered into three different “policies”: some weighted task performance most heavily, some weighted counterproductive behaviour most, and some weighted both [7]. One manager’s “solid, reliable” is another’s “fine, but where’s the initiative?”. And partly because ratings drift from performance for reasons that have nothing to do with skill: measurement error, systematic non-performance factors such as how much the rater likes or sees you, and sometimes deliberate distortion, when raters have reasons to rate higher or lower than they believe [8].

Myth CheckYour rating measures your performancePartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

It does measure it, partly: performance is a real and substantial source of variation in ratings. But in two large data sets, the raters’ own tendencies explained more than twice as much variance as the ratees’ actual performance [4]. Two supervisors rating the same person agree at about .52 [6], raters weigh identical facts differently [7], and ratings drift for reasons that have nothing to do with the work, including deliberate distortion [8]. A rating is a measurement taken with a noisy instrument, and the rater is part of the instrument.

Two practical lessons follow, and they pull in slightly different directions our view.

The first is for your self-esteem. One rating from one manager is weak evidence about you. Do not build your self-image on it, in either direction. A disappointing review from a manager you have worked with for eight months is a data point, not a verdict, and the same goes for a glowing one.

The second is for your strategy. Since the rater is part of the instrument, making your work easy for the rater to see accurately is not vanity; it reduces the noise. A manager who has a clear, specific record of what you did has less room to fill the gaps with impressions. That is the snapshot game’s lesson from “Signals, not secrets” in Chapter 1, replayed for an internal audience, and it is most of what you can do about the 62%.

Curves#

Bea’s washing-up rota is a joke, but the system behind it is real. Under forced distribution, also called stack ranking or a forced curve, managers must place fixed shares of their people in each rating band: so many at the top, most in the middle, a few at the bottom, whatever the team actually did.

The case for it is that it stops managers rating everyone “exceeds” and makes them identify weak performers. A simulation gives that case its best hearing: a system that fired a fixed share of the bottom each year could noticeably raise a workforce’s average potential, with most of the gain in the first several years [9]. But the simulation left out morale, collaboration, gaming and legal costs, and experiments have since measured some of them. Raters found forced systems harder to use and less fair, especially when the ratings fed administrative decisions such as pay, and when the team’s performance did not vary much, and that lowered their confidence in giving feedback [10]. In three experiments, forced ratings sped up individual work but slowed teamwork and reduced knowledge sharing [11]. On a creative task, they raised stress without raising performance, and supervisors’ ratings started tracking eloquent writing and strategic gaming more, and actual creativity less [12].

Microsoft, one of the best-known users, ended its curve in November 2013, its HR head announcing “No more curve” and no “pre-determined targeted distribution” [1]. That is a record of what one company did, not proof that curves fail. But the experimental evidence and that decision point the same way.

Underneath every curve sits an assumption: that performance in a team falls on a bell curve, so that the right shares can be fixed in advance.

Myth CheckPerformance follows a bell curvePartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

This one is genuinely contested. Across 198 samples and more than 630,000 people, O’Boyle and Aguinis found that performance followed a heavy-tailed, “power law” distribution, with a few stars far ahead of the rest, not a bell [13]. But their measures were mostly outputs such as publications, awards and wins, and Beck, Beatty and Sackett showed that measures like those tend to produce skewed distributions, while measures that capture whole jobs, over a fixed period, for comparable roles, fit a normal curve better; they concluded that large departures from normality are often an artefact of measurement [14]. So neither “a bell” nor “a few superstars” is settled. What neither side supports is the assumption a forced curve makes: that every team of eight contains exactly the same shares of high and low performers.

You may never work under a formal curve. Many companies use softer “guidelines” for how many people should land in each band, and a calibration meeting can work like a curve even without a rule. The practical point is to know which system you are in, because it changes what a rating means. Under a curve, your rating depends on your teammates as well as on you. A “meets expectations” in a strong team under a forced distribution may be a better result than an “exceeds” somewhere else.

The room you’re not in#

Calibration is where the dashed box in Figure 18.1 lives. Managers bring their proposed ratings to a meeting, compare them across teams, argue about the borderline cases and adjust. It exists because individual managers rate so differently, and there is some evidence that it helps. In two field studies, managers saw calibration as an attempt to improve rating quality; it came with more accountability and more information, and ratings after calibration correlated better with other measures of performance than ratings before it [2].

That is one of very few academic studies of calibration, and it does not test the question you probably care about: what happens to people whose manager argues weakly in the room. So what follows is our view, built from how calibration works rather than from a test of it our view. In calibration, your rating is decided partly by people who mostly know you through your manager’s summary. Two things follow. Your manager needs to be able to say what you did, and why it mattered, in a sentence or two that strangers will accept. And it helps a great deal if someone else in the room has seen your work for themselves.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

Give your manager the sentence. Before review season, and ideally before each quarter’s check-in, send your manager a short note: two or three of your most important results, each in one line, with the evidence attached (a link, a number, a name). Write it so that it would make sense to someone who has never met you. Managers in calibration often have a dozen people to represent and a few minutes for each; the one whose case is already written down is easier to argue for. This is practitioner advice, widely given and not tested as far as we know.

Calibration is a smaller version of the hiring funnel in “The funnel, with real numbers” in Chapter 3: a fixed budget, a panel of people who do not know you well, and noise. And the second need, someone in the room who has seen your work for themselves, is the beginning of a much bigger idea.

What else gets into the number#

A rating carries the same social biases as any other human judgement. The evidence on this is real, and it is also mixed in size, so it is worth being precise about where the gaps show up and where they mostly do not.

Start with the ratings themselves. A meta-analysis of field studies found that women, on average, scored slightly higher than men on measures of job performance (d = −.11) [15]. A recent study of more than 20,000 employees at one multinational company found gender differences in performance and potential ratings that were statistically significant but small to negligible, slightly favouring men, and larger or smaller depending on the gender mix of the department [16]. So the headline rating is not where the largest gaps usually appear.

They appear around it. In the words. In one Fortune 500 technology company, men and women were equally likely to be described as technically able in their reviews, but women were more often called “too aggressive” and men “too soft”, and “taking charge” went with the highest ratings for men, but not for women [17]. In “potential”. The same meta-analysis that found performance ratings slightly favouring women found that ratings of promotion potential favoured men [15], and Chapter 19 shows how much that matters. In turning ratings into money. In personnel data from a large service organisation, women and minority employees received less salary growth than white men with the same performance-evaluation scores [18]. And in three experiments with 445 participants who had managerial experience, presenting a company as explicitly meritocratic made them award a man a larger bonus than an equally performing woman [19]. Believing your system is fair, it seems, can make it less so.

None of this means that every disappointing review is bias, and none of it means that bias is rare. It means the gaps tend to live in the softer, less visible parts of the process: the adjectives, the judgement of “potential”, the step from a rating to a pay rise. Castilla’s own conclusion is that merit systems with little transparency or accountability can increase bias [18]. That is mainly a job for organisations, and for any reader who will one day manage.

For the person being rated, the evidence suggests one partial defence, and it is the same as before: concrete, checkable evidence. Self and supervisor ratings agree more when they rest on non-judgemental performance indicators [20]. “Caught a defect before go-live that was duplicating shipments in a client’s ledger” is harder to reframe than “detail-oriented”. It will not remove bias. It narrows the room bias has to work in.

The self-review#

Back to Dev’s three boxes. The self-review is the stage of the cycle you control most completely, and the one most people treat as a chore. It is worth knowing how good people are at it, because the answer is: not very, and not in the way you might think.

Across 22 meta-analyses, the average correlation between people’s self-evaluations of ability and their objective performance was .29 [21]. At work, self-ratings and supervisor ratings agree at about ρ = .34, and in Western samples self-ratings run more lenient than supervisors’ (d = 0.32) [20]. So people have some insight into their own performance, but, in the authors’ words, “only moderate insight”, and that insight is better for specific, objective and familiar tasks [21], which is a hint about how to write a self-review.

The errors are not evenly spread, either. When women and men described their own performance on a male-typed maths and science task, women rated themselves lower than equally performing men, even when there was no incentive to promote themselves at all [22]. And overconfidence pays, at least at first: in three studies, overconfident people gained status in groups, and a fourth found that their confidence made them look competent [23]. Self-review season rewards confident self-description, and confidence and accuracy are only loosely connected.

You have probably heard the most famous claim about self-assessment, and it deserves a check before it shapes how you read yours.

Myth CheckThe Dunning-Kruger effect: the less skilled you are, the more confident you arePartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

The original 1999 studies found that the lowest-scoring quarter on tests of grammar, logic and humour placed themselves around the 62nd percentile while scoring around the 12th [24]. But much of that pattern is produced by regression to the mean and the general tendency to think we are above average [25], and random numbers reproduce the famous graph [26]. With better methods, one large study found a small effect confined to a tiny fraction of people [27], while a large replication supports a real, modest deficit: low performers are worse at telling whether each of their answers is right [28]. Overall, self-assessment and ability are positively correlated [21]. Low performers overestimate the most, but they are not the most confident people in the room.

So what makes a good self-review? Nothing in the research shows that writing a better one raises your rating; that is our view, not a finding our view. But the evidence above says what a more accurate one looks like, and accuracy is the job. It is the standard “Accuracy, not bragging” in Chapter 5 set for a résumé, applied to an audience that has seen part of the film: accuracy, not modesty, and not inflation.

In practice, that means four things craft:

  • Lead with outcomes, not activity. “Worked on the reconciliation project” is a duty. “Caught a defect before go-live that was duplicating shipments in a client’s ledger, and proposed the flag-off release that let us keep the date” is a result. The three questions from Figure 4.1 work here unchanged: what changed, by how much, who noticed?
  • Make it checkable. Link the ticket, the pull request, the thank-you message, the corrected credit. Specific, objective claims are where self-assessments are most accurate [21], and they are what your manager can carry into calibration.
  • Credit precisely. Say what the team did and what you did. The Marcus correction in “Why decent people over-claim credit” in Chapter 16 is a gift here: a written, public record of who did what.
  • Name the gaps yourself. One honest sentence about what did not go well, and what you learned, does more for your credibility than a fifth success. It also takes the sting out of your manager having to raise it.

That is a story bank again, and it is no accident. The rows of the Wins tab are the raw material, the self-review is the cut, and the stories you rehearsed in “The story bank” in Chapter 4 turn out to be the same kind of object: true, specific, and built to survive a follow-up question.

A strong rewrite might read:

My biggest result was the reconciliation release: before go-live I showed that a test failure the squad had written off as flaky was duplicating shipments in the client’s ledger, and proposed shipping on the date with that shipment type switched off, telling the client two days early. I found and fixed the invoice-sync race condition that had been paging people at 3 am (Marcus reviewed it and added the logging). Notice what changed. Outcomes lead: the defect and the date, not “worked on”. Credit is precise: Marcus is named for his part. The miss is in it, with what changed afterwards, which turns a weakness into evidence of reliability (Chapter 12). The coffee machine is not: filing a ticket was a good deed, but it is not what this review is for, a point Chapter 19 makes at greater length.

For your own: if a sentence needs a number you do not have, use before-and-after or scale, as in “What if there’s no number?” in Chapter 4. If you cannot point to evidence for a claim, either find some or cut it. And check your verbs: if you find yourself writing “helped” and “supported” where “led” and “decided” would be accurate, you are shading your work down, which the research on self-description finds women doing more than equally performing men [22].

Dev’s first instinct, sitting in that chair, was to argue. Her second, which she followed, was to stop talking. Both instincts have reasons, and the evidence on feedback explains why the second one was right.

Hearing it#

Feedback, on average, improves performance. But in a classic meta-analysis, more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse, and feedback helped less the more it drew attention away from the task and towards the self [29]. “Feedback: why a third of it backfires” in Chapter 13 used that finding to explain how to give feedback. Here it explains how a review lands. A rating is about the most self-focused piece of feedback there is: a number, about you, compared with other people, with money attached. It is almost designed to make you think about yourself rather than the work our view.

People are also not neutral seekers of feedback. Research on why people ask for it finds three motives at work: an instrumental one (to get better at something), and two defensive ones, protecting the ego and protecting the image others have of us [30]. In a review conversation, we would expect the defensive motives to wake up first our view. That is what makes two opposite mistakes so tempting: arguing the number on the spot, or going quiet and resentful and asking for no more feedback at all craft. The second gives up the one input you fully control: feedback-seeking has a small direct link to performance, and it fades with tenure unless you keep doing it on purpose [31].

A few habits help you hear a review as information rather than a verdict craft:

  • Separate the number from the content. The number is mostly settled. The content, what your manager and others saw, what they think you should do differently, is where the information is. Ask about the content.
  • Ask the forward question. “What would a four have looked like, this year, in that room?” is more useful than “Why did I get a three?”, because the answer is something you can act on.
  • Take the pause. “Can I come back to you on this?” is a perfectly professional sentence. It moves the conversation from the moment when your defences are highest to a moment when you have read the written review twice.
Trapdoor

Deciding the job is over because of one rating. A disappointing review is a good moment to look at your options, and a bad moment to make the decision. Remember what a single rating is: one noisy measurement, with the rater and the room built in. If the rating is part of a pattern (no growth, no clear path, a manager who will not explain), that is a real push reason, and “Push and pull” in Chapter 2 is where to sort it. If it is one disappointing number after a good year, the cheaper move is usually to fix what fed it, and see what the next cycle says.

Disagreeing well#

Sometimes, after the pause, you still think the review is wrong. Then the question is not whether to say so but how, because disagreeing with your manager has costs. As “Disagreeing without damage” in Chapter 13 showed, in a field study and two experiments managers rated employees who voiced challenging ideas as worse performers than those who voiced supportive ones, partly because the challenges felt less loyal and more threatening [32]. The answer is not silence; it is to disagree in a way that is plainly on the same side.

INT. LANTERNFISH, TOMÁS'S OFFICE — THURSDAY, 10 AM

Dev has brought one page. She has rewritten it four times, mostly to make it shorter.

Dev Three things. Two are facts and one is a request.
Tomás Go on.
Dev First, the review describes the reconciliation release as a squad achievement. It was. But finding the duplicate-shipment defect was mine, and so was the flag-off proposal. I’ve attached the ticket and the thread. Second, the race condition. It says “contributed to”. I found it, reproduced it and wrote the fix; Marcus reviewed it and added the logging. That’s in the correction he posted in the demo channel.
Tomás Both fair. I can change the write-up. I can’t change the rating; calibration’s closed.
Dev I know. That’s the request. I’d like to agree now what a four looks like next year, in writing. And I’d like to send you three lines every quarter, so you’re not defending a list next time.

Tomás writes something in his notebook and underlines it.

Tomás Yes to both. And one more, from me. Kai’s section is about the onboarding and the coffee committee because that’s what Kai saw you do. The next time you do the work that matters, I’d like someone other than me and Kai to see it.

Look at the structure of what Dev did. She separated facts from judgements: the facts (who found the defect, who wrote the fix) she corrected, with evidence; the judgement (a three or a four) she did not relitigate. She knew what could change: once calibration has closed, the rating is often fixed, but the written record, which later reviews and promotion committees will read, usually is not craft. And she turned the disagreement into a plan: expectations for next year, a habit that gives her manager what he needs for the room, and a request that the conversation continue. That is disagreement as “Disagreeing without damage” in Chapter 13 describes it: about the work, aimed at the future, and plainly on the same side.

Tomás, for his part, did something good managers do and many do not: he admitted his share. “I was defending a list, not making a case” is a sentence that will matter a great deal in the next chapter.

Your Move 18.2Rehearse a disagreement about a rating

Here are three ways Dev could have opened on Thursday. Rank them from best to worst and say why. Then write the opening two sentences you would use about a review of your own that you think was wrong.

  1. “I’ve thought about it, and honestly I think the three is unfair. Everyone knows I carried the reconciliation release.”
  2. “Two facts in the write-up are wrong, and I’ve brought the evidence. I’d also like to agree what a four looks like for next year.”
  3. “It’s fine, really. I know calibration is tough. I’ll just try harder next year.”

Best: 2. Then 3. Worst: 1.

  • 2 separates facts from judgement, brings evidence, and points the conversation at something that can still change. It treats the manager as an ally with a problem to solve, which makes them more likely to act like one.
  • 3 is polite and costs nothing today, but it gives up the two things that could change (the written record and next year’s expectations), and “try harder” is not a plan. It also teaches your manager that disappointing you is free.
  • 1 argues the one thing that is usually closed (the number), leads with a judgement (“unfair”) rather than facts, and claims credit too broadly (“carried”), which invites a dispute about the word rather than the work. It is the kind of challenge that the research suggests gets read as disloyal [32].

For your own: start with the strongest fact, not the strongest feeling. Keep it to two or three points. And end with what you would like to happen next.

What you control#

Put the chapter together and you get an honest, slightly sobering list. Much of what decides a rating is out of your hands. But the parts in your hands are concentrated in a few habits, and most of them happen long before the review.

The table is our synthesis of the evidence in this chapter, not a tested programme our view. Two of its rows deserve a word more.

Expectations come first. A review of a century of appraisal research, covering scale formats, rater training, rating sources and more, ends by calling for more attention to performance management, what happens all year, than to the mechanics of the rating [33]. Pulakos and O’Leary argue that review systems fail precisely when they become administrative steps cut off from day-to-day expectation-setting and guidance [34]. You cannot fix your company’s system. But you can fix the version of it that applies to you, by agreeing at the start of the year what good looks like, and checking it at every quarter.

Feedback is yours to ask for. People ask for feedback less as their tenure grows, and more when they have a learning orientation and a good relationship with their manager [31]. Its direct link to performance is small, which argues for asking well rather than constantly: specific questions, about the work, a few times a year, and especially before the cycle closes rather than after it. Used that way, feedback stops being only something a review does to you and becomes a way of getting better at things, which is the subject of Chapter 20.

Your Move 18.3Set expectations for the year

Draft the note you would send your manager at the start of a review year (or the start of next quarter, if the year is under way). It should fit on one screen and contain:

  1. the two or three results that would make this an excellent year, in your words;
  2. for each, how you would both know it had happened;
  3. one question about what the next level of your role looks like;
  4. a proposed rhythm for checking in (for example, three lines from you every quarter).
Reveal one way to play it

Strong notes share three features.

  • Results, not activities. “Ship the carrier integrations” is an activity; “new carriers live within two weeks of signing, without on-call pages” is a result someone can check.
  • The evidence is agreed in advance. If you both know what would count, the review becomes a comparison rather than a debate, and the self-review almost writes itself.
  • It asks about the next level early. That question tells your manager you are thinking about growth, and the answer tells you whether this year’s work is the right kind of evidence for it, which is the subject of Chapter 19.

If your manager never replies, send it anyway, and keep it. A record that you asked what good looks like, in writing, at the start of the year, is useful in any review conversation that follows.

A few things people want to ask about reviews and rarely do.

Ask Anyway

QShould I rate myself high, to anchor my manager?

AWe found no evidence that a high self-rating pulls a manager’s rating up, and some reason to think inflation costs you: self-promotion tends to annoy listeners more than self-promoters expect [35]. Rate yourself where your evidence puts you. If that is a four, say four, and show why.

QMy manager is the problem. What then?

AManagers form relationships of different quality with different people on the same team, and the quality of that relationship is driven mostly by the manager [36]. You cannot fix that alone. You can make sure other people see your work (a skip-level, a partner team, a project lead), keep your evidence in writing, and, if the pattern holds over more than one cycle, treat it as a real push reason (Chapter 2).

QMy company says it has no ratings. Does any of this apply?

AAlmost all of it. Someone still decides who gets the raise and who gets promoted [3]; the judgement has just moved somewhere you cannot see it. Ask your manager how pay and promotion decisions are actually made, and keep the evidence habit anyway.

Rewind
  • A review turns months of film into a paragraph and a number, through a cycle: expectations, the year, the self-review, the manager’s draft and calibration, and the conversation. Most of your influence comes before the room you are not in.
  • Ratings are noisy measurements. In two large data sets, raters’ own tendencies explained 62% and 53% of rating variance, and the ratee’s performance 21% and 25%. One rating is weak evidence about you.
  • Because the rater is part of the instrument, making your work easy to see accurately reduces noise. That is translation, not vanity.
  • Forced curves may weed out some low performers early, but they cost collaboration and rating accuracy, and whether performance even follows a bell curve is contested. Know which system you are in.
  • Calibration decides part of your rating through your manager’s summary. Give your manager a short, checkable account of your year, and make sure others see your work first-hand.
  • Bias in headline ratings is often small; the larger gaps tend to appear in review language, in “potential” and in turning ratings into pay. Concrete evidence narrows the room it has.
  • People’s self-assessments are only moderately accurate, and women tend to shade theirs down. Write the self-review for accuracy: outcomes, evidence, precise credit and one honest gap. The Dunning-Kruger meme is overstated.
  • Hear it, then disagree well: separate the number from the content, take the pause, correct facts with evidence, and turn the disagreement into next year’s expectations.

  35. Scopelliti, I., Loewenstein, G., & Vosgerau, J. (2015). You Call It “Self-Exuberance”; I Call It “Bragging”. Psychological Science, 26(6), 903–914. doi.org/10.1177/0956797615573516 ↩

    Moderate evidence experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Self-promoters overestimate how happy and proud listeners feel and underestimate their annoyance, so they over-promote and come across as braggarts.

    Caveats: Experimental settings.

  36. Dulebohn, J. H., Bommer, W. H., Liden, R. C., Brouer, R. L., & Ferris, G. R. (2012). A Meta-Analysis of Antecedents and Consequences of Leader-Member Exchange. Journal of Management, 38(6), 1715–1759. doi.org/10.1177/0149206311415280 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: LMX quality is driven most by leader behaviours and perceptions, also by follower traits and the relationship itself; LMX mediates many links to outcomes.

    Caveats: Published online 2011, volume 38 (2012).
