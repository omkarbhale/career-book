INT. LANTERNFISH, THE KITCHEN — WEDNESDAY, 7:52 AM

Dev is on her knees in front of Old Faithful with a torch in her mouth, because the machine is showing E-47 again and she is the only person who knows that you have to hold the descale button for eleven seconds while gently kicking the left panel.

It is the third time this week. Since the committee, everyone has quietly agreed that the coffee machine is Dev’s. The service engineer comes once a quarter; Dev comes whenever anyone posts a photo of the error screen in the channel, which is often.

Behind her, a new joiner watches with interest.

“Are you facilities?”

Dev Backend engineer.

“Oh. Then why are you…”

Dev does not have a good answer. She takes the torch out of her mouth, presses the button for eleven seconds and kicks the panel. The machine sighs and starts to work.

At her desk, her calendar for the day has seven meetings, two interviews she agreed to cover for someone, a review for Rex that he described as “tiny” (it is 900 lines), the onboarding session she runs for new testers, and, somewhere between 4 and 4:30, a block called actual work.

Dev is not in crisis. Nobody is shouting at her; nobody would describe her job as bad. She likes most of the people and most of the work. She is simply doing slightly too much of the wrong kind of thing, for slightly too long, and it is starting to show: in the evenings she spends answering messages, in the irritation she feels when the channel lights up, in the small voice that has started asking what the point is.

That is what pressure usually looks like at work. Not a dramatic collapse, but a slow drift, in workload, in boundaries and, sometimes, in standards. This last chapter of the long game is about holding your shape under that drift: what burnout actually is, why helpful people get buried, how good people end up doing things they would once have refused, and how to tell when the job itself is the problem.

Pause

Before you read on: think of the last time you felt worn out by work for more than a few weeks. What do you think caused it, mostly: the amount of work, something about the work, or something about you? Write down your guess. The chapter comes back to it.

What burnout is, and what it is not#

“Burnout” gets used for everything from a bad week to a serious collapse, which makes it hard to think about. It helps to start with the definition.

The World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases describes burn-out as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition: a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It has three dimensions: feelings of exhaustion; increased mental distance from the job, or feelings of negativity or cynicism about it; and reduced professional efficacy. It applies specifically to work, not to the rest of life [1]. That matches the model Christina Maslach and colleagues developed over decades, which places burnout in the relationship between a person and their work, rather than only inside the person [2].

Two consequences follow. The first is that burnout is not weakness. The definition itself says it comes from chronic stress at work that was not successfully managed, which is a statement about a situation, not a character. The second is that you should be very sceptical of headline statistics. A review of 182 studies of physician burnout, covering more than 109,000 doctors in 45 countries, found prevalence estimates ranging from 0% to 80.5%, because the studies used at least 142 different definitions [3]. When an article says “X% of workers are burned out”, the first question is: by whose definition?

Demands and resources#

What causes it? The most widely used model, the Job Demands-Resources model, says that strain comes mainly from high demands (workload, time pressure, emotional effort, conflict), while motivation comes mainly from resources (control over how you work, support from colleagues and managers, feedback, chances to learn), and that resources buffer the damage demands do [4]. Its authors present it as deliberately flexible [4], and that flexibility makes it hard to prove wrong our view. But as a way of looking at your own job, it is extremely useful, because it says there are two ways to feel better: reduce demands, or increase resources.

Some demands have particularly good evidence behind them:

  • Long hours. A joint WHO and International Labour Organization analysis attributed an estimated 745,194 deaths from heart disease and stroke in 2016 to working 55 or more hours a week, when 8.9% of the world’s population worked such hours [5]. That is a population estimate, not a prediction for any individual, but it is a reminder that long hours are not merely a matter of feeling tired.
  • Being expected to be always on. In three studies, organisational expectations that employees monitor email after hours raised their anxiety and harmed their health and relationship satisfaction, and their partners’ too, even apart from how much time they actually spent on email [6]. A survey of 570 working adults found the same expectation working through worse detachment and emotional exhaustion [7]. The expectation is the problem, not just the email.
  • Faking feelings. As Chapter 10 showed, surface acting, displaying emotions you do not feel, is substantially associated with impaired well-being [8]. A job that requires a cheerful mask all day is a demanding job, whatever its hours.

And the resource with the best evidence is surprisingly simple. Mentally detaching from work in your own time, not just being away from it but not thinking about it, goes with better life satisfaction and less strain, without any loss of engagement at work [9]. Switching off is not slacking; it is part of the work.

There is one fix for the always-on problem that anyone can apply to other people tomorrow. In eight pre-registered studies with more than 4,000 people, recipients of non-urgent emails sent outside working hours overestimated how quickly the sender expected a reply, which stressed them; a single line from the sender saying a reply was not urgent reduced that stress [10]. If you send messages late, add the line. It costs five words.

What actually helps#

If you look for advice on burnout, most of what you find is about the individual: sleep, exercise, meditation, resilience. Those are worth doing for their own sake. But the best evidence on interventions points somewhere else. A meta-analysis of controlled trials of interventions to reduce burnout in physicians found that they helped only modestly overall (a standardised mean difference of −0.29), and that interventions directed at the organisation (changes to workload, scheduling, teamwork) worked better (−0.45) than interventions directed at the individual (−0.18) [11]. That is doctors, not everyone, and the studies were few. But it fits the definition: if burnout comes from the relationship between a person and their job, changing only the person is half a fix.

So the most useful thing you can do about your own strain is often the least comfortable: talk to the person who can change the demands. That is usually your manager, and it is a workload conversation, not a confession our view.

Your Move 17.1A burnout inventory

This is not a diagnostic test, and no score here means you are or are not burned out. It is a way of seeing your own situation through the two ideas above. Use the last month.

Part A: the three dimensions. For each, choose rarely, sometimes, often or most days.

  1. I feel drained by work, and rest doesn’t fully refill me.
  2. I feel distant, cynical or negative about my job, or about the people it serves.
  3. I feel less effective at work than I used to be.

Part B: the seesaw. Write down your three heaviest demands and your three most important resources (or the resources you most lack). For each demand, mark it reducible, movable to someone else or fixed.

Reveal one way to play it

Read the pattern, not a total.

  • Mostly “exhaustion”, with cynicism and effectiveness intact? That usually points at the demand side: too much, for too long. Your reducible and movable demands from Part B are the agenda for a workload conversation, which is the kind of organisational fix the intervention research favours [11].
  • Cynicism high? That dimension is about your relationship with the job [2]. Ask what changed: a decision you disagree with, a feeling of being disrespected, a role you never chose. Those are worth naming to someone who can change them.
  • Effectiveness low? Look at the resource side: feedback, support and control are the resources the model says buffer demands [4]. A job with no feedback can make a competent person feel incompetent.
  • All three, most days, for months, and spreading into the rest of your life? Take it seriously. Talk to your manager about the job, and to a doctor or other clinician about you. “Mental health beyond burnout” in “Left Out, on Purpose” has starting points.

If Part B has no reducible or movable items at all, that is information too, and the end of this chapter is about what to do with it.

Before acting on any of this, two popular ideas about tiredness and positivity are worth checking.

Myth CheckWillpower is a tank that runs dryPartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

The idea that self-control draws on a limited resource that gets used up (“ego depletion”) was one of the most popular findings in psychology, and it is often used to explain why people are exhausted by the end of the day. A registered replication across 23 laboratories, with 2,141 participants following one agreed protocol, found an effect close to zero (d = 0.04) [12]. That replication tested one protocol, and researchers still argue about whether other versions of the effect exist, which is why the verdict is “partly” rather than “busted”. But the strong claim, that willpower is a fuel tank, is not supported. If you are worn out by work, the cause is far more likely to be the demands and the lack of recovery than a depleted inner reserve.

A second idea from popular resilience training deserves the same treatment.

Myth CheckFlourishing needs a positivity ratio of about 3 to 1Busted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

A 2005 paper claimed that people flourish when their ratio of positive to negative emotions is above about 2.9 [13], and the same mathematics had been used to claim a similar ratio for high-performing teams [14]. In 2013, a critique showed that the fluid-dynamics mathematics behind both ratios had been misapplied, and concluded that the claim to have found a critical positivity ratio was “entirely unfounded” [14], and the journal went on to publish a formal correction to the 2005 paper [15]. That does not mean positive emotions are unimportant, or that negative interactions don’t weigh more than positive ones; there is good evidence that bad experiences often have more impact than good ones [16]. It means there is no magic number, and no reason to count.

The helping trap#

Look again at Dev’s calendar. Very little of it is unreasonable on its own. Covering two interviews is helpful. Reviewing a colleague’s code is part of the job. Running onboarding for testers uses her QA background. Keeping the coffee machine alive is, frankly, a public service. The problem is the total, and what the total is made of.

The research on helping beyond your role, what organisational psychologists call citizenship behaviour, has a reassuring half and a worrying half, and “Reciprocity: give first, give small, give often” in Chapter 10 gave a first look at both. The reassuring half: across 168 samples and more than 51,000 people, citizenship behaviour went with better performance ratings from managers, more rewards, and lower turnover and absence [17]. (Some of that may be halo: managers who like you rate both your helpfulness and your performance highly.) The worrying half: in a study of 98 couples, people whose spouses rated them high on individual initiative reported more role overload, job stress and work-family conflict, the last of these more strongly for women [18]. And a theory paper points out the paradox: time spent helping comes out of time for your core work, and since reviews and promotions mostly reward the core work, helping too much can quietly hurt a career [19].

Not all helping is equal, either. In field and laboratory studies, researchers found that women were asked more often than men to do tasks that matter to an organisation but do little for the person’s advancement (sitting on committees, writing reports), and accepted such requests more often, driven by a shared expectation that women would say yes [20]. They called these low-promotability tasks. Many people now call the wider category glue work: the coordinating, onboarding, documenting, note-taking and fixing that holds a team together and rarely appears in anyone’s promotion case.

Dev’s week fits the loop exactly. She fixed Old Faithful once, became the person who fixes it, and is now asked several times a week. She ran onboarding once because she had the slides; now it is hers. None of it is in her review, and all of it comes out of the 4 pm block marked actual work.

Coming SoonChapter 19, “The Promotion Case”

In Chapter 19, glue work meets the promotion committee. Some of it counts and much of it does not, and the chapter looks at how to tell which is which, and how to make the parts that matter visible.

The way out is not to stop helping, which would make you a worse colleague and, on the evidence above, probably a lower-rated one. It is to choose which help to give and to stop the rest from hardening into a role. And that means saying no more often than feels natural.

Saying no#

Saying no costs less than it feels, as “Saying no, so your yes means something” in Chapter 12 showed. People asked to help find it awkward to refuse, which is why requesters underestimate how often others will say yes [21]. And people who decline overestimate how much it will hurt: in experiments on social invitations, people who said no expected the person inviting them to be more upset, and less likely to invite them again, than the inviters actually were [22]. That research was about invitations rather than work requests, so treat it as a strong hint rather than proof. But it points the same way as Dev’s experience the first time she tries it.

A good no has three parts, in our experience: a clear answer, a reason tied to priorities rather than feelings, and, when you can, a smaller alternative our view. “I can’t take the two interviews this week; I’m behind on the reconciliation work Kai asked me to finish. I can do one next week if that helps” is a no that leaves the relationship intact. “I’m so sorry, I’m just really swamped, maybe?” is not a no at all, and the person asking will hear a yes.

Your Move 17.2Your boundary sentence

Write the sentence you will use, in your own words, for each of these. Keep each to one or two sentences.

  1. A colleague asks you to “quickly” review something large, for the third time this month.
  2. Your manager messages at 9:30 pm with a question that is not urgent.
  3. You have become the unofficial owner of something that is not your job (a machine, a spreadsheet, a ritual) and want to hand it back.
  4. Someone asks you to join a committee that sounds worthwhile and will take two hours a week.
Reveal one way to play it

Sample sentences, and why they work:

  1. “I can’t do a full review of 900 lines this week. If you split out the risky part, I’ll look at that properly by Thursday.” A no to the size, a yes to the part that matters, with a date.
  2. Answer in the morning, or, if it really is quick: “Answering now since it’s quick, but I’m offline in the evenings from next week.” The research suggests that what hurts is the expectation of after-hours monitoring [6], so the useful move is to reset the expectation, kindly, once.
  3. “I’ve written up how to reset it and pinned it in the channel. From next month I’d like facilities to own it; can we agree that?” Writing it down is what breaks the “person who” step of the helping trap; naming an owner is what makes it stick.
  4. “It sounds worthwhile. Before I say yes, can I check with my manager how it fits with my priorities?” This is not stalling. Committees are the textbook example of a low-promotability task [20], so it is worth deciding deliberately, and asking whether the work will be recognised, rather than saying yes because it is hard to say no.

The common thread: each sentence is specific, ties the boundary to something real (time, priorities, ownership) and, where possible, offers something smaller.

Dev, meanwhile, takes the question to the person she always takes questions like this to.

INT/EXT. DEV'S FLAT AND MEERA'S GARDEN — SUNDAY, 6 PM (VIDEO CALL)

Meera is in her garden with a mug that says NO IS A FULL SENTENCE.

Dev I’m not burned out. I’m just… always slightly behind, and slightly annoyed, and I spend my Sundays dreading Monday’s coffee machine.
Meera Who decided the coffee machine was yours?
Dev Nobody. Everybody.
Meera That’s how it always happens. The first time you help, it’s a favour. The third time, it’s a job. The tenth time, it’s your identity, and nobody is paying you for it. When I was a team lead I was the person who organised every leaving party for nine years.
Dev What happened?
Meera I stopped. Nobody died. Someone else organised the next one, and it was better, because they actually liked parties.
Dev Your mug’s very on the nose today.
Meera My mugs are always on the nose. That’s what they’re for. And Dev, it’s not always a full sentence. Sometimes it’s “no, but here’s what I can do”. The point is that the word comes first.

Meera’s experience is not evidence, but it matches the research above: the cost of saying no is mostly in the anticipation. On Monday, Dev writes a one-page guide to E-47 (hold for eleven seconds, kick gently, call the service number if it happens twice in a day), pins it in the channel, and asks the facilities manager to own the machine from the first of the month. He says yes in four minutes. Two people react to the post with a crying emoji. Nobody dies.

The first small step#

Workload is one kind of pressure. There is another, which is rarer and more dangerous: pressure to do something you would not normally do.

INT. LANTERNFISH, THE RELEASE ROOM — WEDNESDAY, 2 PM

A large warehouse client goes live on the new reconciliation system on Friday. The product director has promised the date to the client’s chief executive. The release pipeline is green except for one test, which fails about one run in five.

Rex That one’s flaky. It’s been flaky for weeks. Mark it as a known issue and we ship.

Dev has spent three years of her life telling flaky tests from real failures, and she is fairly sure this one is real: when it fails, a small number of shipments appear twice in the client’s ledger. She also notices something she would rather not. Two weeks ago, the squad skipped a performance test “just this once”. Last week, the status report to the client said green for an item that was amber, because it was “basically fine”. Each step was small. Each made the next one feel normal.

Dev I don’t think it’s flaky. I think it’s duplicating shipments under load. If we ship it, the client’s numbers will be wrong, and they won’t know.

The room goes quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has made Friday more complicated.

Very few people set out to do something wrong at work. The research on how they end up doing it anyway is sobering, because it describes ordinary people in ordinary situations.

In four multi-round experiments, David Welsh and colleagues found that people who were led into dishonesty through small transgressions that grew gradually were more likely to commit a large transgression later than people faced with the large one straight away, an effect explained by moral disengagement, the gradual rationalising that lets people stop seeing an act as wrong [23]. That is the slippery slope, and it explains how a skipped test becomes a misreported status, which becomes a shipped defect, which becomes a client’s wrong numbers. A related theory paper describes ethical fading: self-deception that lets the ethical side of a decision fade from view, so that people act unethically without experiencing it as an ethical choice at all [24]. Nobody in the release room thinks they are discussing ethics. They think they are discussing a flaky test.

The situation matters as much as the people. The meta-analysis from “When something is actually wrong” in Chapter 16 found that unethical choices come from the person, the issue and the environment together [25], and a contested critique of goal-setting argues that goals which are too specific or too hard, like a fixed date promised to a client’s chief executive, can narrow attention and increase unethical behaviour among their other side effects [26]. Pressure rarely announces itself as an ethics test. It arrives as a deadline.

The defence the research points to is unglamorous: notice the first small step, and decide your lines in advance, in calm conditions, rather than in the release room on a Wednesday afternoon our view. People who have thought in advance about what they will not do are not immune to pressure, but they recognise the staircase when they see it.

What Dev does next is the same pattern as a good no: a clear answer, a reason tied to shared goals and an alternative. She does not accuse anyone. She says what she believes is happening, what it would do to the client, and what she suggests instead: ship on Friday with the reconciliation feature switched off for the duplicate-prone shipment type, fix it by Tuesday, and tell the client today, before Friday, exactly what is happening and why.

Tomás, who has been listening from the doorway, asks one question.

Tomás How sure are you it’s real?
Dev Sure enough that I can show you in twenty minutes.

She shows him in fifteen. He calls the product director. The client’s chief executive, told on a Wednesday instead of discovering it in a month, is irritated for an afternoon and grateful by Monday. Tomás, it turns out, hates Friday surprises a great deal more than he hates Wednesday ones, a preference Dev first learned about the hard way (Chapter 12).

Not every story ends this well. Sometimes the person who says no is overruled; sometimes they pay for it. The research on voice in “Speaking up to people with power” in Chapter 16 is honest that challenging the people above you carries risk [27]. But the rungs of the escalation ladder exist for exactly this reason, and so does the pre-commitment below. And in a repeated game, being known as the person who will not sign a false number is worth a great deal, including to the people who were annoyed with you on the Wednesday.

Your Move 17.3What would you refuse to do?

In calm conditions (now is good), write three short lines that start “I will not…”, about your own work. They should be specific enough that you would recognise the first small step towards each one. Then, for each, write the first small step.

Reveal one way to play it

There is no right list; strong ones are concrete and tied to your actual job. For example:

  • “I will not tell a customer something is tested when it isn’t.” First small step: agreeing to describe a skipped test as “covered”.
  • “I will not change a number in a report to make it look better than it is.” First small step: rounding an awkward figure in the direction that helps, “just for the slide”.
  • “I will not ship something I believe will harm users without telling someone who can stop it.” First small step: deciding not to mention a doubt because the release is close.

What makes these work is the second line: naming the first small step turns an abstract principle into something you can notice in the moment, which is exactly where the slippery-slope research says people go wrong [23]. Keep the list somewhere you will see it. If you never need it, nothing is lost.

When the job is the problem#

Sometimes boundaries, conversations and a better no are not enough, because the problem is the job itself: the demands are too high and are not going to fall, the resources are missing and are not going to arrive, or the culture keeps asking for the first small step.

When people do leave, they usually leave for concrete reasons. The Pew survey from “Why people actually leave” in Chapter 2 found that US workers who quit a job in 2021 most often cited low pay, no opportunities for advancement and feeling disrespected at work, followed by childcare problems, inflexible hours and poor benefits [28]. Those are self-reported reasons, from one country, but they are a useful checklist: if three or four of them describe your job, and they are not changing, the problem is probably not your resilience.

Some signals that it might be time to go, which are our synthesis rather than a validated test our view:

  • You have raised the workload or the problem clearly, at least twice, with the person who can change it, and nothing has changed.
  • You are regularly asked to take the first small step, and the people asking do not seem to notice.
  • The three burnout dimensions (exhaustion, distance or cynicism, and a sense that you are no longer good at the job) have lasted for months, not weeks, and are spreading into the rest of your life.
  • You no longer recognise the person you are at work, and you do not like them.

If those describe you, it is worth remembering where this book started. Leaving a job is not failure; it is the loop from “The loop” in Chapter 1. Chapter 2 is about making that decision well, and Chapter 22 comes back to the same decision years later, made from strength. If any of it is affecting your health, a doctor or other clinician is the right person to talk to, not a book.

Field NoteWorking hours and switching off

UKEUUSIndia

United Kingdom. You cannot work more than 48 hours a week on average, normally averaged over 17 weeks, unless you choose to opt out. The opt-out must be voluntary and in writing, you cannot be dismissed or treated unfairly for refusing to sign it, and you can cancel it at any time with at least seven days’ notice (or up to three months, if your agreement says so). Some jobs are exceptions [29].

European Union. The Working Time Directive requires member states to limit average working time to 48 hours a week including overtime, to guarantee at least 11 consecutive hours of rest in every 24, and to give at least four weeks of paid annual leave; national law sets the details and some states allow individual opt-outs under strict conditions. There is no EU-wide “right to disconnect” law yet: as of our check, the European Commission had consulted employers’ and workers’ representatives on one (that consultation ended in October 2025) and planned a proposal for a broader Quality Jobs Act by the end of 2026. Some member states have their own rules, which we did not check [30].

United States. Federal law sets no limit on the number of hours employees aged 16 and over may work. Covered employees who are not exempt must be paid at least one and a half times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek; many salaried professionals are exempt, and states can add rules [31].

India. Under the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, which took effect with the other labour codes on 21 November 2025, the Labour Ministry describes 8 hours a day and 48 hours a week as the standard, with overtime paid at twice the normal rate. We could not verify how far this covers office and IT staff, or how it interacts with state Shops and Establishments Acts [32].

For other countries, and for what applies to your own contract, check an official source.

Answering the pause#

At the start of the chapter you guessed what mostly caused the last stretch of feeling worn out by work: the amount of work, something about the work, or something about you. If you guessed “me”, it is worth knowing that this is the answer the evidence supports least. The WHO definition locates burnout in chronic workplace stress [1], the best-studied model puts it in the balance of demands and resources [4], and interventions aimed at the organisation beat interventions aimed at the person [11]. Your habits matter at the margin. The job matters more.

Ask Anyway

QIsn’t saying no just going to make me look less committed?

AIt might, a little, in the moment our view. But the evidence suggests people overestimate how much refusals cost [22], and a theory paper argues that helping beyond your role comes at the cost of your core work, which is what reviews mostly reward [19]. A clear, well-reasoned no, with an alternative, usually reads as someone who knows their priorities.

QI can’t afford to leave. What then?

AThen the levers are the ones in this chapter: move demands off the scale where you can, add resources where you can (support, control, recovery), have the workload conversation with the person who can change it, and start rebuilding your options in the background. Chapter 2 covers searching while employed, which is easier than searching after leaving.

Which brings us back to Lanternfish, and to Friday.

INT. DEV'S FLAT — FRIDAY, 6:15 PM

Release day. The reconciliation system went live at noon, with one feature switched off and a client who knows why. Nothing is on fire. Dev left the office at 5:30, which she has not done on a Friday in months.

Old Faithful, under new management, has been working all week. The facilities manager has posted a photo of himself holding the descale button, captioned eleven seconds, apparently.

She opens her spreadsheet and scrolls to the Wins tab. She adds a line: Caught a duplicate-shipment defect before go-live; proposed the flag-off release; client informed Wednesday. She looks at the tab for a while. There is nothing on it about coffee, and nothing about the 900-line review, or the onboarding sessions, or the interviews she covered. There are a lot of weeks on it with nothing at all.

Her first annual review with Tomás is in six weeks.

She closes the laptop, puts her phone in a drawer, and goes out.

That is the end of Part II, and of Dev’s first fourteen months at Lanternfish. She arrived as Corporate Dev, stiff and afraid to ask. She leaves Part II as someone with a register, a friend with rules, a record of what she did, the habit of fighting about the work and not the people, and a few lines she will not cross. That is the long game played well: not a performance, but a set of defaults that hold under pressure.

It is also, she is about to find out, only half of what it takes for good work to count. The long game builds the film. The career game decides who sees it, and what they write about it. Part III begins with the most ordinary and most consequential piece of that game, the annual review, in Chapter 18.

Rewind
  • Burnout is an occupational phenomenon, not a weakness: chronic workplace stress that wasn’t managed, with exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy. Distrust headline percentages; definitions vary wildly.
  • Strain comes from demands, motivation from resources, and resources buffer demands. Long hours, always-on expectations and faking feelings are demands with strong evidence behind them; switching off is a resource.
  • Fix the job, not only yourself. Interventions aimed at the organisation beat interventions aimed at the individual, so the most useful step is often a workload conversation with the person who can change it.
  • The helping trap is real. Helping is rewarded up to a point, then eats the work your review rewards, and low-promotability tasks fall unequally on women. Break the loop at “the person who”: write it down and give it an owner.
  • Saying no costs less than you fear. A good no is clear, tied to priorities, and offers something smaller.
  • Integrity fails one small step at a time. Gradual transgressions lead to larger ones, and pressure arrives as a deadline, not an ethics test. Set your lines in advance and learn to recognise the first step.
  • Sometimes the job is the problem. Pay, progression and respect are the commonest reasons people leave. Leaving well is part of the loop, and Chapter 2 is where to start.
  • Myths: willpower is not a tank that runs dry, and there is no magic positivity ratio.

  3. Rotenstein, L. S., Torre, M., Ramos, M. A., Rosales, R. C., Guille, C., Sen, S., & Mata, D. A. (2018). Prevalence of Burnout Among Physicians. JAMA, 320(11), 1131. doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.12777 ↩

    Strong evidence systematic review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Physician burnout prevalence estimates ranged from 0% to 80.5% because studies used at least 142 different definitions - burnout statistics are only as good as their definition.

    Caveats: Physicians only, but the measurement lesson is general.

  4. Bakker, A. B., & Demerouti, E. (2007). The Job Demands-Resources model: state of the art. Journal of Managerial Psychology, 22(3), 309–328. doi.org/10.1108/02683940710733115 ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence theory and review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The Job Demands-Resources model - strain comes from high demands, motivation from resources (support, autonomy, feedback), and resources buffer demands.

    Caveats: Very widely used and flexible (critics say too flexible to falsify).

  5. Pega, F., Náfrádi, B., Momen, N. C., Ujita, Y., Streicher, K. N., Prüss-Üstün, A. M., et al. (2021). Global, regional, and national burdens of ischemic heart disease and stroke attributable to exposure to long working hours for 194 countries, 2000–2016: A systematic analysis from the WHO/ILO Joint Estimates of the Work-related Burden of Disease and Injury. Environment International, 154, 106595. doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2021.106595 ↩

    Strong evidence WHO/ILO systematic estimates opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Working 55+ hours a week was linked to an estimated 745,194 deaths from heart disease and stroke in 2016; 8.9% of the global population worked such hours.

    Caveats: Population-attributable estimates, not individual prediction.

  6. Becker, W. J., Belkin, L. Y., Conroy, S. A., & Tuskey, S. (2021). Killing Me Softly: Organizational E-mail Monitoring Expectations’ Impact on Employee and Significant Other Well-Being. Journal of Management, 47(4), 1024–1052. doi.org/10.1177/0149206319890655 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence experience sampling + dyadic surveys opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Organisational expectations to monitor email after hours cause anxiety that harms employees' health and relationship satisfaction, and their partners' too - even apart from time actually spent on email.

    Caveats: Published online 2019 in volume 47 (2021).

  7. Belkin, L. Y., Becker, W. J., & Conroy, S. A. (2020). The Invisible Leash: The Impact of Organizational Expectations for Email Monitoring After-Hours on Employee Resources, Well-Being, and Turnover Intentions. Group &amp; Organization Management, 45(5), 709–740. doi.org/10.1177/1059601120933143 ↩

    Moderate evidence time-separated survey opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Expectations of after-hours email monitoring reduce work-life balance via poor detachment and emotional exhaustion; time spent on email was not the main driver.

    Caveats: Survey design.

  8. Hülsheger, U. R., & Schewe, A. F. (2011). On the costs and benefits of emotional labor: A meta-analysis of three decades of research. Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 16(3), 361–389. doi.org/10.1037/a0022876 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Surface acting (and feeling a clash between felt and required emotions) relates substantially to impaired well-being (rho .39-.48) and worse job attitudes; deep acting has weak links to strain and positive links to emotional performance and customer satisfaction.

    Caveats: Mostly correlational.

  9. Sonnentag, S. (2012). Psychological Detachment From Work During Leisure Time. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 21(2), 114–118. doi.org/10.1177/0963721411434979 ↩

    Strong evidence review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Mentally switching off from work during leisure relates to better life satisfaction and less strain without lower engagement at work.

    Caveats: Short review of a robust literature.

  10. Giurge, L. M., & Bohns, V. K. (2021). You don’t need to answer right away! Receivers overestimate how quickly senders expect responses to non-urgent work emails. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 167, 114–128. doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2021.08.002 ↩

    Strong evidence pre-registered experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Receivers overestimate how quickly senders expect replies to non-urgent after-hours emails ("email urgency bias"), which adds stress; a one-line note from the sender about expected reply time reduces it.

    Caveats: Abstract read via web search (publisher-linked copies).

  11. Panagioti, M., Panagopoulou, E., Bower, P., Lewith, G., Kontopantelis, E., Chew-Graham, C., et al. (2017). Controlled Interventions to Reduce Burnout in Physicians. JAMA Internal Medicine, 177(2), 195. doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.7674 ↩ ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis of controlled trials opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Burnout interventions for physicians produced small reductions (SMD -0.29); organisation-directed interventions worked better (SMD -0.45) than individual-directed ones (SMD -0.18).

    Caveats: Physicians only.

  12. Hagger, M. S., Chatzisarantis, N. L. D., Alberts, H., Anggono, C. O., Batailler, C., Birt, A. R., et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573. doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873 ↩

    Strong evidence multi-lab registered replication opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The ego-depletion effect (willpower as a limited resource) was near zero (d = 0.04, CI including zero).

    Caveats: One protocol; debate continues, but the strong "willpower tank" claim is unsupported.

  13. Fredrickson, B. L., & Losada, M. F. (2005). Positive Affect and the Complex Dynamics of Human Flourishing. American Psychologist, 60(7), 678–686. doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.60.7.678 ↩

    ✕Myth or unsupported daily diary study opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Claimed a "critical positivity ratio" of 2.9 positive to negative emotions separates flourishing people.

    Caveats: The mathematical basis was shown to be invalid (brown2013), and the journal published a formal correction notice (fredrickson2013).

  14. Brown, N. J. L., Sokal, A. D., & Friedman, H. L. (2013). The complex dynamics of wishful thinking: The critical positivity ratio. American Psychologist, 68(9), 801–813. doi.org/10.1037/a0032850 ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence critique opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The fluid-dynamics mathematics behind the 2.9013 positivity ratio (and the Losada team ratio) was misapplied; the claim is "entirely unfounded".

    Caveats: Does not show positive emotions are unimportant - only that the magic ratio is baseless.

  15. Fredrickson, B. L., & Losada, M. F. (2013). "Positive affect and the complex dynamics of human flourishing": Correction to Fredrickson and Losada (2005). American Psychologist, 68(9), 822–822. doi.org/10.1037/a0034435 ↩

    Strong evidence correction notice opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Formal correction to fredrickson2005, published after the brown2013 critique.

    Caveats: No machine-readable text was checked; cite only for the fact that a correction was published.

  16. Baumeister, R. F., Bratslavsky, E., Finkenauer, C., & Vohs, K. D. (2001). Bad is Stronger than Good. Review of General Psychology, 5(4), 323–370. doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.323 ↩

    Strong evidence narrative review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Across many domains, bad events, feedback and impressions have more impact than equivalent good ones, and bad impressions form faster and resist change.

    Caveats: Very influential; some individual findings it cites have since weakened, but the broad asymmetry is widely accepted.

  17. Podsakoff, N. P., Whiting, S. W., Podsakoff, P. M., & Blume, B. D. (2009). Individual- and organizational-level consequences of organizational citizenship behaviors: A meta-analysis. Journal of Applied Psychology, 94(1), 122–141. doi.org/10.1037/a0013079 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Organizational citizenship behaviours (helping beyond the job description) relate to better managerial performance ratings and reward allocations, lower turnover and absence, and better unit-level performance; longitudinal links are somewhat stronger.

    Caveats: Rater halo is possible - managers who like you may rate both your helping and your performance highly.

  18. Bolino, M. C., & Turnley, W. H. (2005). The Personal Costs of Citizenship Behavior: The Relationship Between Individual Initiative and Role Overload, Job Stress, and Work-Family Conflict. Journal of Applied Psychology, 90(4), 740–748. doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.90.4.740 ↩

    Moderate evidence field survey (dyads) opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Higher individual initiative (a form of citizenship) was linked to more role overload, job stress and work-family conflict, more strongly for women.

    Caveats: Small, cross-sectional.

  19. Bergeron, D. M. (2007). The potential paradox of organizational citizenship behavior: Good citizens at what cost?. Academy of Management Review, 32(4), 1078–1095. doi.org/10.5465/amr.2007.26585791 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence conceptual opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Time spent on citizenship behaviour can come at the expense of task performance, and since rewards mostly track task performance, helping too much can hurt careers.

    Caveats: Theory; see bolino2005 and babcock2017 for data.

  20. Babcock, L., Recalde, M. P., Vesterlund, L., & Weingart, L. (2017). Gender Differences in Accepting and Receiving Requests for Tasks with Low Promotability. American Economic Review, 107(3), 714–747. doi.org/10.1257/aer.20141734 ↩ ↩

    Strong evidence lab and field experiments + field data opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Women volunteer more, are asked more, and accept more requests for "non-promotable" tasks (committees, reports); a shared expectation that women will say yes drives it.

    Caveats: Published in American Economic Review; mechanism shown experimentally.

  21. Flynn, F. J., & Lake, V. K. B. (2008). If you need help, just ask: Underestimating compliance with direct requests for help. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 95(1), 128–143. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128 ↩

    Strong evidence experiments + field studies opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People underestimate by as much as 50% how likely others are to agree to a direct request for help, because they overlook how awkward it is to say no.

    Caveats: Replicated conceptually by later work (e.g., givi2024 on the other side of the request).

  22. Givi, J., & Kirk, C. P. (2024). Saying no: The negative ramifications from invitation declines are less severe than we think. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 126(6), 1103–1115. doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000443 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: People who decline invitations overestimate how angry or hurt the inviter will be and how much it will cost future invitations.

    Caveats: Social invitations, not work requests; recent (2024).

  23. Welsh, D. T., Ordóñez, L. D., Snyder, D. G., & Christian, M. S. (2015). The slippery slope: How small ethical transgressions pave the way for larger future transgressions. Journal of Applied Psychology, 100(1), 114–127. doi.org/10.1037/a0036950 ↩ ↩

    Moderate evidence multi-round experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Small ethical transgressions that grow gradually lead to larger ones (a slippery slope), via moral disengagement.

    Caveats: Lab tasks; builds on a Gino co-authored framework but is not itself co-authored by Gino.

  24. Tenbrunsel, A. E., & Messick, D. M. (2004). Ethical Fading: The Role of Self-Deception in Unethical Behavior. Social Justice Research, 17(2), 223–236. doi.org/10.1023/B:SORE.0000027411.35832.53 ↩

    Moderate evidence conceptual opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: "Ethical fading" - self-deception lets the ethical side of a decision fade from view, so people act unethically without seeing it as such.

    Caveats: Abstract not machine-readable; claim is title-level.

  25. Kish-Gephart, J. J., Harrison, D. A., & Treviño, L. K. (2010). Bad apples, bad cases, and bad barrels: Meta-analytic evidence about sources of unethical decisions at work. Journal of Applied Psychology, 95(1), 1–31. doi.org/10.1037/a0017103 ↩

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Unethical choices come from individual traits ("bad apples"), the moral issue ("bad cases") and the environment ("bad barrels") together.

    Caveats: Correlational.

  26. Ordóñez, L. D., Schweitzer, M. E., Galinsky, A. D., & Bazerman, M. H. (2009). Goals Gone Wild: The Systematic Side Effects of Overprescribing Goal Setting. Academy of Management Perspectives, 23(1), 6–16. doi.org/10.5465/amp.2009.37007999 ↩

    Moderate evidence critical review opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Goal setting has side effects - narrow focus, more risk-taking, more unethical behaviour, less learning - when goals are too specific or too hard.

    Caveats: Contested by goal-setting researchers; best read as a warning label.

  27. Burris, E. R. (2012). The Risks and Rewards of Speaking Up: Managerial Responses to Employee Voice. Academy of Management Journal, 55(4), 851–875. doi.org/10.5465/amj.2010.0562 ↩

    Moderate evidence field study + experiments opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Managers rated employees who voiced challenging ideas as worse performers and endorsed their ideas less than those who voiced supportive ideas; perceived loyalty and threat explained this.

    Caveats: Shows the real risk of speaking up, which psychological safety tries to reduce.

  28. Parker, K., & Horowitz, J. M. (2022). Majority of workers who quit a job in 2021 cite low pay, no opportunities for advancement, feeling disrespected. Pew Research Center, 9 March 2022. www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/03/09/majority-of-workers-who-quit-a-job-in-2021-cite-low-pay-no-opportunities-for-advancement-feeling-disrespected/ ↩

    Strong evidence probability-based survey (American Trends Panel) opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: Among US adults who quit a job in 2021, the most cited reasons were low pay (63%), no opportunities for advancement (63%) and feeling disrespected at work (57%); childcare issues were cited by 48% of those with a child under 18 at home, lack of flexibility over hours by 45% and poor benefits by 43%.

    Caveats: Self-reported reasons, one country, one unusual year; people could give several reasons. Disrespect is partly about managers, but pay and advancement rank as high.

  29. GOV.UK (2026). Maximum weekly working hours (Overview; Calculating your working hours; Opting out of the 48 hour week). www.gov.uk/maximum-weekly-working-hours ↩

    Strong evidence law opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: "You cannot work more than 48 hours a week on average - normally averaged over 17 weeks." The reference period "can be extended to a maximum of 52 weeks if this is agreed" (26 weeks for trainee doctors; 52 weeks offshore oil and gas). Counted as work: paid overtime, "unpaid overtime you're asked to do", job-related training, working lunches, on-call time at the workplace. Opt-out: "You can choose to work more by opting out of the 48-hour week." It "must be voluntary and in writing"; you "cannot be sacked or treated unfairly for refusing"; you "can cancel your opt-out agreement whenever you want - even if it's part of your employment contract", giving "at least 7 days' notice" (or "up to 3 months' notice depending on what's in your agreement"). Under-18s "cannot work more than 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week".

    Caveats: Official guidance page, not the Working Time Regulations 1998 text (not opened). Some workers cannot opt out (airline staff, ships/boats, road transport, others under EU drivers' hours rules, security guards on high-value-goods vehicles); some jobs (e.g. 24-hour staffing, armed forces, emergency services, police) are exceptions to the 48-hour limit. No last-updated date was visible; year is the year accessed.

  30. European Commission, D. f. E. S. A. a. I. (2003). Working Time Directive (2003/88/EC). employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies-and-activities/rights-work/labour-law/working-conditions/working-time-directive_en ↩

    Strong evidence law opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: The Directive requires Member States to guarantee: "the average working time for each seven day period must not exceed 48 hours, including overtime", averaged "over a reference period of up to 4, 6 or 12 months" depending on national law/collective agreements; "in every 24 hours a worker is entitled to a minimum of 11 consecutive hours of rest"; weekly rest of "a minimum of 24 uninterrupted hours in addition to the 11 hours' daily rest"; a rest break if on duty longer than 6 hours; and "paid annual leave of at least 4 weeks per year". Member States "may allow an individual worker to opt-out from maximum weekly working time subject to strict conditions", including that the worker "should not suffer any detriment if he/she refuses". Right to disconnect (status, from the Commission page "Quality jobs for companies and workers in Europe", https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies-and-activities/rights-work/quality-jobs-companies-and-workers-europe_en): October 2025, "Two-stage consultation of European social partners on the right to disconnect and telework ends"; Dec 2025 - Jan 2026 first-stage consultation on a Quality Jobs Act "explores areas such as the right to disconnect"; timeline item "End of 2026: EU law proposal for a Quality Jobs Act". The page describes no EU right-to-disconnect law in force.

    Caveats: Official Commission summary, not the Directive's article text (EUR-Lex blocked automated access, so Articles 3, 6, 7 and 16 were not read directly). Derogations exist for specific sectors/categories and transport has separate directives. "No EU right-to-disconnect law yet" is what the Commission page implies as of 2026-10-25 (only consultations and a planned end-2026 proposal); a proposal may have appeared after this check, and some Member States have national rules (not checked).

  31. U.S. Department of Labor, W. a. H. D. (2026). Wages and the Fair Labor Standards Act; Overtime Pay. www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa ↩

    Strong evidence law opened 2026-10-25

    Shows: "There is no limit on the number of hours employees 16 years or older may work in any workweek" (overtime page: "There is no limit in the Act on the number of hours employees aged 16 and older may work in any workweek").     Caveats: Federal law only; state laws may add daily overtime or other rules (not checked). Exempt employees (e.g. under the white-collar exemptions) are not entitled to FLSA overtime; the exemption tests were not verified here. No page date shown; year is the year accessed.
