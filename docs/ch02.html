INT. CORVID SYSTEMS, MEETING ROOM 3 — MONDAY, 4:30 PM

Dev has rehearsed this. She has a one-page note, three examples, and a sentence she practised in the lift: I’d like to move into development, and I think I’m ready to start.

Her manager listens properly, which almost makes it worse: a nod for the regression suite, a nod for the deployment scripts, a remark that the client loves her test reports.

Dev So, could I move onto the development team? Even part-time, to start?

Her manager leans back, wearing the face of someone who would like to say yes and has a budget that says no. “Maybe next year. Let’s see how the headcount looks after the planning round. You’re too valuable where you are.”

It is meant as a compliment. Dev walks back to her desk turning it over. Too valuable where you are sounds a lot like staying where you are.

At the next desk, Rex is taking a photo of his monitor.

Rex Big news. I accepted one of them. Two weeks and I’m gone. Another fresh start, Dev!
Dev Six jobs in eight years isn’t a fresh start. It’s a pattern.
Rex Patterns are just luck you can repeat.

That evening Dev opens her spreadsheet and clicks on the third tab, the one she made at the end of Chapter 1. It is called Should I stay? and it is still empty.

This chapter is about that tab. The snapshot game does not start with a résumé. It starts with a decision most people make badly: whether to play at all, and if so, which version of the game. You can leave the company, you can move within it, or you can stay and change something. And sometimes the decision is made for you, which is the hardest version and the one this chapter treats most seriously.

Pause

Before you read on: what is the single best reason you could have for leaving a job? Write it down. By the end of the chapter you will know whether it is a push or a pull, and why that matters more than how good the reason sounds.

Push and pull#

Most people decide to leave a job the way you decide to leave a party: a slow build of small irritations, and then, suddenly, coats. The trouble is that “I want to leave” bundles two different things together, and they need different answers.

A push is something wrong here: the pay, the boredom, the manager, the commute, the lack of a path. A pull is something right there: work you want to do, people you want to learn from, money, a place you want to live. The two often arrive together, which is why they are easy to confuse. But they lead to different first moves.

Push reasons come with a question you should ask before you start applying elsewhere: could this be fixed here? Some pushes can be. A pay gap can sometimes be closed by asking, a bad project left, a schedule changed. Others cannot, and you only learn which by asking.

Pull reasons come with a different question: is this on offer here? If what you want is a different kind of work, it may exist two floors up. If it doesn’t, your pull list becomes something precious: the specification for your search. It is the start of the target list you will build in Chapter 4.

The reason to do this sorting on purpose is that a search driven only by push tends to end badly our view. If the only criterion is “not here”, almost any offer looks like rescue, and you can find yourself, a year later, in a new job with the same problem and a shorter tenure on your résumé. Pull is what turns an escape into a move.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

Write both lists down, on paper or in a spreadsheet tab, and give each item a score for how much it matters. Then, for every push, write what it would take to fix it and who could fix it. Nobody has tested this as an intervention, but it forces the useful question of whether you are running from something or towards something, and usually it is both.

Why people actually leave#

There is a confident piece of folk wisdom about why people quit, and it is worth checking against what people say when they are asked.

In a 2022 Pew Research Center survey of US workers who had quit a job in 2021, the most common reasons were low pay (63%), no opportunities for advancement (63%) and feeling disrespected at work (57%), followed by childcare problems (48% of those with children under 18 at home), inflexible hours (45%) and poor benefits (43%) [1]. Those are self-reported reasons, from one country in one unusual year, and people can cite more than one. Still, look at the top of the list. Two of the three biggest reasons are about money and a path forward, and the third is about how people are treated. Dev’s list has all three in it.

Researchers who study turnover more broadly find no single cause. A large meta-analysis of the predictors of voluntary turnover found many of them, from job attitudes and the alternatives people have to how embedded they are in the place and how they are led, with effects that vary by context [2]. People leave for a mixture of reasons, and the mixture is personal.

Myth CheckPeople leave managers, not companiesPartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Weak evidence

Managers clearly matter. Gallup reports that in its survey of 7,272 US adults, about one in two had left a job at some point “to get away from their manager” [3]. But that figure comes from a company that sells manager training and engagement surveys, and its page is inconsistent about the related claim that managers account for “at least” or “up to” 70% of the variance in engagement [3]. In an independent survey, the reasons quitters gave most often were low pay and no advancement, each cited by 63%, with feeling disrespected at work, which is partly about managers, at 57% [1]. Turnover research finds many causes, not one [2]. The honest version: people often leave managers, and just as often leave pay, prospects and the place.

The myth matters for your own decision because it suggests a shortcut: if the manager is the problem, a new manager is the solution. Sometimes that is true. But if the real push is a missing path, a new manager in a company with the same missing path will not fix it, and a move to a better-paid job under a worse manager may not either. Sort the reasons first.

Your Move 2.1Push or pull?

Here are ten reasons people give for wanting to leave. Sort each into push (something wrong here), pull (something right somewhere else) or both. Then, for each push, decide whether it could possibly be fixed without leaving. Commit before you open the answer.

  1. “My manager takes credit for my work.”
  2. “A friend’s company is building exactly the product I care about.”
  3. “I’ve been doing the same thing for three years.”
  4. “I’m paid less than people doing my job elsewhere.”
  5. “I want to move to another city.”
  6. “There’s nobody senior here I can learn from.”
  7. “The new office is a ninety-minute commute.”
  8. “I want to try managing people.”
  9. “Everyone I liked has left.”
  10. “I’m bored.”
Reveal one way to play it
  • 1. Push. Possibly fixable: you could raise it, document your work in writing (Chapter 16 has the tactics) or move teams. If it is a pattern your organisation tolerates, it may not be.
  • 2. Pull. A clear one, and specific, which is what makes it useful as a search criterion.
  • 3. Push, and often fixable: ask for a different project or a stretch assignment first (Chapter 20 is about choosing what to get good at).
  • 4. Both. The push is underpayment, the pull is the market. It is the most fixable push on the list, because you can bring evidence and ask (Chapter 9 covers the evidence on asking). If the answer is no, it becomes a strong reason to look.
  • 5. Pull, and probably not fixable here unless your company has an office there or allows remote work. Ask; some do.
  • 6. Push, sometimes fixable through a team move or a mentor elsewhere in the company, sometimes not at all in a small firm.
  • 7. Push. Fixable only if a hybrid or remote arrangement is on the table.
  • 8. Pull, and worth asking about internally first: trying it inside a company you know is lower-risk (Chapter 21).
  • 9. Push, and an honest one. Not fixable, but worth noticing: the people who left are now your contacts elsewhere (Chapter 6).
  • 10. Neither, yet. “Bored” is a symptom. Ask of what, and you will usually find a push (no challenge) or a pull (something else you would rather be doing).

The pattern: pushes deserve a conversation before a search; pulls deserve a search, starting inside.

Ask before you leave#

If any of your reasons might be fixable, the cheapest experiment in your whole career is to ask. Not to hint, not to “flag it in your review”, but to ask: here is what I want, here is why I think I am ready, can we make it happen, and by when?

There is a good economic reason to try. Moving inside a company and moving to a new one trade different things. In a study of an investment-banking division’s personnel records, as reported by Wharton, external hires were paid about 18 to 20% more than people promoted internally into similar jobs, but received significantly lower performance ratings for their first two years and were more likely to leave; those who stayed beyond two years were promoted faster [4]. That is one industry, read through a secondary write-up, so treat the numbers as an illustration rather than a law. But the shape makes sense. An outsider is paid a premium partly because the employer cannot see their film, and then has to rebuild a reputation from scratch. An insider is paid less partly because everybody has already seen it.

Internal moves are also a much bigger channel than they look from outside. In one recruiting-software vendor’s customer data, internal candidates were 0.27% of applications but 12.56% of hires [5]. That is a vendor’s data, with a product to sell and a customer base that is not a random sample of employers, but it points the same way: once someone is inside, they are hired at a very different rate.

This is the loop from Chapter 1 working in your favour. Inside your company, you do not need a trailer. People have watched the film.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

How to ask so that the answer means something. Be specific about the move (“join the payments team as a junior developer”), give evidence you are ready (two or three things you have already done that the new role needs), offer a bridge (“I could pair on their tickets for a month alongside my current work”) and ask for a date (“could we decide by the end of the next planning round?”). Then write a short follow-up message summarising what you agreed. This is practitioner advice rather than tested research, but it turns a vague hope into a commitment someone can keep or break, which is information either way.

What about “maybe next year”? It can mean exactly what it says. It can also mean “no, and I would rather not say so”. You cannot read minds, but you can read commitments. If “maybe” comes with a date, a condition you can meet and a follow-up meeting in the diary, it is a real maybe. If it comes with none of those, treat it as a no for planning purposes, and stay polite about it our view. Dev’s “maybe next year” came with no date and no condition, and her manager’s “too valuable where you are” was a reason to keep her, not a reason to move her. That does not make her manager a villain; there is a team to run. It does tell her that the push will not fix itself.

Does switching pay?#

Rex would tell you that moving jobs is the fastest way to a pay rise, and the internet agrees with him loudly. The data agree with him more quietly.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta tracks the median yearly wage growth of US workers, with separate series for people who have recently changed jobs and people who have not. We downloaded the data file and checked it ourselves. From January 1997 to September 2026, job switchers’ wage growth beat stayers’ in 91% of months, but by only 0.73 percentage points on average [6]. The gap moves with the economy: it peaked at 2.2 points in November 2022 [6], in a year when US job openings stood at record highs [7], and it disappeared, and reversed for more than a year, in 2009–10, after the financial crisis [6].

Three cautions keep this honest. First, these are medians, and the series counts people as switchers fairly broadly, including some who changed occupation or duties rather than employer [8]. Second, it is descriptive, not causal: the people who switch may differ from the people who stay, and some of them would probably have earned more anyway our view. Third, it is American; we did not find a comparable series for the UK, the EU or India that we could verify.

So switching tends to pay more, especially when employers are short of people. It is not a guaranteed raise, and in a slow market the premium can be close to zero.

Myth CheckJob hoppers always earn morePartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

In US data, switchers’ median wage growth has beaten stayers’ in about 91% of months since 1997, but by an average of 0.73 percentage points, and the gap reversed in 2009–10 and was close to zero in 2025 [6]. Frequent moves also have a cost that the slogan leaves out: in a field experiment, résumés listing fewer job changes received 40 to 50% more callbacks than otherwise identical résumés with more changes, and HR professionals read frequent changes as a sign of poorer reliability and cooperativeness [9]. Switching usually pays a little; switching constantly can cost you the next interview.

Rex’s pattern#

Which brings us back to Rex, and to the conversation that happens at Corvid on his last Friday, over a sad tray of supermarket samosas.

Bea Six jobs in eight years. Doesn’t that worry the people hiring him?
Dev Apparently not. He’s had three offers this month.
Bea Because he’s brilliant in interviews. But one day an interviewer is going to see all those jobs on one page and ask why.

Bea has put her finger on the cost. In the experiment above, the penalty did not come from hiring managers thinking job changers were less skilled; it came from them inferring a poorer work attitude, roughly whether the person would stick around and cooperate [9]. The authors also point out that moves can signal good things, such as ambition and breadth [9]. What decides which way a reader leans is whether the moves tell a story.

Some perspective helps here. In January 2026 the median US worker aged 25 to 34 had been with their current employer for 3.0 years [10]. A two- or three-year stint early in a career is close to normal, not a red flag. Rex’s problem is not that he moves; it is that each move is shorter than the last and none of them is explained by anything except the next offer. His trailer has six scenes and no plot.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

Make every move make sense. If you have changed jobs often, give each move a one-line reason that a stranger would accept: more scope, a new skill, a team that folded, a relocation. Put it in your story bank (Chapter 4) so it comes out the same way every time. Several stays of under a year in a row are where the signal turns negative; one short stay with a clear reason rarely matters. This is our reading of the evidence above, plus common practice, rather than a tested rule.

Timing and the runway#

Once you have decided to look, when and from where you look matter more than most people expect.

The clearest finding is that it pays to search from a job. In a large US survey run by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, employed job seekers received more offers per application than people who were not working, and better-paid offers even after controlling for their characteristics; per unit of effort, they were nearly three times as efficient at turning search into offers [11]. Some of that gap is about who happens to be unemployed, which the researchers model, but not all of it.

Employers can read current unemployment as a signal too, and some read it fast. In a US field experiment that sent about 12,000 fictitious résumés to 3,000 job ads in sales, customer service and clerical work, callbacks fell sharply over the first eight months of unemployment, to about 45% fewer at eight months than at one month, before levelling off [12]. Not every study agrees: a later US experiment with experienced, college-educated women applying for administrative jobs found no link at all between how long they had been out of work and their callbacks [13]. We will come back to what that means if you have already lost your job. For now, the lesson for someone like Dev is simple: if you can, start looking before you leave.

That makes a job search while employed a project with two constraints: time and money. Dev builds a new tab for them. She calls it Runway.

INT. DEV'S FLAT — TUESDAY, 11 PM

The Runway tab has four rows so far.

  • Savings: a number she rounds down on purpose.
  • Monthly spend: a number she rounds up, on purpose, then rounds up again because she has met herself.
  • Notice period: she opens her contract to check, and it is longer than she remembered.
  • Hours a week I can actually search: she types 10, deletes it, and types 6.
Dev Six hours. That’s one evening and a Saturday morning.

She stares at the notice period for a while, then adds a fifth row: Things that could make this faster. Under it she writes “people” and “a better résumé”, and underlines both.

A runway is just two numbers: how long you could last without income if things went wrong, and how long it will take to get from “yes” to a start date. The first decides how much risk you can take. The second decides how far ahead you need to start. A long notice period does not stop you searching, but it means an employer who needs someone next month will not choose you, and it means your search takes longer than the interview process alone suggests.

Field NoteNotice periods and non-competes

USUKGermanyIndia

Notice when you resign. The law sets a floor and your contract usually sets the actual period, so read your contract now: the notice period is part of your runway. In the US, most employment is “at will”, so notice is set by contract and policy rather than by a general legal minimum [14]. In the UK you must give at least a week once you have been in a job for a month [15]. In Germany, our verified example for the EU, the basic period is four weeks [16]. In India notice is set mainly by contract, and a law firm reports one to two months for non-managerial employees and three to six for senior staff [17]. “Leaving well” in Chapter 9 has the fuller rules, including garden leave and probation, for when you resign.

Non-competes, clauses that stop you working for a competitor after you leave, differ even more.

  • US. The Federal Trade Commission’s nationwide ban was struck down in court, and the appeal was dismissed in September 2025, so there is no nationwide ban and state law sets the main rules [18]. California voids most non-competes and bars employers from trying to enforce them [19].
  • UK. If your contract has a restrictive covenant, your employer can take you to court for breaching it [15]. A proposed three-month cap was never legislated, and the government reopened the question in late 2025 [20]; we found no outcome yet.
  • India. Agreements in restraint of trade are void under section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, and courts have held covenants that extend beyond the end of employment to be void, though restrictions during employment are treated differently [21].

We have not verified rules for other EU countries or other regions; check your contract and local official guidance. Chapter 9 returns to notice when you actually resign.

Searching quietly#

Searching while employed raises practical questions that nobody likes to ask out loud. Most of the answers are etiquette and common sense rather than research, so treat them as craft craft. Search on your own time and your own devices, because work accounts and laptops are your employer’s. Take interviews as holiday or at the edges of the day rather than inventing dentist appointments; a reasonable hiring manager will work round your current job, and one who will not is telling you something. Do not use your current manager as a reference until you have an offer, and say so to recruiters. And keep doing your job properly. You are still in the long game where you are, and the people watching it now are your future references.

Ask Anyway

QShould I tell my manager I’m looking?

AUsually not until you have an offer, because some managers will start planning around your departure. The exception is when the conversation you need is the internal one: if you would stay for the right move, say so plainly (“I want to grow into development; if that’s not possible here, I’ll need to look elsewhere”) without making it a threat. That is the conversation Your Move 2.3 asks you to draft.

QIsn’t searching while employed a bit disloyal?

ADoing your job badly while you search would be. Looking is not. Employment is an agreement both sides can end, and the evidence says you search far better from a job than without one [11]. Loyalty shows in how you leave (Chapter 9).

QWhat if I’ve only been here eight months?

AFrequent changes cost callbacks in experiments [9]; one short stay with a clear reason is, in our view, a much smaller thing our view. If the reason is real, say it in one sentence and move on. If the reason is “it’s hard”, ask whether the next job will be any easier at eight months.

Before any of that, though, you need your own numbers.

Your Move 2.2Your runway

Work out four numbers, roughly, and write them where you will see them.

  1. Months of savings: what you have, divided by what you actually spend in a month.
  2. Notice period: read your contract, not your memory.
  3. Search hours per week: what you can sustain for three months, not what you could do in a burst.
  4. Earliest realistic start date for a new job: today, plus the time a search usually takes in your field, plus your notice.

Then answer one question: if your job disappeared tomorrow, how many months would you have before money forced a decision?

Reveal one way to play it

There is no right number, but the shape of the answers tells you how to search. A long notice period with plenty of savings means you can be choosy and should start early. A short runway means your first job is to protect income: do not resign before you have an offer, and if you lose your job, start the search in the first week rather than the first month (see the next section for why). Most people overestimate their search hours: six honest hours a week, spent mostly on people and on tailored applications rather than on mass-applying, beats twenty that never happen. And if the notice row surprised you, as it surprised Dev, you are in good company; contracts are written to be signed, not remembered.

When the decision is made for you#

Everything so far assumes you get to choose. Many people enter the snapshot game without choosing at all: a reorganisation, a lost client, an email with a meeting invitation from someone in HR you have never met. If that is you, this section is the one to read first.

Start with something true that does not feel true at the time: a layoff is an event, not a verdict. Companies often cut roles for reasons that have little to do with the people in them our view. And the evidence we have points at time out of work, not at layoffs as such. In a Swedish field experiment with applicants in their twenties and early thirties, employers ignored even long spells of unemployment in an applicant’s past, and short current spells too, but marked down applicants who were currently out of work for nine months or more [22]. In the first US experiment above, callbacks fell sharply over the first eight months out of work [12], though the second found no such effect [13]. And many employers filter on gaps: 48% of employers in one large survey said they screened out middle-skills candidates with employment gaps of more than six months [23].

Put plainly: the evidence is mixed, but where a current gap hurts, it hurts more the longer it stays open. That is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to start.

It is also a real shock, and it is fine to treat it as one. A meta-analysis of studies of unemployment found that people out of work have lower psychological and physical well-being, and that coping resources and how people appraise their situation matter more than demographics [24]. One sobering long-run study of high-seniority men displaced in mass layoffs in Pennsylvania in the 1970s and 1980s found mortality 50 to 100% higher than expected in the following year, and still 10 to 15% higher twenty years later [25]. That is old data from one US state, about one group of workers, and it describes averages, not your future. But it is why the advice below starts with people and structure, not with your résumé.

Help works, and some kinds of help work better than others. A meta-analysis of 47 evaluated job search programmes found that participants had 2.67 times the odds of finding work compared with control groups, and that the effective programmes taught job search skills, improved self-presentation, built confidence, encouraged proactivity, set goals and enlisted social support [26]. Crucially, programmes worked only when they combined building skills with keeping motivation up, and they helped people who had been out of work for a short time more than the long-term unemployed [26]. Those findings are about structured programmes, not about a person working alone, but the ingredients are worth copying: skills, motivation, goals and other people, early.

Craft Note

Practitioner experience, not research

The first two weeks. A checklist drawn from the evidence above and from common practice; it is not legal advice, and the official links in the Field Note below come first.

  1. Read before you sign. If you are offered a severance or settlement agreement, ask for time to read it, and get advice if it waives legal claims.
  2. Claim what you are owed, quickly. Unemployment benefits, redundancy pay and final dues all have rules and deadlines.
  3. Tell people, today. Former colleagues, friends, your old team lead. Most will want to help, more than you expect (Chapter 6).
  4. Build a week. Fixed hours for searching, fixed hours for not searching, and something physical every day. Setting goals is one of the ingredients of effective programmes [26].
  5. Write your one-sentence explanation and practise it: “My role was one of 120 cut when the company closed its London office.” Then move straight on to what you did and what you want next.
  6. Fill the gap with evidence. A course, a project, some freelance work: anything you can talk about, because in some markets a current gap costs more callbacks the longer it runs [12].

Where the official rules exist, the deadlines in them are real, so look them up in the first week.

Field NoteIf you are laid off

USUKIndia

  • US. Employers with 100 or more employees generally must give 60 days’ written notice of a plant closing or mass layoff under the federal WARN Act, with exceptions [27; 28]. Unemployment insurance is run by each state within federal guidelines; the Department of Labor advises contacting your state’s programme “as soon as possible after becoming unemployed”, in the state where you worked [29].
  • UK. Employees with two or more years’ service are normally entitled to statutory redundancy pay, and have six months from the end of the job to claim it; redundancy can also bring a notice period, consultation and time off to look for work, and selection must be fair [30; 15].
  • India. The four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025, and a law-firm summary notes that wages due on resignation must now be paid within two working days, the same deadline as on termination [31]; we did not verify the rules on retrenchment compensation or unemployment benefits, so check current official guidance.

If you are one of the people who stayed, notice what usually happens next. Organisations that downsize tend to see more voluntary departures afterwards, because survivors’ commitment drops, though fair procedures soften the effect [32]. A layoff round is often a moment to ask the push-and-pull questions again, before everyone else does.

Myth CheckAny gap on your résumé is fatalPartly true

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Strong evidence

A current gap can cost callbacks: in one US experiment the cost grew over the first eight months [12], though another found no effect for experienced applicants [13]; some employers also filter out gaps longer than six months [23]. But a past gap that was followed by work, even a long one, showed no measurable effect in a Swedish field experiment with young applicants, while a current spell of nine months or more did [22]. A gap you have closed, and can explain in a sentence, is a small thing. A gap that is still open, and silent, is the one to work on.

Changing fields#

Dev is, in a small way, a career changer: she wants to move from testing software to building it. Bigger changes, from teaching to product management, from nursing to data analysis, from the army to almost anything, raise a fair worry. Does anything you have learned come with you?

More than people think, but not in the form of job titles. Economists who measured the tasks involved in different occupations found that skills are more portable than was previously assumed: people tend to move to occupations with similar task requirements, and the skills tied to tasks account for a large part of wage growth over a career [33]. The useful unit is not “I was a teacher”. It is “I explained hard things to thirty people at once, planned a year of work backwards from a deadline, and handled parents who were upset”. Some of those tasks transfer to product management almost unchanged.

That gives career changers a practical method our view. List the tasks you actually did, not your titles. Mark the ones the new field needs. Then find the gap, the tasks the new field needs that you have never done, and close it with evidence rather than with adjectives: a project, a course with a real output, a bridging role. Moves to an adjacent role, inside your company or out, are often easier than a leap, because more of your tasks come with you. Dev’s adjacent move is the one she asked for this afternoon: from testing the payments code to writing it.

Chapter 4 turns this into a method for translating experience without overselling it, and Chapter 5 puts the result on paper.

Boomerangs, and the door you leave through#

One more reason to take this decision seriously: in the long game, leaving is not the end.

Some people return to employers they left. A study of rehired managers in one large organisation found that these “boomerang” employees performed about the same as other hires in their first year but improved less over time, and were more likely to leave again, often for the same reasons as the first time [34]. That is a sobering result for anyone planning to “leave and come back for more money”: if the push was real, it will probably still be there.

How you leave matters too. Research on resignations finds that people resign in recognisably different styles, shaped by their relationships with their employer and their boss, and that the style affects how their managers feel about the departure [35]. People you leave behind become references, referrers and, sometimes, your future colleagues. Chapter 9 is about resigning well; for now, the rule is simply that the door you leave through stays in the building.

Stay, go, or grow#

Put the pieces together and you get a path you can walk in an evening.

Notice what the figure does not include: “go because you are fed up”, “go because a friend did”, “go because a recruiter called”. Those are feelings, and feelings are real data, but they are data about pushes and pulls you have not yet named. Name them, and the path works.

Notice too that stay is a legitimate answer. A push that gets fixed, a pull that turns out to be available inside, or a runway too short to risk a move are all good reasons to stay, at least for now. “Check again later” means it: put a date in your diary, six months out, and ask the questions again.

Whichever way the path points, the first move is usually the same: a conversation with the person who could make staying worth it.

Your Move 2.3The conversation before you leave

Draft the message or the opening lines you would use to ask for the thing that would make you stay. Use four parts: what you want, why you are ready (two pieces of evidence), a bridge (a way to try it) and a date. Keep it under 120 words. Then write one sentence describing how you will know the answer is a no.

Reveal one way to play it

A strong draft, using Dev’s case:

“I’d like to move into backend development, starting with the payments team. I think I’m ready: I rebuilt our regression suite, which meant reading most of the payments code, and I fixed the deployment scripts last spring. Could I pair with the payments developers for one sprint a month, alongside my testing work, starting next quarter? I’d like us to decide by the end of the planning round in March.”

And the test: “If there’s no date or no condition by the end of March, I’ll treat it as a no and start looking.”

What makes it strong: it names a specific move, gives evidence rather than enthusiasm, makes it cheap for the manager to say yes (a bridge, not a leap), and attaches a date. The final sentence protects you from waiting forever on a “maybe” our view. Notice what it leaves out: no threat, no list of complaints, no comparison with Rex’s salary. You can still leave later; this message makes sure you gave staying a real chance.

Dev sends hers.

INT. DEV'S FLAT — SUNDAY, 9 PM

The Should I stay? tab now has two columns, PUSH and PULL. The push column has three rows and the pull column has three. Next to “no route into dev” Dev has written asked Monday: “maybe next year”, no date.

The message goes to her manager with the bridge and the date in it. The reply comes on Wednesday: Let’s talk after planning. No date.

So she makes the decision she has been circling for a month. She will stay, do her job well, and look, properly, from here.

She texts Bea.

Dev I’m doing it. Searching while employed. Six hours a week, no dentist appointments.
Bea I’ve been “searching” for four months. I’ve sent nine applications and had four phone screens. All no.
Dev Then we’ll do it together. But first I need to understand why the noes happen.
Bea Because the game is rigged.
Dev Maybe. I want to see the rigging.

To see it, you have to sit on the other side of the table, which is where Chapter 3 takes Dev next.

Rewind
  • “I want to leave” bundles push (what is wrong here) and pull (what is right there). Pushes deserve a conversation before a search; pulls become your search criteria.
  • People who quit most often cite low pay, no advancement and feeling disrespected; “people leave managers” is only part of the story.
  • Ask before you leave, specifically and with a date. Internal moves keep the reputation you have built; external hires are paid more but start from zero.
  • Switching usually pays a little: in US data, switchers beat stayers in about 91% of months by about 0.7 points, and the premium can vanish in slack markets. A run of short stays costs callbacks.
  • Search from a job if you can: employed searchers get more offers per application and better ones. Know your runway: savings, notice period and real search hours.
  • A layoff is an event, not a verdict, but a current gap can cost callbacks, and more the longer it lasts. Start early, use people and structure, and close the gap with evidence.
  • Career changers carry tasks, not titles. Translate what you did into what the new field needs.
  • Stay is a legitimate answer. Whatever you choose, leave through a door you would be happy to walk back through.
