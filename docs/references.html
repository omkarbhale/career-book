How we grade evidence

Every factual claim in this book points at a source, and every source is graded for how much weight it can bear. Advice that rests on experience rather than research is labelled craft, and our own arguments are labelled our view.

  • Strong evidence Meta-analyses, large randomised or natural experiments, official statistics and the text of laws. Hard to overturn.
  • Moderate evidence Single well-designed studies, large surveys with clear methods, careful reviews. Probably right; could shift.
  • Weak evidence Small or non-random samples, vendor surveys, case studies, expert opinion. A hint, not a finding.
  • ✕Myth or unsupported Claims contradicted by stronger evidence, retracted, or with no traceable support; we cite these only to show where a myth comes from.

  • Axelrod, R., & Hamilton, W. D. (1981). The Evolution of Cooperation. Science, 211(4489), 1390–1396. doi.org/10.1126/science.7466396

    Strong evidence formal model + computer tournament opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: In an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, reciprocity-based cooperation (tit-for-tat style) can start in a mostly selfish population, thrive against many strategies, and resist invasion once established.

    Caveats: Strong as a model of when cooperation pays (repeated interaction, a future that matters). It is not workplace data; applying it to offices is an analogy the book must label as such.

  • Barrick, M. R., Shaffer, J. A., & DeGrassi, S. W. (2009). What you see may not be what you get: Relationships among self-presentation tactics and ratings of interview and job performance. Journal of Applied Psychology, 94(6), 1394–1411. doi.org/10.1037/a0016532 (copy read)

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Appearance, impression management and verbal and nonverbal behaviour influence interviewer ratings; "what you see in the interview may not be what you get on the job", and unstructured interviews are especially affected.

    Caveats: Abstract-level reading; effect sizes not quoted here.

  • Brown, M., Setren, E., & Topa, G. (2016). Do informal referrals lead to better matches? Evidence from a firm's employee referral system. Journal of Labor Economics, 34(1), 161-209. doi.org/10.1086/682338 (copy read)

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: In one large US corporation (62,127 applicants), referred candidates were 6.1% of applicants but 21.4% of interviewees, 27.3% of offer recipients and 29.1% of hires; job-board applicants were 60.1% of applicants but 23.5% of hires. Referred hires start with a wage advantage that dissipates by year 3, and stay longer. Also cites Pellizzari (2004): 25-40% of Europeans in most countries heard of their job via informal contacts.

    Caveats: One firm; descriptive stage shares are raw, not causal.

  • Cheng, C., Wu, Y., Wang, R., & Wang, Z. (2026). Seductive details, cognitive load, and learning outcomes: A multi-level meta-analysis and MASEM. Educational Psychology Review. eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1510171

    Strong evidence multi-level meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25 · cited in How to Use This Book

    Shows: Interesting but irrelevant additions ("seductive details") have a small negative effect on learning (g = -0.16 over 177 effect sizes from 50 studies), mainly by adding extraneous cognitive load.

    Caveats: Small average effect with moderators (sample size, language, learning environment).

  • Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(1), 87–114. doi.org/10.1017/s0140525x01003922 (copy read)

    Strong evidence theoretical review opened 2026-10-25 · cited in How to Use This Book

    Shows: Miller's "seven" was a rough estimate; under conditions where chunks can be counted, short-term memory holds about four chunks.

    Caveats: Capacity estimates depend on how chunks are defined and measured.

  • Dekker, S., Lee, N. C., Howard-Jones, P., & Jolles, J. (2012). Neuromyths in education: Prevalence and predictors of misconceptions among teachers. Frontiers in Psychology, 3, 429. doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00429 (copy read)

    Moderate evidence survey opened 2026-10-25 · cited in How to Use This Book

    Shows: Teachers interested in neuroscience believed 49% of neuromyths on average, including learning styles and "left-brain/right-brain" learners; more general brain knowledge predicted more belief in myths.

    Caveats: Self-selected sample of teachers interested in the brain.

  • Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266 (copy read)

    Strong evidence systematic review of ten techniques opened 2026-10-25 · cited in How to Use This Book

    Shows: Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility ratings; rereading and highlighting received low ratings because they do not consistently improve performance.

    Caveats: Focused on students learning material for later tests.

  • Ginns, P., Martin, A. J., & Marsh, H. W. (2013). Designing instructional text in a conversational style: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 25(4), 445–472. eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036785

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25 · cited in How to Use This Book

    Shows: Conversational-style versions of instructional text improved retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.54); the clearest boundary condition was time, with small, non-significant effects in studies longer than 35 minutes.

    Caveats: Abstract-level reading; most studies are short lab lessons.

  • GOV.UK (2026). Handing in your notice (giving notice; garden leave; restrictive covenants) and Redundancy: notice periods. gov.uk. www.gov.uk/handing-in-your-notice/giving-notice

    Strong evidence government opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Employee must give at least a week's notice after a month in the job (contract may require more); garden leave defined; restrictive covenants can be enforced in court; statutory employer notice - one week (1 month to 2 years), one week per year (2-12 years), 12 weeks (12+ years).

    Caveats: Great Britain; contracts commonly specify longer periods.

  • Hülsheger, U. R., & Schewe, A. F. (2011). On the costs and benefits of emotional labor: A meta-analysis of three decades of research. Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 16(3), 361–389. doi.org/10.1037/a0022876

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Surface acting (and feeling a clash between felt and required emotions) relates substantially to impaired well-being (rho .39-.48) and worse job attitudes; deep acting has weak links to strain and positive links to emotional performance and customer satisfaction.

    Caveats: Mostly correlational.

  • Khaitan & Co (2025). Lengthy notice periods in employment contracts: Understanding law and market trends. Khaitan & Co Compass (Indian law firm). compass.khaitanco.com/lengthy-notice-periods-in-employment-contracts-understanding-law-and-market-trends

    Moderate evidence web opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: In India non-managerial employees usually serve 1-2 months' notice, senior roles 3-6 months; direct judicial precedents on lengthy notice periods are limited; Supreme Court voided a termination clause under s.23 Contract Act in a particular case.

    Caveats: Law-firm commentary; year approximate (not shown in extracted text).

  • Legal Information Institute, & School, C. L. (2026). Employment-at-will doctrine (Wex). law.cornell.edu. www.law.cornell.edu/wex/employment-at-will_doctrine

    Strong evidence web opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: At-will is the US default; employment may be terminated by either party at any time and for almost any reason (subject to exceptions).

    Caveats: Legal encyclopedia, not statute; state exceptions vary (e.g., Montana).

  • Levashina, J., Hartwell, C. J., Morgeson, F. P., & Campion, M. A. (2014). The structured employment interview: Narrative and quantitative review of the research literature. Personnel Psychology, 67(1), 241-293. doi.org/10.1111/peps.12052 (copy read)

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Past-behaviour questions slightly more valid than situational (uncorrected .31 vs .25-.26 in cited meta-analyses); meta-analysis of impression management in structured interviews - self-promotion r=.26, nonverbal .18, other-focused .13, defensive .12; structure reduces but does not eliminate IM.

    Caveats: Validity values quoted from other meta-analyses are uncorrected.

  • Mayer, R. E., & Moreno, R. (2003). Nine ways to reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 43–52. doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_6 (copy read)

    Moderate evidence synthesis of the authors' experiments opened 2026-10-25 · cited in How to Use This Book

    Shows: Learners understand an explanation better when interesting but extraneous material is cut (coherence effect, median effect size .90 over 5 experiments), when cues signal the structure (.74), and when printed words sit next to the parts of the graphic they describe (.48).

    Caveats: Mostly short lessons with university students in one lab; later meta-analyses find smaller effects for some principles.

  • Powell, D. M., Stanley, D. J., & Brown, K. N. (2018). Meta-analysis of the relation between interview anxiety and interview performance. Canadian Journal of Behavioural Science. cannexus.ceric.ca/download/378/en/2735/deborah-powell-silvia-bonaccio-understanding-interview-anxiety-causes-coping-and-consequences.pdf

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: 30 studies; average correlation between self-reported interview anxiety and interviewer-rated performance r = -.19; moderated by measurement approach and mock vs real interviews.

    Caveats: Read via author slides; volume/pages not verified.

  • Rajkumar, K., Saint-Jacques, G., Bojinov, I., Brynjolfsson, E., & Aral, S. (2022). A causal test of the strength of weak ties. Science, 377(6612), 1304-1310. doi.org/10.1126/science.abl4476 (copy read)

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Randomised LinkedIn "People You May Know" experiments (2015, 2019) on 20M+ people; inverted-U between tie strength and job transmission; moderately weak ties (by mutual connections) most useful; weak ties matter more in digital industries, strong ties in less digital ones.

    Caveats: Job transmission = later working at the connection's company; per-tie probabilities are tiny (fractions of a percent). LinkedIn users only; users were not told they were in the experiments (see acs2022-linkedin).

    Conflicts: Two authors were LinkedIn employees.

  • Sackett, P. R., Zhang, C., Berry, C. M., & Lievens, F. (2022). Revisiting meta-analytic estimates of validity in personnel selection: Addressing systematic overcorrection for restriction of range. Journal of Applied Psychology, 107(11), 2040-2068. doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994 (copy read)

    Strong evidence article opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Revised validity estimates (Table 3) - structured interviews .42 (top), job knowledge .40, empirically keyed biodata .38, work samples .33, cognitive ability .31, integrity .31, assessment centres .29, unstructured interviews .19, years of job experience .07. Most estimates fall .10-.20 vs Schmidt & Hunter.

    Caveats: Contested by some meta-analysts (see ones2023); the ranking is more robust than the exact values. Validities are correlations with supervisor-rated performance, not certainty about any one hire.

  • Sackett, P. R., Zhang, C., Berry, C. M., & Lievens, F. (2023). Revisiting the design of selection systems in light of new findings regarding the validity of widely used predictors. Industrial and Organizational Psychology, 16(3), 283–300. doi.org/10.1017/iop.2023.24 (copy read)

    Strong evidence focal article summarising a meta-analytic revision opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Structured interviews top the revised list with a mean validity of .42, but with an 80% credibility interval from .18 to .66 ("really .42, plus or minus .24"); unstructured interviews were revised down by .19; predictors specific to the job (structured interviews, job knowledge, work samples, biodata) beat general measures.

    Caveats: Validities are for single predictors used alone; combinations of predictors do better.

  • Spence, A. M. (2001). Signaling in retrospect and the informational structure of markets. Prize Lecture, Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, 8 December 2001. www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/spence-lecture.pdf

    Strong evidence author's retrospective of an economic model opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: "The value of individuals to firms is not directly observed, at least at the time of hiring"; a signal separates people only when its cost is negatively correlated with the unseen quality employers value.

    Caveats: A model of markets; applying it to an individual's job search is the book's interpretation.

  • Sutton, A. (2020). Living the good life: A meta-analysis of authenticity, well-being and engagement. Personality and Individual Differences, 153, 109645. doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2019.109645

    Strong evidence meta-analysis opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Felt authenticity correlates with well-being (r = .40) and work engagement (r = .37); relationships vary with individualism.

    Caveats: Correlational; direction of cause unclear (feeling good may make people feel authentic).

  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2025). Number of jobs, labor market experience, marital status, and health for those born 1957-1964 (news release USDL-25-1322, 26 August 2025). www.bls.gov/news.release/nlsoy.nr0.htm

    Strong evidence longitudinal survey (National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979) opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: People born 1957-64 held an average of 12.9 jobs from ages 18 to 58: 5.6 at ages 18-24 and 4.5 at ages 25-34, falling to 1.3 at 55-58.

    Caveats: One US cohort; "jobs" counts employers, and jobs spanning age groups are counted in each. Younger cohorts and other countries may differ.

  • US Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). Employee tenure in 2026 (news release USDL-26-1532, 24 September 2026). bls.gov. www.bls.gov/news.release/tenure.nr0.htm

    Strong evidence government opened 2026-10-25 · cited in Chapter 1

    Shows: Median tenure with current employer 4.1 years in January 2026 (3.9 in January 2024); ages 25-34 3.0 years; ages 55-64 9.6 years; public sector 5.6 vs private 3.9.

    Caveats: US only; median, not distribution.
