INT. BEA'S KITCHEN — SUNDAY, 2 PM

Bea’s kitchen table is covered in laptops, mugs and a plate of biscuits that is disappearing faster than the work. Dev has brought her résumé. Bea has brought hers, reluctantly, as if it might bite.

Dev Read me your best bullet.
Bea “Responsible for maintenance and enhancement of the billing engine.”
Dev Bea. You rewrote the billing engine. In a fortnight. Nobody else could have done it.
Bea That’s what maintenance and enhancement means.
Dev It means nothing. It’s what the job ad said. What actually happened?

Bea thinks about it. She takes a biscuit. She thinks some more.

Bea Month-end billing used to take the finance team two days of manual fixes. After the rewrite it ran overnight and nobody had to fix anything. They sent a cake.
Dev That’s the bullet. Where’s the cake on your CV?
Bea You don’t put cake on a CV.
Dev You put what the cake was for.

Both of their résumés, it turns out, describe jobs. Neither describes a person. Dev’s says she was “responsible for regression testing” and “involved in release activities”. Bea’s says she “maintained” and “enhanced” things. Every word is true, and almost none of it is evidence.

This chapter is about the material you bring to the snapshot game before you write a word of a résumé or answer a single interview question: an inventory of what you have actually done, a bank of true stories, and a short list of targets chosen on purpose. Most of it is craft rather than research, and we label it that way. But it rests on two findings you have already met: strong signals are specific, checkable and costly to fake, and interviews mostly ask you to describe what you have done before.

Pause

Think of the best thing you did at work, or in your studies, in the last year. Now try to say what changed because of it, in one sentence, with a number in it. If you can, good. If you cannot, hold on to that feeling: the first section is for you.

Duties are not evidence#

Flashback“Signals, not secrets” in Chapter 1

In Chapter 1, Spence’s idea of a signal did the work: a claim tells an employer something only if it is harder to make for people who lack the quality it advertises [1].

A duty is what your employer paid you to do. It is in your job description, and it is the same for everyone who held the job, whether they did it brilliantly or barely. That is exactly why it carries no information. “Responsible for regression testing” is true of the best tester at Corvid and the worst.

Evidence is what changed because you did the job. It is specific to you, it can be checked, and it invites the questions that only the person who did the work can answer. Turning one into the other takes three questions.

What changed? Something was different after you did the work: faster, cheaper, safer, clearer, possible for the first time. If nothing changed, it may still have been valuable work, but it is not your best evidence.

By how much? A number makes a claim checkable and helps a stranger judge its size. “Faster” could mean anything; “from six hours to 40 minutes” means something.

Who noticed? The people who benefited: the team, a client, finance, users. “The team now tests every merge” tells the reader the change mattered to someone other than you.

What if there’s no number?#

Plenty of good work does not come with a dashboard. Numbers are one kind of specificity, not the only one craft. You can use before and after (“month-end used to need two days of manual fixes; now it runs overnight”), scale (“for a client with 3 million policyholders”), frequency (“every release, for two years”) or firsts (“the first automated test of the claims API”). Estimates are fine if you label them honestly and can explain how you got them. Invented precision is not: “improved efficiency by 37.2%” invites exactly the question you cannot answer.

That is the pause question’s answer. If your best piece of work comes out as a duty, put it through the three questions until it comes out as a change. If you still cannot find one, that is worth knowing too: it may be a sign to collect better evidence in your current job, starting now, which is what Dev’s Wins tab is for in Chapter 11.

Your Move 4.1Duty to evidence

Rewrite each of these duties as evidence. You will have to invent the details, so make them plausible. Then do the same for three duties from your own résumé.

  1. “Handled customer queries via email and phone.”
  2. “Assisted with the migration to a new CRM system.”
  3. “Participated in code reviews.”
Reveal one way to play it

Strong rewrites share a shape: an action you took, a change it caused, and a sign that someone cared.

  1. “Answered about 40 customer queries a day; wrote the six template replies that the team now uses for the most common problems, cutting average reply time from two days to same-day.” The duty became a change (reply time), with scale (40 a day) and a contribution only one person made (the templates).
  2. “Mapped 18,000 customer records from the old system to the new one and wrote the checks that caught 900 duplicates before go-live; the sales team started on the new system without a cleanup week.” “Assisted” hides what you did; this says it.
  3. “Reviewed about ten pull requests a week; started a short checklist for database changes after two outages, and we have not had another schema-related outage since.” “Participated” is a duty; the checklist is a contribution.

For your own: if the rewrite needs a number you do not have, use before-and-after, scale or frequency instead. If it needs a claim you could not defend for five minutes under questioning, cut the claim, not the honesty.

Mining your own film#

The hard part is not writing evidence; it is finding it. Most people remember their work as a blur of tasks, and the best things they did are often the ones they have forgotten, because at the time they were just solving the problem in front of them. Bea’s billing rewrite felt like a busy fortnight, not a story.

So do not rely on memory alone. Go looking craft:

  • Your own records: old emails and chat messages, tickets you closed, documents you wrote, release notes, your calendar for the busiest weeks.
  • Other people’s reactions: thank-you messages, a manager’s comments in a review, a client email forwarded “for info”, the cake.
  • The before-and-after: anything that was broken, slow or manual when you arrived and is not now.
  • Other people’s memories: ask two colleagues, “What do you think I’m best at? What’s something I did that you remember?” People often remember your best work better than you do, precisely because it helped them.

This is your film, as other people saw it. In the long game, it was being shot whether you noticed or not. The snapshot game asks you to go back through the footage and find your best scenes.

The story bank#

Bullets are for paper. Interviews want stories, and there is a good reason to prepare them in advance.

Many structured interviews use past-behaviour questions: “Tell me about a time when you…” The idea is that how you handled real situations is good evidence of how you will handle the next ones. Research reviews find that both past-behaviour questions and situational questions (“What would you do if…”) predict job performance, and that past-behaviour questions do slightly better on average [2]; “Two kinds of question” in Chapter 7 has the numbers. Whatever the exact figures, if you are interviewed by a well-run company you will be asked to describe what you actually did, many times, under some pressure.

You cannot predict every question, but you do not need to. A small set of good stories covers most of them, because most behavioural questions are asking about the same few things: a hard problem, a mistake, a disagreement, leading without authority, learning something fast, working under pressure. A story bank of eight to twelve true stories, each of which can be bent to answer several of those questions, is enough for most interview loops craft. That number is practitioner experience, not a tested figure; what matters is that every kind of question has at least one story ready, and preferably two.

Each story in the bank needs four parts, in this order: the situation (one or two sentences of context, no more), what you did (the bulk of the story, in the first person), the result (what changed, with your evidence from the first section) and what you learned or would do differently. This is a widely taught answer structure, often abbreviated as STAR (situation, task, action, result) craft; Chapter 7 shows how to tell it out loud, under time pressure, and how interviewers score it.

“I”, not “we”#

Most work is done in teams, and most people describe it that way. In an interview, that is a problem: the interviewer is hiring you, not your team, and every “we” leaves them guessing which part was yours.

Trapdoor

Hiding in “we”, or hiding behind it. Two opposite mistakes cost the same thing. The modest version says “we rebuilt the suite” when you designed it and did most of the work, and the interviewer gives the credit to people they will never meet. The inflated version says “I rebuilt the suite” when you wrote one module, and the follow-up questions find the edge of what you know. The fix for both is the same: describe the team’s goal in a sentence, then say exactly what you did, in the first person, and name what others did. “The team was rebuilding the suite; I designed the parallel runner and wrote the scheduling code; two colleagues migrated the old tests.” Accurate credit is more convincing than either version.

There is evidence that self-promotion works in interviews, which makes the inflated version tempting: as “What else the interview is measuring” in Chapter 1 showed, it raises ratings even in structured interviews [2]. But that is the trailer game, and the trailer has to survive the film. A story bank built from true stories, told with accurate credit, lets you promote yourself honestly: you are not adding anything, you are just declining to leave things out. Chapter 5 takes that argument further, with Bea, who would rather not promote herself at all.

Your Move 4.2Your first five stories

Draft five stories for your bank. For each, write a title of three to five words, the four parts (situation, what you did, result, what you learned) in no more than 120 words in total, and tick which of the six columns from Figure 4.2 it could answer. Then look for the empty column.

Reveal one way to play it

Good first drafts usually have three problems, and all three are fixable.

  • Too much situation. The context should take one or two sentences. If the story takes a paragraph to get started, the interviewer is still waiting for you to appear in it.
  • Too little you. Count the sentences that start with “I”. If the answer is one, rewrite with you in the middle of the action.
  • A missing result. “And then it went live” is not a result. What changed? Use the three questions from Figure 4.1.

The empty column is the most useful thing this exercise gives you. Most people have plenty of “hard problem” stories and few about mistakes or disagreements, because those are less comfortable to remember. They are also exactly the stories that are hard to fake, which is why careful interviewers like to ask for them craft. Find a true one; a mistake you owned and learned from is a good story, not a bad one (Chapter 12 has the evidence on how mistakes are actually judged).

Finally, a check: could each story survive five minutes of follow-up questions? If not, it is not ready, or it is not quite true.

Choosing targets#

Evidence is only half of what you are selling. The other half is where you sell it. Chapter 3 showed that the chance per application matters more than the number of applications; the cheapest way to raise that chance is to apply where you are a strong fit, and to know why.

A useful target sits where three things overlap: what you want (your pull list from “Push and pull” in Chapter 2), what they need (the must-haves in the ads you are reading, decoded as in Your Move 3.3) and what you can show (your evidence and your story bank).

Each of the outer overlaps is worth naming, because each is a common mistake our view. What you want and can show, but nobody needs is a passion without a market, at least locally; keep it, but do not build a search on it. What they need and you can show, but you do not want is the job you would get easily and leave quickly, which is how a career fills up with short stints (“Rex’s pattern” in Chapter 2). What you want and they need, but you cannot yet show is a stretch, and it is not a dead end: it is a plan. Build the evidence (a project, a course with a real output, an internal move) and it moves into the middle.

Dev’s middle is narrower than she would like. She wants backend development. Employers need people who can write production code, and many of them say “three years of experience”. What she can show is test automation, a lot of code reading, scripts and one parallel test runner. So her targets are roles where her testing background is an asset, not a footnote: teams that care about reliability, quality engineering roles with a development path, companies whose ads mention testing in their backend requirements.

Close is often close enough#

How closely do you need to match an ad before you apply? You have probably heard a confident answer to that question, with numbers attached.

Myth CheckWomen only apply when they meet 100% of the requirements; men apply at 60%Busted

Strength of the evidence behind this verdict: Moderate evidence

The figure was popularised by a 2014 Harvard Business Review article, which attributed it to an internal Hewlett-Packard report [3]. We found no public copy of that report, and a researcher who traced the claim back to an earlier McKinsey article was told by its first author that it came from confidential interviews with executives, and that he had no access to the HP document [4 weak]. When researchers tested the claim experimentally, their first study found no gender difference in intentions to apply, at either 60% or 100% fit; later studies found a gender difference that was significant but not robust, while women did consistently report a stronger wish to feel prepared [5]. Those studies measured intentions, not real applications, so the last word is not in. But the famous 60/100 split is folklore with a footnote that leads nowhere.

The useful question is not about gender; it is about what “requirements” mean. Some lines in an ad are true knockouts: the right to work in the country, a licence, a core skill the job cannot be done without. Others are wishes. Employers themselves admit that their filters on exact criteria screen out qualified people, with 88% agreeing in one large survey [6]. And “years of experience”, one of the most common requirements, is one of the weakest predictors of performance, at .07 [7].

So a reasonable rule, which is our view rather than a tested threshold our view: apply when you meet the knockouts exactly and can show real evidence for most of the rest. Then use your application to show it, rather than hoping the reader infers it.

Your Move 4.3A target list of ten

List ten specific targets: a role at a named organisation, or, if you are early in your search, a role type in a named kind of organisation. For each, write:

  1. which of your pull reasons it satisfies;
  2. its knockouts, and whether you meet each one exactly;
  3. the two stories from your bank that best fit it;
  4. a route in: anyone you know there, or anyone who knows someone.

Then sort the list into “middle” (apply now) and “stretch” (build evidence first).

Reveal one way to play it

A strong list is uneven, and that is the point. Some targets will have a clear route in and a perfect fit; others will need a story you do not have yet. Three patterns are worth watching for.

  • Pull reasons missing. If a target satisfies none of your pulls, it belongs in “jobs you’d get, then regret”. Cross it off, however good the pay.
  • Knockouts you nearly meet. “Nearly” is not a match for a knockout. Right to work, licences and required certifications are often filtered by software (“What the software actually does” in Chapter 3); do not tick boxes you do not meet. Soft requirements are different: if you can show the evidence, apply.
  • No route in. If none of your ten has a person attached, that is your next job, before more applications. Chapter 6 is about finding and asking those people honestly.

Ten is a manageable number to research properly. It is not a limit: once the first ten are under way, add more.

Your one-line answer#

Sooner or later, someone will ask the question every job seeker dreads: “So, what do you do?” A recruiter on the phone, an old colleague at a wedding, the first two minutes of an interview. Most people answer with their current title, which is a duty again, or with a nervous tour of everything they have ever done.

Once you have your evidence and your targets, you can do better, in one or two sentences with three parts craft: what you do now, the evidence that you are good at it, and what you want next. It sits right in the middle of the overlap in Figure 4.3.

Dev’s first attempt was “I’m a QA engineer at Corvid, but I’m looking to move into development.” It is true, and it invites the listener to think of her as a tester who wants something she has not got. Her second:

“I build the test tooling for an insurance payments platform: I cut our regression run from six hours to forty minutes. Now I want to build the backend systems themselves, ideally somewhere that cares about reliability.”

The second version does three things the first does not. It leads with work rather than a title, it puts one checkable piece of evidence where a stranger will remember it, and it names a target specific enough that a listener can think “I know someone who needs that”. That last part is what turns a pleasant conversation into a referral, which matters a great deal in Chapter 6. The same lines become the top of a résumé in Chapter 5.

Changing fields: translate, don’t oversell#

Flashback“Changing fields” in Chapter 2

Chapter 2 showed that skills travel between occupations as tasks, not titles: people tend to move to occupations with similar task requirements [8].

If you are changing fields, your evidence exists but is written in the wrong language. A teacher’s “planned and delivered lessons” is, in a product team’s language, “planned a year of work backwards from fixed deadlines and explained complex material to audiences who did not want to hear it”. Both are true. Only one is legible to the new reader.

Translation has a failure mode, and it is overselling. A teacher who writes “product manager for a 30-person classroom” is not translating; they are claiming a title they did not hold, and an interviewer will notice. The line between the two is the same test as always: could you be questioned about it for five minutes, by someone from the new field, and come out well? Translate the task, keep the title true craft.

Dev’s version of this is smaller but real. On paper she is a tester. In fact she has read most of the payments code, written thousands of lines of test automation, built a parallel test runner and fixed the deployment scripts. Translated for a backend team, her best evidence is not “found 212 defects” but “understood the payments system well enough to find its defects before the client did, and wrote the tooling that made it faster to test”.

For technical roles, there is one more kind of evidence worth knowing about: visible work. For a career changer, a new graduate or anyone whose title undersells them, a small, real project with a history behind it is a costly signal in exactly Spence’s sense. “Profiles, portfolios and code” in Chapter 5 looks at the evidence on what portfolios do and do not prove, and Chapter 8 at showing your work in interviews.

Ask Anyway

QAll my best work is confidential. What can I say?

ADescribe the shape without the secrets: the kind of problem, your role, the scale, and the result in relative terms (“cut processing time by about two-thirds for a large insurer”). Interviewers hear this often, and discretion reads as professionalism craft. What you cannot do is invent a public version of a private result.

QIsn’t a rehearsed story bank just a performance? I thought this book was against acting.

ARehearsing is not acting. Acting is pretending to be someone you are not; rehearsing is making sure the person you are comes across clearly under pressure. Every story in the bank is true. You are choosing which scenes to show and practising the cut, which is exactly what a good trailer is.

QWhat if I genuinely haven’t done anything impressive?

AThen lower the bar for “impressive” to “specific”. Helping a new colleague get productive in two weeks instead of two months is a story. So is a mistake you caught, a process you made less painful, or a skill you learned fast because you had to. If you still come up short, that is information for your current job: start collecting better evidence now (Chapter 11, Chapter 18).

Back at the kitchen table, Bea has been busy.

INT. BEA'S KITCHEN — SUNDAY, 7 PM

The biscuits are gone. On Bea’s screen is a spreadsheet Dev made for her, with a tab called Story bank. Twelve rows.

Bea Twelve.
Dev Twelve. You’ve been sitting on twelve.
Bea The billing rewrite. The outage at 3 a.m. The intern who couldn’t get the build working. The time I told the CTO the migration plan wouldn’t work, and it didn’t. I’ve never told anyone most of these.
Dev You’ve never told interviewers. Your whole team knows them. They’re just not written down anywhere a stranger can see.

Bea scrolls up and down the list, the way you look at a photo of yourself you did not know existed.

Bea Fine. But I’m not bragging about them.
Dev That’s next week’s problem.

Dev’s own story bank has nine rows and one empty column, led, no title. She has one story for it, about training a new tester, and she knows it is thin. She adds a note: ask the release team what they remember.

That is what this chapter produces: an inventory of evidence, a bank of true stories, and a list of targets where the two meet what employers need. It is the raw material for everything else in Part I: the résumé in Chapter 5, the stories you tell in Chapter 7, the trailer that will finally match the film. And it does not stop being useful when you get the job. Kept up to date for a year, the same story bank becomes most of Dev’s first self-review, in Chapter 18.

Rewind
  • Duties are not evidence. A duty is the same for everyone who held the job; evidence is what changed because you did it.
  • Turn duties into evidence with three questions: What changed? By how much? Who noticed? If there is no number, use before-and-after, scale, frequency or firsts. Never invent precision.
  • Mine your film: records, reactions, before-and-afters and colleagues’ memories find stories that your own memory has filed as “just work”.
  • Interviews often ask about past behaviour, which predicts slightly better than hypotheticals. A story bank of eight to twelve true stories, each answering several kinds of question, covers most of them.
  • Say “I”, not “we”: describe the team’s goal, then exactly what you did. Accurate credit beats both modesty and inflation.
  • Choose targets where what you want, what they need and what you can show overlap. The 60/100 application rule is folklore; meet the knockouts exactly and show evidence for most of the rest.
  • Have a one-line answer: what you do now, one piece of evidence, and what you want next.
  • Career changers translate tasks and keep titles true. Visible work can help where your title undersells you.
