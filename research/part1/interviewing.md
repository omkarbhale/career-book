# Interviewing: behavioural, technical, anxiety, first impressions, fit, questions, legality

Research notes for Part I. Keys refer to `sources.yaml`. Grades are defined in `README.md`. The validity of interview formats is in `selection-methods.md`.

## The one-paragraph version

An interview is a noisy measurement, and structure makes it less noisy. Past-behaviour questions ("tell me about a time…") are about as valid as anything employers use. That is the honest case for preparing STAR-shaped stories. The STAR acronym itself has no well-documented origin. Technical interviews have a measured anxiety problem: in one randomised trial, performance on a whiteboard problem halved when an interviewer watched. Anxiety correlates about −.19 with interview ratings across 30 studies. First impressions in the opening minutes do predict later ratings and offers. But the famous thin-slice studies were about *teachers*, not interviews, and "you're judged in 7 seconds" is folklore. Power posing doesn't do what the TED talk said. "Culture fit" often means "like me". The best-documented study of elite firms found it often outweighed productivity.

## Findings

### 1. Behavioural interviews and STAR
- **Evidence for the format** (strong): past-behaviour questions predict performance about as well as situational questions, slightly better on average. Mean uncorrected validities: .31 vs .25–.26 in the meta-analyses reviewed by `levashina2014`. Structured interviews top the revised validity table (`sackett2022`, .42).
- **Impression management works** (strong): self-promotion correlates r = .26 with structured-interview ratings (`levashina2014`). Preparing clear accounts of what *you* did isn't gaming the system. It's what the system measures.
- **The STAR acronym** (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Its origin is **unverified**. Commonly attributed to Development Dimensions International (DDI). Some secondary sources also credit psychologist Tom Janz's behaviour-description interviewing. No primary source was opened that settles it. *Recommendation:* describe STAR as "a widely taught answer structure" and attribute nothing.
- *Author view:* prepare 6–8 true stories that cover conflict, failure, ambiguity, influence without authority, a technical or quality call, and delivering under pressure. Make each about 60–90% "Action" in your telling. The ratio is practitioner advice and is unmeasured.

### 2. Technical interviews: coding, take-homes, system design
- **Stress effect** (moderate; small RCT): `behroozi2020` (ESEC/FSE 2020). A randomised controlled trial with **48 CS students** comparing a private whiteboard setting with one where an interviewer watched. "Performance is reduced by more than half, by simply being watched by an interviewer". Stress and cognitive load were significantly higher in the traditional format. Caveats: students, small N, one task.
- **Developer perceptions** (weak–moderate; qualitative): `behroozi2019` (VL/HCC 2019) analysed Hacker News discussions. Concerns included "lack of real-world relevance, bias towards younger developers, and demanding time commitment", along with anxiety and "arbitrary, implicit, and obscure norms".
- **Volatility** (weak; commercial platform data): `interviewingio2016`. On interviewing.io, the same people interviewing repeatedly with different interviewers showed large score swings. "Roughly 20% of interviewees are consistent in their performance… the rest are all over the place." Self-selected users, mostly practice interviews. Run by a company that sells interview practice.
- **Brainteasers** (moderate; employer testimony): Google's then-SVP of People Operations Laszlo Bock told the *New York Times* in 2013: "brainteasers are a complete waste of time". He said what works "are structured behavioral interviews, where you have a consistent rubric" (`abc2013-google`).
- **Take-homes and system design:** no rigorous comparative validity evidence was found for software-specific take-homes or system-design rounds. They resemble work samples, which have a revised validity of .33 (`sackett2022`). Their costs to candidates (time) are a recurring complaint (`behroozi2019`). Grade the debate as **practitioner opinion**.
- *Author view on practice:* rehearse under observation (mock interviews, thinking aloud), because the observed condition is the bottleneck, not knowledge (`behroozi2020`). Treat any single loop as one noisy sample (`interviewingio2016`). Ask for the format in advance; many companies will tell you.

### 3. Interview anxiety. Grade: strong
- `powell2018` (*Canadian Journal of Behavioural Science*; summarised in the first author's own conference slides). A meta-analysis of **30 studies**: average correlation between self-reported interview anxiety and interviewer-rated performance **r = −.19**. Moderated by measurement approach and by mock vs real interviews.
- Interpretation: anxiety costs something, but much less than anxious candidates fear. The interviewing.io self-assessment post (`interviewingio-selfassess`) reports people are poor judges of their own interview performance. That is weak evidence (commercial and unsourced in detail), but it is consistent with this.

### 4. First impressions and "thin slices"
- **In interviews** (strong): `barrick2010` (*JAP*): candidates who made better impressions in the rapport-building opening got higher interviewer ratings (r = .42) and more internship offers (r = .22). Judgments of competence carried over even to separate interviewers. Liking and similarity did not.
- `swider2016` (*JAP*, 163 mock interviews): "a significant portion of initial impressions' influence overlaps with job-related interview content". The effects are "distinct from other image-related constructs" and relate more strongly to ratings of early rather than late answers. (The BPS Research Digest write-up stresses an "image" inflation effect. The abstract is more measured, so follow the abstract.) The upshot: the opening chit-chat partly carries real signal about verbal and social skill, and partly leaks into how your first answers are scored.
- **The handshake** (moderate): `stewart2008` (*JAP*): 98 students in mock interviews; five trained raters scored handshakes. Handshake quality was associated with interviewer hiring recommendations and mediated the effect of extraversion. Small student sample.
- **Thin slices proper** (strong for what it shows): `ambady1993`. Strangers' ratings of **silent clips of college teachers under 30 seconds** (and 6 or 15 seconds in Study 3) predicted end-of-semester student evaluations. This is about teaching, not hiring.
- **Overstated:** "interviewers decide in 7 seconds / 90 seconds" has no interview-specific source. The studies show early impressions *correlate* with outcomes and leak into later ratings. They do not show decisions are final at second N. Grade: **myth-or-unsupported** as a precise timing.
- **Power posing** (myth-or-unsupported for hormonal and behavioural effects): Dana Carney, first author of the 2010 study, wrote publicly: "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real" (`carney2016`). A meta-analysis of 88 studies and 9,779 participants found effects on self-reported feelings of power, but "no reliable effects on physiological outcomes", and behavioural effects likely inflated by publication bias (Körner et al. 2022, via `forrt-powerposing`).
- *Author view:* the opening minutes count because people are people. So be on time, warm, and ready with a crisp answer to "tell me about yourself". Don't believe any trick works on cortisol.

### 5. "Culture fit". Grade: strong (qualitative, specific setting)
- `rivera2012` (*American Sociological Review*): 120 interviews with hiring professionals at elite US investment banks, law firms and consultancies, plus participant observation. Employers sought candidates "culturally similar to themselves in terms of leisure pursuits, experiences, and self-presentation styles". Shared culture "often outweighed concerns about absolute productivity".
- Implications: (a) for candidates, legitimate common ground helps, and pretending doesn't; (b) for Part II, "fit" assessed without structure is a known bias channel. Prefer explicit values and behaviours to "would I have a beer with them".

### 6. Questions to ask the interviewer. Grade: practitioner opinion
- No rigorous evidence was found that asking questions changes offers. *Author view:* ask questions that help *you* decide. Make them about the work (what the first 90 days look like), the team (how decisions get made and disagreements resolved), the manager (how they give feedback) and the risks (why the role is open). Part II's first-principles conduct starts here: you're interviewing them too.

### 7. Illegal or improper interview questions by region
- **US** (strong; regulator text): `eeoc-prohibited`. Pre-employment information "should be limited to those essential for determining if a person is qualified". Race, sex, national origin, age and religion "are irrelevant". "Employers are explicitly prohibited from making pre-offer inquiries about disability." Most other questions aren't banned outright by federal law, but "may be used as evidence of an employer's intent to discriminate". The EEOC says employers should not ask for a photograph before an offer. Protected characteristics include sex (including transgender status, sexual orientation and pregnancy), age 40+, disability and genetic information. State laws add more; salary-history bans are covered in `offers-negotiation.md`.
- **UK** (strong; government answer): it is "generally unlawful under the Equality Act 2010… for an employer to ask a job applicant about their health or disability… during the initial stages of a recruitment process, except in a few specific circumstances" (`ukparl2020-health`). Employers may make an offer conditional on health checks. The statute itself (s.60) is on legislation.gov.uk, which blocked automated access; that page was not opened.
- **EU:** the strong regional item is pay transparency, not interview questions (see `offers-negotiation.md`). Not separately verified here.
- **India:** no verified general statute restricting private-sector interview questions was located during this research. Leave out or mark as unverified. Constitutional equality protections (Arts 15–16) apply mainly to the state; not opened here, so **unverified**.
