# Channels: cold applications, recruiters and agencies, internal moves, timing

Research notes for Part I. Referrals are in `referrals.md` and networking in `networking.md`. Keys refer to `sources.yaml`.

## The one-paragraph version

Every channel works some of the time, at very different yields. Cold applications through job boards are the biggest source of applicants and still a quarter to two-fifths of hires. They have the lowest yield per application. Being approached by a recruiter, called "sourced", converts about 5× better than applying, mostly because someone already pre-screened you. Internal moves are a large and efficient channel. External hires are paid more but rated lower for their first two years. Agencies are paid by the employer, typically a share of first-year salary. Applying early in a posting's life is associated with better odds, but the evidence is thin.

## Findings

### 1. Cold applications (job boards, career sites). Grade: moderate
- Share of applications vs share of hires:
  - `brown2016`: internet job boards were 60.1% of applicants and 23.5% of hires. Firm website was 14.8% and 10.6%.
  - `gem2025`: job boards and sourcing sites were 48.98% of applications and 24.55% of hires.
  - `careerplug2025`: job boards were 61.3% of applicants and 43.3% of hires (small, mostly hourly employers).
- Inbound applicant hire rate was 0.4% in 2024 (`gem2025`).
- *Author view:* cold applications are a volume game with low yield. Their yield goes up when the application is specific to the role, and when it's paired with a contact (see `referrals.md`).

### 2. Being sourced by a recruiter. Grade: moderate (vendor data)
- `gem2025`: sourced (outbound) candidates passed the first stage at 44%, against 6% for inbound. They were hired at 2.0% against 0.4%. Sourcing was 2.47% of applications and 9.94% of hires.
- The effect is mostly selection: the recruiter picked you. Gem sells sourcing tools.
- Implication: a findable profile (LinkedIn, a portfolio, GitHub for tech) is a channel in itself. See `positioning.md`.

### 3. Agencies and third-party recruiters. Grade: weak (industry sources)
- Agencies are paid by the employer. The fee is usually a percentage of the hire's first-year salary. **Contingent** search is paid only on a hire. **Retained** search is paid in instalments and usually costs more (`topechelon-fees`). That source is a vendor to agencies and its examples are illustrative. Its examples use 18–25%.
- Agency was a minor source of hires in `gem2025`'s customer data.
- *Author view, labelled:* the agency's incentive is a placement, quickly. That is often aligned with yours, but not on salary ceilings or on which job is best for you. Legitimate agencies in the US, UK and EU don't charge candidates. In the UK, the Conduct of Employment Agencies Regulations govern fees (see `regional.md` if verified there).

### 4. Internal moves. Grade: moderate (a peer-reviewed study read via a secondary write-up, plus vendor corroboration)
- `bidwell2011`: personnel data from a US investment-banking division, 2003–2009, as reported by Knowledge at Wharton. External hires were paid "about 18% to 20% more" than internal promotions into similar jobs. They received "significantly lower performance evaluations for their first two years" and had higher exit rates. If they stayed beyond two years, they were promoted faster. Similar results appeared at another investment bank and a publishing company.
- `gem2025`: internal candidates were 0.27% of applications and 12.56% of hires.
- Implication for the book: switching employers is often the faster way to a pay bump (`switching.md`). Moving internally carries lower risk and keeps your accumulated reputation. That trade-off is the tension of the "Switching" chapter.
- Caveat: Bidwell's main data is one industry. The figures here are as reported by Wharton's own outlet, and the ASQ paper itself was not opened. `bidwell2011` is graded on that basis.

### 5. Timing: does applying early help? Grade: weak
- `startwire2011` (via ERE, 8 June 2011): job-search startup StartWire collected data on 6,600 hires from employers in 10 industries. Almost 50% of hires had applied within the first week a job was posted. 27% applied within two days, and three-quarters within three weeks. Its own founder cautioned: "On a job-by-job basis though, it might not be so."
- `fradkin-bu2025`: BU summary of Andrey Fradkin's job-board research, published in *Management Science*. Fewer people apply after a posting's first few days, and people are more likely to apply when told few others have. This is about applicant behaviour, not hire odds.
- *Author view:* apply within the first week or so when you can, especially for high-volume roles. Never send a rushed, generic application just to be early.

### 6. Search while employed vs not employed. Grade: strong
- `faberman2017` (NY Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations supplement, fielded each October since 2013): "the employed fare better than the non-employed in job search: they receive more offers per application and are offered higher pay even after controlling for observable characteristics." The unconditional wage offer gap was 40 log points (49%). After controls it was 25 log points. The employed are "nearly three times more efficient" in turning search effort into offers.
- Combine with `kroft2013` (callbacks fall over the first eight months of unemployment). See `switching.md`.
