## 1. How accurate are self-assessments?

**Evidence**

- Across 22 meta-analyses, the mean correlation between people's self-evaluations of ability and objective performance is **.29** (range .09 to .63). Accuracy is better for domain-specific self-ratings and for objective, familiar or simple tasks. **[strong]** `zell2014`
- Self-estimated and measured cognitive ability correlate **.33**. Accuracy improves when people rate themselves against a clearly defined comparison group. **[strong]** `freund2012`
- At work, self and supervisor ratings agree at ρ = .34-.35. Self-ratings are more lenient than supervisors' in Western samples (d = 0.32), and agreement is better when ratings rest on non-judgmental indicators. **[strong]** `harris1988`, `heidemeier2009`

**Researcher's synthesis**: people have *some* insight into their own performance, about as much as a mediocre outside predictor. The usable lesson is that specific, objective, comparative self-assessments ("I closed 14 of the team's 40 tickets this quarter") are more accurate than global ones ("I'm a strong engineer").

## 2. The Dunning-Kruger effect: what was found, what is disputed

**The original finding**

- In four studies, bottom-quartile scorers on tests of humour, grammar and logic estimated themselves at about the **62nd percentile while scoring at the 12th**. The authors attributed this to a metacognitive deficit, and training improved both skill and self-insight. **[moderate]** `kruger1999`

**The statistical-artefact critiques**

- **Regression and better-than-average.** A replication found the same asymmetric errors but no mediation by metacognitive skill. The asymmetry disappeared once regression to the mean or the general better-than-average effect was removed statistically. **[moderate]** `krueger2002`
- **Random numbers make the famous graph.** Simulations with pure noise reproduce the standard Dunning-Kruger plots (difference-score charts and quartile line charts). Plotting each person's self-assessment against measured competence avoids the artefact. **[moderate]** `nuhfer2016`
- **Better tests find little.** Using tests that the quartile method can't fool (heteroscedasticity and nonlinear regression), 929 adults showed an essentially linear relation between measured and self-assessed intelligence, contrary to the hypothesis. **[moderate]** `gignac2020`. The method was challenged in a published comment (`hiller2023`, title-level) and defended in a reply titled "Still no Dunning-Kruger effect" (`gignac2023`, title-level).
- **How big, if real?** With the recommended methods, the effect peaked at 7-9 percentile points of relative overestimation in two samples of about 3,500, and was confined to about 0.2% of participants, all scoring at chance. **[moderate]** `gignac2024`

**The defence (a real, smaller effect)**

- A rational model plus two large replications (about 4,000 participants each) found support for **low performers being less able to judge whether each of their answers is correct** in grammar and logical reasoning. That is a genuine metacognitive difference, just not the cartoon. **[strong]** `jansen2021`

**Where this leaves the popular version**

- The popular chart of a confidence peak at low skill ("Mount Stupid"), then a valley, then a slow climb, does not appear in the evidence listed here. Self-assessment and measured ability are *positively* correlated (`zell2014`, `freund2012`), and the relation is close to linear (`gignac2020`). So on average, low performers rate themselves *lower* than high performers do. They overestimate more, but they are not the most confident people in the room. See `myths.md` #3.
- **Writers should say**: everyone tends to think they are above average. Low performers overestimate the most, partly because of statistics and partly because judging your own errors takes the very skill you lack. The effect is modest, and the meme exaggerates it.

## 3. Who over- and under-sells themselves

**Evidence**

- **Gender gap in self-promotion.** Asked to describe their own performance on a male-typed maths and science task, women rated themselves lower than equally performing men. This happened both when the descriptions went to potential employers and when there was no incentive to self-promote. The gap appears by sixth grade and disappears on a female-typed task. **[strong]** `exley2022` (4,000+ adults online, 10,000+ school pupils)
- **Overconfidence buys status.** In six studies, overconfident people gained status in groups because their behaviour made them look competent, and wanting status made people more overconfident. **[moderate]** `anderson2012`
- Self-promoters overestimate how much listeners enjoy it. `scopelliti2015` (Part II) **[moderate]**. Self-promoting women can lose likability. `rudman1998` (Part II) **[moderate]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: self-review season rewards confident self-description, but the evidence says confidence and accuracy come apart. The defensible middle path is to **describe your work accurately and concretely, with evidence**, and not shade it down. The shading-down is the documented gap (`exley2022`). Over-selling has a documented social cost (`scopelliti2015`).

## 4. Feedback seeking: the part you can control

**Evidence**

- **Origin of the idea.** In 1983, research on feedback shifted from what supervisors hand down in the annual review to feedback that people *proactively seek* in everyday interaction. This is dated to Ashford and Cummings (1983) in the authors' own review. **[strong]** `ashford2016`
- **What drives seeking.** In an early field study, role ambiguity, contingency uncertainty (not knowing what leads to what), tenure and job involvement predicted proactive feedback seeking, and tolerance for ambiguity moderated the links. The abstract does not give directions, so cite `anseel2015` (Part II) for "falls with tenure". **[moderate]** `ashford1985`
- **Three motives.** People seek feedback to achieve goals (instrumental), to protect their ego and to protect their image. These motives shape how often people ask, whether they ask directly or just watch for cues, when, whom they ask and about what. **[strong]** `ashford2003`
- **Effects.** Meta-analysis: feedback seeking falls with tenure and age, rises with learning orientation and good leader relationships, and has a **small direct link to performance**. `anseel2015` (Part II) **[strong]**
- Newcomers who seek information adjust better. `morrison1993` (Part II) **[moderate]**
- Feedback helps on average (d = .41), but over a third of feedback interventions reduce performance. Feedback helps less the more it draws attention away from the task and toward the self. `kluger1996` (Part II) **[strong]**

**Not verified (do not print as fact)**: the popular claim that seeking *negative* feedback raises how effective others think you are, while fishing for *positive* feedback lowers it. It is attributed to a 1991 field study of 387 managers by Ashford and Tsui. Only the study's design could be confirmed (its abstract is truncated in every source opened), so the result is left out.

**Researcher's synthesis**: feedback seeking is the most controllable input in this whole topic. The evidence supports (a) asking, (b) asking more as tenure grows, against the natural decline, and (c) keeping feedback about the task, not about you as a person. That feedback seeking has only a small direct link to performance (`anseel2015`) argues for asking well, not asking constantly.
