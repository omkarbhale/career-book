# Performance reviews and ratings: what they measure, forced ranking, calibration, and what you control

Part III research notes. Grades in **[brackets]**; keys resolve in `sources.yaml` (Part III) or, where marked (Part I) / (Part II), in that part's registry.

Statements are tagged as **Evidence** (a study found it), **Practitioner** (a company, consultant or author says so, untested) or **Researcher's synthesis** (my interpretation, for writers to adopt or reject).

## 1. How reliable is a performance rating?

**Evidence**

- In two large 360-degree datasets (n = 2,350 and n = 2,142 managers, each rated by 7 people), **idiosyncratic rater effects explained 62% and 53% of the variance in ratings**. The ratee's actual performance (general plus dimension-specific) explained only 21% and 25%, and random error 11% and 18%. Whether the rater was a boss, peer or subordinate added little. **[strong]** `scullen2000`
  - Read this carefully. It is a variance share across many ratings: who rates you moves the number more than what you did. It does **not** mean that 62% of any one person's rating is "about the rater".
  - These were developmental ratings, not ratings tied to pay. Administrative ratings add politics (see §2), so they are unlikely to be cleaner.
  - A popular "62% of a rating reflects the rater" line is often credited to HBR's 2015 Deloitte article (`buckingham2015` **[weak]**; its body text could not be read here). The number matches this study, so cite the study, not the magazine.
- Meta-analysis: two supervisors rating the same person's overall performance agree at a mean interrater reliability of **.52**. Supervisors agree with each other more than peers do. **[strong]** `viswesvaran1996`
- Managers combine the same facts differently. Given identical profiles, raters clustered into three "policies": some weighted task performance most, some weighted counterproductive behaviour most, and some weighted both heavily. Rater demographics did not predict which policy a rater used. **[moderate]** `rotundo2002`
- Why ratings drift from performance: measurement error, systematic non-performance factors (liking, visibility, the rater's own standards) and **intentional distortion**. Raters need both the tools and the incentive to rate honestly. **[moderate]** `murphy2008`
- A century of appraisal research has refined scale formats, rater training and sources, but the field's own review concludes that the bigger open problem is performance *management* (what happens all year), not the rating form. **[strong]** `denisi2017`. Pulakos and O'Leary argue the same: systems fail because they have become administrative steps disconnected from day-to-day expectation-setting and guidance. **[moderate]** `pulakos2011`
- Self versus others: self-ratings agree only moderately with supervisors (ρ = .35) and peers (ρ = .36), while peers and supervisors agree more (ρ = .62). **[strong]** `harris1988`. In a larger meta-analysis (k = 115, n = 37,752), self-supervisor agreement was r = .22 (ρ = .34), and self-ratings were more lenient in Western samples (d = 0.32). **[strong]** `heidemeier2009`. See `self-assessment.md`.

**Researcher's synthesis**

A rating is a measurement taken with a noisy instrument, one that is partly a person with their own standards and blind spots. Two practical consequences for the reader:

1. A single rating from a single manager is weak evidence about you. Do not build your self-image on one review, good or bad.
2. The rater is part of the measurement, so **making your work legible to the rater** is not vanity. It reduces noise. That is different from impression management for its own sake (Part II `power.md` §3).

## 2. What goes into the number besides performance?

**Evidence**

- **Gendered language.** In one Fortune 500 technology company's written reviews, men and women were equally likely to be described as technically able. But women were more often called "too aggressive" and men "too soft", and "taking charge" went with the highest ratings for men but not for women. **[moderate]** `correll2020`
- **Potential ratings.** At a large retailer, women received *higher* performance ratings but *lower* "potential" ratings, which drove promotion. **[strong]** `benson2026`. See `promotions.md` §3.
- **Face time and social access.** Employees who got more face-to-face time with their manager were promoted faster. **[strong]** `cullen2023b`. Part II documents passive face time `elsbach2010` (Part II) and remote-work promotion effects `bloom2015`, `bloom2024` (Part II).
- **Credit attribution** is biased by gender in group work. `sarsons2021` (Part II) **[strong]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: ratings carry the same social biases as any human judgment. The reader can't remove them, but can reduce how much room they have: bring concrete, checkable evidence (§6), and make sure the rater sees the work directly rather than through hearsay.

## 3. Forced distribution and stack ranking

**Evidence**

- **Simulation (the best case for it).** A forced distribution system that fires a fixed share of the bottom each year *could* noticeably raise average workforce potential. Most of the gain comes in the first several years, and its size depends on how many are fired and on voluntary turnover. **[moderate]** `scullen2005`
  - The simulation leaves out morale, collaboration, gaming and legal costs. Its own result is that the gains flatten after a few years.
- **Raters dislike it.** In two experiments, raters found forced distribution harder and less fair than a normal scale, especially when it was used for pay or promotion decisions and when the team's performance didn't vary much. That lowered their confidence in giving feedback. **[moderate]** `schleicher2009`
- **It hurts collaboration.** In three real-effort experiments, forced ratings sped up individual work but **slowed teamwork and reduced knowledge sharing**, and were seen as unfair in collaborative settings. **[moderate]** `loberg2021`
- **It distorts what gets rated.** On a creative task, forced ratings did not raise effort or output, but they raised stress (including biomarkers). Supervisors' ratings tracked actual creativity *less*, and eloquent writing and strategic gaming *more*. **[moderate]** `cardinaels2021`

**Practitioner / case**

- Microsoft ended stack ranking in November 2013. The HR head's email: "No more curve ... there will no longer be a pre-determined targeted distribution." Under the old system, one 1-5 rating (manager assessment plus calibration) drove merit pay, bonus and stock awards. **[moderate]** as a factual record. `shrm2013`
- GE's "vitality curve" (top 20 / middle 70 / bottom 10) is the usual origin story. **Not verified here**: no primary GE source was opened, so do not print the percentages as fact.
- Deloitte publicly redesigned its review process in 2015 (`buckingham2015` **[weak]**, title and summary only).

**Researcher's synthesis**: forced ranking turns colleagues into competitors for a fixed number of good ratings. The evidence says that may squeeze out some low performers early, at the price of collaboration and rating validity. For the reader, the practical point is to *know which system you are in*. Under a curve, your rating depends on your peers as well as on you. That changes how you should read a "meets expectations".

## 4. Calibration committees

**Evidence**

- Calibration meetings, where managers review and adjust each other's ratings, are widespread but understudied. In two field studies, managers saw them as attempts to improve rating quality. Calibration came with more perceived accountability and more information, and **post-calibration ratings correlated better with other performance measures** than pre-calibration ratings. **[moderate]** `speer2019`
  - One of very few academic studies. It does not test what happens to people whose manager advocates weakly in the room.

**Practitioner**

- At Microsoft (2011-2013) the single rating came from "their manager's assessment and a calibration process (typically with managers one or two levels above the employee)". `shrm2013`
- In big-tech promotion processes, committees of senior engineers and managers judge written packets against level definitions. **[weak]** `orosz2019`. See `promotions.md` §6.

**Researcher's synthesis**: calibration means **your rating is decided partly in a room you are not in, by people who mostly know you through your manager's summary**. Two things follow, both of them the author's view and neither tested: (1) your manager must be able to state your impact in a sentence or two that people who don't know you will accept; (2) people beyond your manager, especially skip-levels and partner-team leads, should have first-hand knowledge of your work. This links forward to sponsorship (`promotions.md` §4) and back to Part II's visibility notes (`power.md` §3).

## 5. Should companies get rid of ratings?

**Evidence / expert debate**

- The SIOP debate summary lists the case for dropping ratings (rater disagreement, weak link to actual performance, conflicting purposes, feedback that often fails to help) and the case against (performance is always evaluated somehow, and the alternatives may be worse, including less transparent judgments). **[moderate]** `adler2016`
- Feedback itself helps on average, but more than a third of feedback interventions *reduce* performance. `kluger1996` (Part II) **[strong]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: "no ratings" rarely means "no evaluation". It usually means the evaluation moves into compensation and promotion discussions that are less visible to the employee. A reader at a "no ratings" company still has a rating. They just can't see it.

## 6. What an individual can actually control

Mostly **researcher's synthesis**, built on the evidence above and in `self-assessment.md`:

| You can't control | You can influence |
|---|---|
| Rater idiosyncrasy (62%/53% of variance, `scullen2000`) | Who sees your work first-hand, so more than one rater has direct evidence |
| The distribution system (curve or not) | Knowing the system and reading your rating relative to it |
| What happens in calibration | Giving your manager a short, checkable account of your impact before the cycle (`speer2019`: more information made ratings better) |
| Bias in language and "potential" judgments (`correll2020`, `benson2026`) | Concrete evidence (numbers, artefacts, named outcomes) that is harder to reframe |
| Whether the organisation gives good feedback | Asking for feedback yourself, specifically and often (`self-assessment.md` §4) |

- Evidence anchor for "concrete evidence": self-other agreement improves when **non-judgmental performance indicators** are used. **[strong]** `heidemeier2009`
- Evidence anchor for "ask for it": feedback-seeking research (`ashford2003`, `ashford2016`, `anseel2015` (Part II)).
- **Do not overstate**: no study here shows that writing a better self-review raises your rating. That is the author's view, not evidence.
