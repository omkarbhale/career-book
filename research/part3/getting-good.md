# Getting good: practice, experience, feedback loops, learning on the job, mentoring, career capital

Part III research notes. Grades in **[brackets]**; keys resolve in `sources.yaml`, or in Part I / Part II where marked.

## 1. Deliberate practice: what survives the debate

**Evidence**

- Recap (Part II): deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and **under 1% in professions**. `macnamara2014` **[strong]**. A replication of the original violin study found a much smaller effect. `macnamara2019` **[strong]**
- **Sports meta-analysis**: practice explained 18% of variance overall but **only 1% among elite athletes**, and elite athletes did not start their sport earlier than less skilled ones. **[strong]** `macnamara2016`
- **Ericsson's reply**: many studies counted practice that doesn't meet his original definition (individualised, teacher-designed training aimed at specific weaknesses). Restricted to studies matching that definition, practice explained **29%** of variance (61% after correcting for measurement error), against 14% overall in Macnamara's analyses. **[moderate]** `ericsson2019` (the theory's originator replying to critics; a small subset of studies)

**Researcher's synthesis**: both camps agree on more than the headlines suggest. Raw hours explain little, especially at work. *Designed* practice, aimed at a specific weakness with fast feedback and usually with a coach, is where the effect lives. Most jobs offer little of that by default. So the reader's lever is to **build practice into the job**: pick a sub-skill, get reps, get feedback, repeat. That is the author's view, consistent with but not proven by the evidence.

## 2. Experience is not the same as expertise

**Evidence**

- Work experience correlates ρ = .27 with performance. Experience measured as **amount** (ρ = .43) or at **task level** (.41) predicts best. The authors contrast amount measures with time-based measures, such as years in the job, and type-of-experience measures. It predicts hard measures such as work samples (.39) better than ratings (.24). **[strong]** `quinones1995`
- For hiring, years of experience predict new-hire performance at only .07 (revised estimate). `sackett2022` (Part I) **[strong]**
- **Doctors**: in 62 evaluations, 52% found quality of care *declined* with years in practice on every outcome, and 21% on some outcomes. Only 2% found steady improvement. The result held for the most objective outcome measures. **[strong]** `choudhry2005`
- Age is largely unrelated to core task performance and creativity. `ng2008` **[strong]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: "ten years of experience" can mean ten years of improvement or one year repeated ten times. What counts is the number of *relevant reps with feedback*, and whether you keep updating. This is a key bridge between Part I (where years of experience are weak evidence for employers) and Part III (where repetitions are what build skill).

## 3. Feedback loops: when can you trust your own judgment?

**Evidence**

- In a joint paper, two schools that usually disagree (heuristics-and-biases and naturalistic decision-making) concluded that intuitive expertise is trustworthy only when (1) the environment is regular enough to be predictable and (2) the person has had the opportunity to learn its regularities through practice with feedback. **"Subjective experience is not a reliable indicator of judgment accuracy."** **[moderate]** `kahneman2009`
- Feedback helps on average but can hurt, particularly feedback aimed at the self rather than the task. `kluger1996` (Part II) **[strong]**
- Self-assessment is more accurate on objective, familiar tasks (`zell2014` **[strong]**). See `self-assessment.md`.

**Researcher's synthesis**: for each part of your job, ask: *do I find out quickly and clearly whether I was right?* Where the answer is no (strategy, hiring, architecture decisions that play out over years), your confidence tells you little, so build artificial feedback: write predictions down, run post-mortems, ask someone who will see the outcome. This links to `self-assessment.md` §4.

## 4. How people actually learn: techniques with evidence

**Evidence**

- **Spacing**: spaced study beats cramming for retention, and the best gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember (839 assessments, 317 experiments). **[strong]** `cepeda2006`
- **Testing yourself** and **spacing** were the only two of ten techniques rated "high utility". Rereading and highlighting were rated low: they "do not consistently boost students' performance". **[strong]** `dunlosky2013` (student learning; workplace transfer plausible, less tested)
- **Learning styles** ("I'm a visual learner"): "virtually no evidence" for matching instruction to a preferred style, and several well-designed studies contradict it. **[strong]** `pashler2008`. See `myths.md`.
- **Growth mindset**: weak overall effects on achievement, both for mindset itself (k = 273, N = 365,915) and for mindset interventions (k = 43, N = 57,155). Low-SES or at-risk students may benefit. **[strong]** `sisk2018`. See `myths.md`.

**Researcher's synthesis**: when the reader needs to learn something for work (a new codebase, a domain, a language), the evidence-backed approach is: retrieve rather than reread, space it out, and get feedback. "I'm just not a numbers person" is a learning-style or mindset claim with weak support in either direction. Neither the label nor the pep talk is the lever. Practice design is.

## 5. Learning on the job: the 70-20-10 claim

**Evidence**

- The "70% rule" (most workplace learning is informal), which underlies 70-20-10, is widely treated as fact in HR development. A critical review finds its **evidential basis weak**, citing sloppy scholarship, inconsistent concepts and research-protocol problems, and concludes it "needs to be set aside". **[moderate]** `clardy2018`
- In the Australian public sector, applying 70:20:10 did not build manager capability as hoped. A key misconception was "that unstructured experiential learning automatically results in capability development". The authors note the framework's effectiveness had not previously been assessed. **[weak]** `johnson2018` (qualitative)

**Practitioner origin (from secondary sources, not verified)**: the split is usually traced to Center for Creative Leadership research on executives in the 1980s, summarised in *The Lessons of Experience* (1988), with the 70/20/10 numbers appearing later in a Lombardo and Eichinger planning tool. The talent-development association page that discusses this refused automated access, so treat the origin story as unverified.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the *direction* is reasonable (most of what you know about your job, you learned doing it), but the *numbers* are not measurements. "Learning by doing" only works when the doing has feedback (§3) and some deliberate structure (§1). See `myths.md` #1.

## 6. Mentoring (beyond Part II)

**Evidence**

- Part II's verdict stands: mentoring is associated with career benefits, but effects on objective outcomes such as pay and promotion are **small**. Subjective outcomes are larger. `allen2004`, `eby2008` (Part II) **[strong]**
- **For new managers specifically**: first-line leaders learned their new role faster when their own boss both **modelled** effective leadership ("show") and **gave job information** ("tell"), especially if they had never worked for an exceptional leader. **[moderate]** `dragoni2014`
- Feedback seeking (asking, not waiting) is the individual's side of the same relationship. `self-assessment.md` §4.
- **Left out**: a meta-analysis on career benefits *for mentors* (Ghosh and Reio 2013) was found in Crossref, but no abstract or full text could be opened, so its findings are not used.

## 7. Career capital and rare skills (practitioner frameworks)

**Practitioner** (all **[weak]**: frameworks, not tested)

- **80,000 Hours**: career capital is "anything that puts you in a better position to make a difference in the future": skills, connections, credentials, character and financial runway. It warns against both neglecting it and endlessly collecting generic credentials. `todd2026`
- **Cal Newport**, *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012): argues that "'follow your passion' is bad advice" and that skill comes first; it popularised "career capital". `newport2012` (author page only; the book was not opened, so don't quote it beyond the title and blurb)

**Evidence that bears on the frameworks**

- Skills are partly occupation-specific (`kambourov2008` **[strong]**), and in fast-changing fields the earnings return to early skills erodes quickly (`deming2020` **[strong]**). See `long-game.md`.
- Passion often develops rather than being found (`okeefe2018` **[moderate]**, with `chen2015` as a counterweight). See `motivation.md`.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the career-capital idea is a useful heuristic with indirect evidential support. Build skills that are valuable, that are hard to acquire (so they're scarce) and that **transfer across employers**. The last point is the one the evidence speaks to most clearly (`kambourov2008`, `deming2020`).
