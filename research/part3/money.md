## 1. Does more income buy more happiness? The $75,000 story and its resolution

**Evidence, in order**

1. **2010**: in more than 450,000 US survey responses, *life evaluation* rose steadily with log income. *Emotional well-being* also rose, but showed "no further progress beyond an annual income of ~$75,000". **[strong]** `kahneman2010`
2. **2021**: 1.7 million experience-sampling reports from 33,391 employed US adults showed both experienced and evaluative well-being rising **linearly with log income**, as steeply for high earners as for low earners, with **no plateau above $75,000**. **[strong]** `killingsworth2021`
3. **2023 adversarial collaboration** (Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers re-analysing the 2021 data):
   - For the **least happy ~15-20%** of people, happiness rises with income up to about **$100,000** and then flattens: "the miseries that remain are not alleviated by high income" (heartbreak, bereavement and clinical depression are given as examples).
   - For the **happier majority** it keeps rising with log income, and *accelerates* for the happiest.
   - The 2010 plateau came partly from a ceiling effect in yes/no questions. Kahneman and Deaton "might have reached the correct conclusion if they had described their results in terms of unhappiness rather than happiness". **[strong]** `killingsworth2023`
4. **The debate continues**: published critiques, including one titled "Inappropriate causal assumptions underlie Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers' conclusions", and a 2024 reply. `killingsworth2024` (title-level)

**Important caveats for writers**

- All of this is **correlational**. Log income means each *doubling* adds about the same amount. Going from $50k to $100k is worth about as much as going from $100k to $200k.
- US data. Dollar thresholds are not portable across countries or years.

## 2. Windfalls: what a large, random money shock does

**Evidence**

- Swedish lottery winners, surveyed 5-22 years later against matched controls, showed **sustained increases in overall life satisfaction lasting more than a decade**, with no sign of fading. Effects on happiness and mental health were significantly smaller. Satisfaction with personal finances explained much of the gain. **[strong]** `lindqvist2020` (pre-registered; a natural experiment)

**Researcher's synthesis**: money reliably improves how people *evaluate* their lives, mostly through financial security. It does less for moment-to-moment mood. That makes building "runway" (savings) a career-capital move in its own right (`todd2026` **[weak]**).

## 3. Raises, adaptation and loss aversion

**Evidence**

- **Adaptation**: in German panel data (7,812 people, 1984-2000), happiness adapted to income gains within four years, but **did not adapt to gains in status**. In the long run, a one-standard-deviation rise in status had the same effect as an income rise of 285% of a standard deviation. **[moderate]** `ditella2010`
- **Raises vs levels**: job satisfaction depends on wage *increases* as well as wage levels. People who stay in the same job become less satisfied over time even as wages rise (19 waves of German panel data). **[moderate]** `grund2007`
- **Loss aversion in real life**: income *losses* hurt well-being more than equivalent gains help (Germany N = 28,723, UK N = 20,570). The authors suggest that small, stable raises may serve well-being better than volatile pay. **[strong]** `boyce2013`
- **Relative income**: comparisons with other people and with your own past (habituation) help explain why national happiness stays flat as incomes grow. **[strong]** `clark2008`

**Researcher's synthesis**: a raise feels great, then becomes the new normal within a few years. A pay cut hurts more than a raise helps. The career implication (author's view): don't take a job you dislike for a raise you will adapt to. Do take seriously the *security* and *status* parts of a job, which adapt less (`ditella2010`, `lindqvist2020`).

## 4. Pay comparison at work

**Evidence**

- When a random subset of University of California employees was told about a website listing colleagues' pay, those **below their unit's median** reported lower pay satisfaction and job satisfaction and more intention to look for a new job. Above-median earners were unaffected. **Pay rank mattered more than pay level.** **[strong]** `card2012`
- Pay transparency law and its equilibrium effects: Part I `offers-negotiation.md` (`cullen2023`, Part I: Cullen and Pakzad-Hurson, *Econometrica*).

**Key note for writers**: Part I's `cullen2023` (pay transparency) and Part III's `cullen2023b` (Cullen and Perez-Truglia, "The Old Boys' Club", AER 2023) are different papers. The Part III key carries a `b` suffix so that keys stay unique across the whole book.

## 5. Meaning vs money

- People accept lower pay for meaningful work (`hu2017` **[moderate]**), and that willingness can be exploited (`kim2020` **[moderate]**). See `motivation.md` §4.
- **Not verified (left out)**: the commercial survey claim that workers would give up a large share (often quoted as 23%) of lifetime earnings for meaningful work. It was not opened.
