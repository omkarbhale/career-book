## 1. What promotions are, structurally

**Evidence and theory**

- **Tournaments.** Promotion works like a rank-order tournament: the prize (the higher job and its pay jump) goes to whoever ranks first, not to everyone who clears a bar. In theory, paying by rank can produce the same efficient effort as paying for output. **[moderate]** (theory) `lazear1981`
- **Inside one firm over 20 years.** The hierarchy was simple and stable, and promotion "fast tracks" existed. Job levels mattered for pay, but pay varied a lot within a level, and career moves reflected both the employee's learning and the firm's learning about their ability. **[moderate]** `baker1994`
- **Up-or-out** (law partnership, academic tenure): a commitment to promote or let go solves a two-sided trust problem. Otherwise the firm could under-report the employee's value, and the employee would not invest in firm-specific skills. **[moderate]** (theory) `kahn1988`
- Career-success meta-analysis: human capital and demographics relate more to objective success (pay, promotions); organisational sponsorship and stable traits relate more to career satisfaction. `ng2005` (Part II) **[strong]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: the tournament frame explains a lot of the reader's experience. Promotions are relative ("better than the other candidates for the slot"), lumpy (one big jump, not steady growth) and partly about the firm *learning* who you are. So being good is necessary, and **being known to be good by the people deciding** is also necessary. That links to §4 and to `reviews.md` §4.

## 2. Performance vs potential vs visibility: what the evidence says drives promotion

**Evidence**

- **Current performance drives promotion, even when it predicts the next job badly.** Among sales workers at 131 firms, firms promoted their best salespeople, yet pre-promotion sales performance *negatively* predicted performance as a manager. Collaboration experience predicted managerial success but was not rewarded with promotion. **[strong]** `benson2019`
  - Working-paper figures (2018 NBER version, 214 firms): doubling a worker's sales raised promotion probability by 14.3% relative to the base rate. Doubling a new manager's pre-promotion sales was associated with a **7.5% decline in each subordinate's sales**. "Lone wolves" who had never collaborated did especially badly as managers.
  - Firms weighted sales less when the manager role carried more responsibility, or when frontline pay was strongly tied to performance (so promotion was less needed as a carrot).
- **"Potential" ratings can override performance.** At a large retailer (29,809 management-track staff), women got *higher* performance ratings but substantially *lower* potential ratings. Potential gaps explained about half of the gender promotion gap, and women later outperformed men who had the same potential rating. **[strong]** `benson2026`
  - In the 2022 working paper, men's annual promotion rate was 12.6% vs 11.0% for women. Moving from potential rating 2 to 3 raised promotion probability by about 9 percentage points (75% of baseline), more than an equivalent step in performance. Ratings used a "Nine Box" performance-by-potential grid.
- **Social access drives promotion.** Using quasi-random manager rotation at a large financial institution, employees with more face-to-face interaction with their manager were promoted faster, which could explain **a third of the firm's gender promotion gap**. **[strong]** `cullen2023b`
  - Working-paper detail: smokers newly assigned to a smoking manager rose 0.70 pay grades more within 10 quarters, and non-smokers saw no such gain. Men moved to a male manager gained 0.60 pay grades (about 14.6% salary) relative to men moved to another female manager.
- Remote work: call-centre workers randomised to work from home were promoted less, conditional on performance (`bloom2015`, Part II **[strong]**). Two hybrid days a week showed no promotion penalty (`bloom2024`, Part II **[strong]**).
- High self-monitors won more promotions. `kilduff1994` (Part II) **[moderate]**. Political skill predicts career success beyond personality and intelligence. `munyon2015` (Part II) **[strong]**
- Women are asked to do, and accept, more low-promotability tasks. `babcock2017` (Part II) **[strong]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: in rough order of what the evidence shows, promotions run on (1) demonstrated performance in the current job, (2) judgments of "potential" that are subjective and biased, and (3) the decision-maker's direct exposure to you. None of this is a reason to stop doing good work: (1) remains the biggest input. It is a reason to (a) do some visible work of the *next* job before you have it, since promotion committees ask for it (§6), and (b) make sure the people who judge potential see you first-hand.

## 3. The Peter Principle: what's true and what isn't

- **The popular claim**: "In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to their level of incompetence" (Peter and Hull, 1969; the book was not opened here and is cited through `lazear2004`).
- **Evidence for a real version**: firms do promote on current-job performance at the expense of traits that predict the next job, and the cost is measurable (§2). **[strong]** `benson2019`
- **A statistical version that needs no incompetence**: people are promoted after a good run, and part of any good run is luck. So average performance after promotion falls even when the firm promotes rationally (regression to the mean). Firms should, and do, raise the bar to compensate. **[moderate]** (theory) `lazear2004`
- **What is not shown**: that people keep rising *until* they are incompetent, or that most managers are incompetent. Firms also partly correct for the problem (`benson2019`: less weight on sales when stakes are higher).
- **Say this instead**: firms tend to promote the best *doer* rather than the best potential *manager*, and some decline after promotion is just luck running out. Being great at your current job is weak evidence that you'll be great at a different one. See `the-fork.md`.

## 4. Sponsorship, the "broken rung" and promotion gaps

**Evidence**

- **The broken rung (industry survey).** "For every 100 men promoted to manager last year, only 93 women were promoted" (82 Asian women and Latinas, 60 Black women), the 11th year of a gap at the first step to manager. Only 31% of entry-level women reported having a sponsor, vs 45% of men. **[moderate]** `leanin2025`
  - Sample: 124 US and Canadian organisations employing about 3 million people, 9,500 surveyed employees, 62 HR-leader interviews.
  - **Flags**: self-selected companies; published by an advocacy organisation and a consultancy that sells related services; the ratio changes every year (secondary coverage gave 81 per 100 for the 2024 report, not verified here). Always cite the year.
- Peer-reviewed mechanisms behind gaps at the first rungs: potential ratings (`benson2026` **[strong]**), manager face time (`cullen2023b` **[strong]**), credit for joint work (`sarsons2021`, Part II **[strong]**), low-promotability tasks (`babcock2017`, Part II **[strong]**) and gendered review language (`correll2020` **[moderate]**).
- Mentoring has small effects on objective career outcomes (`allen2004`, `eby2008`, Part II **[strong]**). Mentoring is not sponsorship (`ibarra2010`, Part II **[moderate]**, practitioner).
- **Not verified**: the popular "Sponsor Effect" percentages (Hewlett and colleagues). Part II also could not verify them; do not print them.

**Researcher's synthesis**: "sponsorship" in the evidence is mostly about *who speaks for you when you're not in the room*, which is exactly the calibration problem in `reviews.md` §4. The peer-reviewed evidence for sponsorship *causing* promotion is thinner than practitioner books suggest. But the mechanisms it would work through (advocacy, exposure, potential judgments) are each well documented.

## 5. Internal promotion vs external hire

**Evidence**

- In a US investment bank's personnel data (2003-2009), workers **promoted into a job performed significantly better for their first two years** than external hires into similar jobs, and were less likely to leave or be let go. Yet external hires were **paid around 18% more** at entry, had more experience and education, and were then promoted faster. Moving up and across at the same time (promotion plus transfer) went worse than other internal moves. **[strong]** `bidwell2011`
  - Part I cited this key at **moderate**, from a Wharton summary that said "18% to 20%". Part III read the ASQ abstract and upgrades the core finding to **strong**. Use "around 18%" as the paper's own figure.
- Pay growth for job switchers vs stayers: see Part I `switching.md` (`atlantafed-wgt`).

**Researcher's synthesis**: the inside path pays less up front but performs better early on (firm-specific knowledge, relationships). The outside path pays more and is judged harder. That's a real trade-off for the reader at every promotion decision, and it connects back to Part I (getting the job) and forward to `long-game.md`.

## 6. How promotion cases and levels work in tech (practitioner)

All **practitioner** sources, graded **[weak]**: they describe practice and are not tested.

- **Three stages as companies grow** (`orosz2019`):
  1. managers meet and decide;
  2. semi-formal committees with written level expectations and short manager write-ups;
  3. formal **promotion packets and committees** (Google, Uber and other large firms are named), with self-, peer- and manager reviews judged against detailed job ladders by senior engineers and managers.
- **Ladders guide, they don't decide.** Dropbox published its engineering career framework, stating it "is not meant to be a promotion checklist" but a picture of impact at the next level. **[weak]** `todi2021`. "Career ladders are a tool that applies better against populations than people." **[weak]** `larson2020`
- **Not verified here** (left out): specific claims about a "six months operating at the next level" rule, Google's promotion-cycle months and named review systems (only seen on aggregator sites); levels.fyi level equivalences (not opened).

**Researcher's synthesis**: across these practitioner accounts, a promotion case is an *argument with evidence*, written for strangers: what changed because of you, at what scope, and with what judgment. That is the same skill as Part I's resume, and it is why `reviews.md` §6 recommends keeping a running evidence log.

## 7. Do promotions make people happier or healthier?

**Evidence**

- In panel data, promotions raised job security, pay perceptions and job satisfaction in the short term, and changed job control, stress, income and hours. But they had **negligible effects on health and happiness**, and mental health had deteriorated significantly two years after promotion. **[moderate]** `johnston2013`
- British panel: healthier people are the ones who later get promoted (so the cross-sectional "seniority is healthy" link is partly selection). Little evidence that health improves after promotion; psychological well-being worsened. **[moderate]** `boyce2012`
- Status, unlike income, showed little hedonic adaptation in German panel data. **[moderate]** `ditella2010`. See `money.md`.

**Researcher's synthesis**: promotion is good for your pay and standing, and roughly neutral to slightly negative for your day-to-day well-being, on average. That is not a reason to refuse it. It is a reason to want it for what the job *is*, not for how you imagine it will feel. See `the-fork.md`.
