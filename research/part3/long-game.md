# The long game: plateaus, mid-career change, skill obsolescence, and the shape of a working life

## 1. Career plateaus

**Evidence**

- **Origin of the concept**: managerial careers show "early rapid upward mobility followed by inevitable leveling-off or plateauing", from interviews with managers in nine large organisations. **[moderate]** `ference1977`
- **Forty years of research** (72 empirical sources, 1977-2017): people on a *hierarchical* plateau (no more promotions) or a *job-content* plateau (no more learning) report lower satisfaction, well-being, performance and commitment, and more intention to quit. This is largely because they feel less supported by the organisation. Effects are weaker for people who care less about promotion, and when the organisation and the individual actively counter the plateau. Proactivity and taking on added responsibilities go with less plateauing. **[moderate]** `yang2019`
- Pyramids narrow: in tournament-style hierarchies, most people stop being promoted at some point by arithmetic (`lazear1981` **[moderate]**, theory). See `promotions.md` §1.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the hierarchical plateau is near-universal and arithmetic. The *content* plateau (no longer learning) is the one the reader can do most about, and the review suggests it is the more damaging feeling when the person still wants to grow. Proactive moves, such as new responsibilities, a lateral move, or mentoring others, are the documented counter.

## 2. Skill obsolescence and the "half-life of skills"

**Evidence**

- **Job skills really do change, and faster in some fields.** Using a near-universe of US online job postings (2007-2019), returns to experience look like a race between on-the-job learning and skill obsolescence. In faster-changing careers, age-earnings profiles are flatter. The earnings premium for applied STEM majors (computer science, engineering) is highest at entry and, in the working paper, falls by **more than 50% in the first decade**. Graduates move out of fast-changing occupations as they age. STEM job ads requiring machine-learning or AI skills rose 460% from 2007 to 2017 (working paper). **[strong]** `deming2020`
- Occupational and industry mobility in the US was high and rising from 1968 to 1997, and human capital is largely occupation- or industry-specific. **[strong]** `kambourov2008`

**Where the "five-year half-life" comes from**

- The earliest traceable version found is a hedged aside in John Seely Brown's talk slides: "the half life of many stocks/skills is constantly shrinking. (perhaps as low as 5 years)". There is no data and no method. **[myth-or-unsupported]** as a statistic. `brown2026`
- By 2010 it circulated as a talk line: an attendee's notes of Brown's NMC plenary read "Most skills we teach have a half-life of about five years." **[weak]** `campbell2010`
- Secondary web pages also attribute similar numbers to IBM, LinkedIn or Gartner. None was traced to a primary report during this research.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the *direction* is real and measurable (`deming2020`): technical skills tied to specific tools lose market value, fastest in tech. The *number* is a guess that became a statistic. The better framing for writers is "your tools expire, your judgment compounds". That's the author's view, consistent with `deming2020`'s finding that ability matters relatively more in slower-changing careers, "because learning gains accumulate" (working paper).

## 3. Mid-career change

**Evidence**

- Occupation changes are common and became more common in the late 20th-century US (`kambourov2008` **[strong]**). The abstract does not give rates, so do not quote a number from it.
- Job switching and wage growth: Part I `switching.md` (`atlantafed-wgt`).
- Because skills are partly occupation-specific (`kambourov2008`), a change of occupation costs some accumulated human capital. Changing *within* a field preserves more.
- **Not verified (left out)**: popular claims such as "the average person changes careers 5-7 times". No primary source was opened. Writers should not use them.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the evidence base for "how to change careers mid-life" is thin compared with the rest of this file. What is well supported is the mechanism (specific skills depreciate on a switch, general ones transfer) plus the interest-development findings in `motivation.md` §3. Present mid-career change as a calculated trade, not a leap of faith. Part I covers the mechanics of getting the new job.

## 4. Age, performance and the shape of a working life

**Evidence**

- **Age and performance**: age is largely unrelated to core task performance, creativity and training performance, but has stronger relationships with citizenship, safety and counterproductive behaviour. The links with task performance and counterproductive behaviour are curvilinear. **[strong]** `ng2008`
- **The happiness U-curve**: across 145 countries, cross-sectional data show a U-shaped relation between age and well-being, with a low point **around age 50**. **[moderate]** `blanchflower2021`
- **The critique**: cross-sectional data can't show within-person change. Cross-sectional and longitudinal evidence is mixed, older adults recall midlife as one of the *better* periods, and the average curve hides very different individual paths. **[moderate]** `galambos2020`
- **Promotions** don't reliably raise happiness or health (`johnston2013`, `boyce2012` **[moderate]**). See `promotions.md` §7.

**Researcher's synthesis**: a "midlife dip" is a population average that may or may not describe a given reader. Present it as a contested finding, not a law. What a reader *can* act on are the plateau mechanisms in §1.
