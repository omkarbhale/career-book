## 1. Seniority-based pay and promotion (Japan)

**Evidence**

- Japan's two hallmark practices, **seniority-based wages** and **lifetime employment**, are eroding. In official wage-survey microdata (Basic Survey on Wage Structure), the age-wage profile has flattened, especially in mid- and late career. Since the early 2000s, the share of lifetime employees among younger university-educated workers has clearly fallen. The authors suggest young educated workers leave because seniority-based pay progression now looks poor, while older workers stay despite stagnating wages because outside options are worse. **[strong]** `hamaaki2012`

**Researcher's synthesis**: for readers in seniority-heavy systems (Japan historically, and many public-sector and unionised workplaces elsewhere), the "how to get promoted" advice in `promotions.md` applies less to *timing*, which follows tenure, and more to *placement*: which role and track you are put on. Even there, the evidence says the system is loosening. Sidebar framing: "Promotion clocks vs promotion contests."

**Gaps (not verified)**: current Japanese age-wage tables (JILPT's Japanese Working Life Profile lists them, but the tables were not opened); South Korea's seniority pay (*hobong*); seniority pay steps in government services (e.g. US federal step increases). Do not state specifics for these without opening a source.

## 2. Up-or-out systems

**Evidence (theory)**

- Up-or-out contracts (law-firm partnership, academic tenure, some consulting and military systems) are explained as a commitment device. The firm promises to promote or release, so it cannot under-report an employee's value, and the employee invests in firm-specific skills. **[moderate]** `kahn1988`

**Researcher's synthesis**: sidebar on "the clock": in up-or-out settings, the plateau of `long-game.md` §1 isn't allowed. You rise or leave. That clarifies the reader's decision but makes it high-stakes.

## 3. Title inflation

**Evidence**

- **US, strategic titling**: firms show a sharp jump in "manager" job titles for salaried jobs paid just above the federal overtime-exemption threshold ($455/week in the study period), with no such jump at nearby thresholds or for hourly jobs. Strategic titling is more than 50% higher where state worker protections are weaker, and is concentrated in retail, food service, janitorial and hotel work. **[moderate]** `cohen2023` (authors' summary of a working paper)
  - The authors cite a court case in which "store managers" spent most of their 60-90-hour weeks on manual work, and give examples of questionable titles such as "Food Cart Manager".
- **UK**: the share of retail-sector "managers" earning under 400 GBP a week rose from 37% to nearly 60% during the 2000s. **[weak]** `resolution2012` (think-tank press release)
- **Titles as compensation**: an equity-theory experiment tested whether a high-status title can compensate for underpayment (title-level only). `greenberg1983`
- **Titles as identity**: self-chosen, self-reflective titles reduced emotional exhaustion. **[moderate]** `grant2014`

**Researcher's synthesis**: titles are partly currency. Firms can pay in titles, sometimes to avoid paying in money (`cohen2023`). So the reader should compare offers and promotions on scope, level and pay, not title. This links to Part I `offers-negotiation.md` and to levels frameworks in `promotions.md` §6. In tech, levels (and their pay bands) carry the information that titles no longer do. That's the author's view; level-equivalence sites were not opened.

**Gaps (not verified)**: India-specific evidence on designation inflation in IT services; Singapore and Vietnam survey claims about firms inflating titles to retain staff (seen only in news snippets). Not opened, so not used.
