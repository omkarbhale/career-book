## 1. Dual ladders: the research

**Evidence**

- Nearly 1,500 engineers and scientists in nine US organisations were asked whether they wanted more managerial responsibility, technical-ladder advancement or simply more interesting technical work. Preferences shifted strongly with age **toward interesting project work over either kind of advancement**. The authors question whether dual ladders deliver what they promise. **[moderate]** `allen1986` (1980s R&D labs)
- In a dual-ladder R&D organisation, who you report to steered which ladder you climbed. Professionals under supervisors promoted on the technical ladder were more likely to be promoted technically. Those under "gatekeeper" supervisors in development were more likely to rise into management. **[moderate]** `katz1995`

**Practitioner**

- Some companies have **no** technical-leadership track and push experienced engineers into management. Where Staff-plus roles exist, they cluster into four archetypes: Tech Lead (most common, roughly one per eight engineers), Architect, Solver and Right Hand. **[weak]** `larson2020`
- Ladders "apply better against populations than people". **[weak]** `larson2020`. Dropbox's framework "is not meant to be a promotion checklist". **[weak]** `todi2021`

**Researcher's synthesis**: a dual ladder on paper is not a dual ladder in practice. Before choosing, the reader should find out whether people at their company actually reach senior IC levels, and who sponsors them (`katz1995`: your supervisor's track matters).

## 2. The best worker is not the best manager

**Evidence**

- Firms promote top salespeople into management even though top sales performance predicts *worse* team results as a manager. Collaboration experience predicts better results but is not rewarded. Workers who had never collaborated did especially poorly. **[strong]** `benson2019`. See `promotions.md` §2-3.
- A boss's **technical competence** is the single strongest predictor of a worker's job satisfaction in US and British survey data, and when a worker's boss becomes more competent, the worker's well-being rises. **[moderate]** `artz2017`
  - So technical depth is not irrelevant to management. Workers value bosses who could do their job. `benson2019` says the *best* doer isn't the best manager. `artz2017` says a *competent* doer makes a better boss. Both can be true.

**Researcher's synthesis**: managing is a different job that uses your technical skill as *judgment and credibility*, not as output. That's why great ICs often struggle at first (§3), and why competent-but-not-stellar ICs who collaborate well can be excellent managers.

## 3. The first-time manager transition

**Evidence**

- New first-line leaders learned their role faster when their own boss both **modelled** good leadership and **explained** the job ("show" and "tell"). The effect was strongest for those who had never had an exceptional leader themselves. Knowing the role better led them to spend more time actually leading. **[moderate]** `dragoni2014` (110 new leaders, 4 waves over about 10 months)

**Practitioner**

- Linda Hill (HBR summary of more than a decade of research on star performers becoming managers): most new managers "initially fail this test because of a set of common misconceptions about what it means to be in charge". **[weak]** `hill2007`. Only the summary could be read; the specific misconceptions and the book *Becoming a Manager* were not opened, so do not attribute details.
- "Management is not a promotion ... it's a change of profession", and "you will be bad at it for a long time after you start". **[weak]** `majors2017` (practitioner essay)

**Not verified (left out)**: the widely repeated statistic that "60% of new managers fail" or "underperform in their first two years". A web search found only secondary pages (training vendors, an executive-education page) attributing it to research by CEB, now Gartner. The wording varies between "fail" and "underperform", and no primary report or method was found. Do not print it as fact (`myths.md` #9).

## 4. Do managers earn more? Are they happier?

**Evidence: pay (US official statistics)**

- Median annual wage, May 2025: **software developers $135,980**, **computer and information systems managers $175,140**. The manager occupation typically requires "5 years or more" of related work experience. **[strong]** `bls2026a`, `bls2026b`
  - Occupation medians are not a like-for-like comparison of one person on two tracks. Senior ICs at large tech firms can out-earn many managers, but that comes from practitioner pay data not opened here.

**Evidence: well-being**

- Promotions in general raise satisfaction with the job and pay briefly, but have negligible effects on health and happiness, and mental health deteriorated two years later. **[moderate]** `johnston2013`. Promoted British workers showed worse psychological well-being. **[moderate]** `boyce2012`
- People in "contradictory class locations", which combine aspects of ownership and labour (the authors' relational class measure), had higher depression and anxiety than people in non-contradictory locations (n = 21,859 US full-time workers). **[moderate]** `prins2015` (cross-sectional; not the same thing as "middle manager")
- Gallup reported that manager engagement fell from 30% to 27% in 2024 while individual-contributor engagement stayed at 18%: "Managers are experiencing the sharpest decline." **[weak]** `gallup2025` (commercial publisher; note managers are still *more* engaged than ICs in these figures)

**Researcher's synthesis**: management usually pays more, and it brings more status and more stress, with no reliable gain in happiness. The evidence doesn't support "managers are miserable" or "management is the only way up". It supports *choosing the work you'd rather do every day*.

## 5. Senior and staff IC paths (practitioner)

All **[weak]**:

- Staff-plus archetypes (Tech Lead, Architect, Solver, Right Hand) and the observation that the Tech Lead role is often the first Staff role because it resembles senior work. `larson2020`
- The "pendulum": the best frontline engineering managers stay within a few years of hands-on work, and the best senior ICs have often done time in management; alternating builds both skill sets. `majors2017`
- Levels frameworks describe *impact at scope*, not tasks. `todi2021`

## 6. How to decide: a synthesis for writers

**Researcher's synthesis** (opinion, built on the evidence above):

| Question | Evidence it draws on |
|---|---|
| Do you want the *work* (one-to-ones, hiring, conflict, being interrupted, leverage through others), or the *title*? | `johnston2013`, `boyce2012` (the title doesn't make you happier); `majors2017` |
| Does your company have a real senior IC path, and who climbs it? | `katz1995`, `larson2020` |
| Are you energised by collaboration, or are you a "lone wolf"? | `benson2019` |
| Will your own manager show and tell you the job? | `dragoni2014` |
| Is it reversible here? | `majors2017` (pendulum, practitioner) |
| What will you stop getting better at? | `allen1986` (interest shifts toward the work itself with age); `deming2020` (fast-changing skills erode, `long-game.md`) |

- **Say this instead of "management is the next step"**: management is a *different* job, often better paid, with no reliable happiness bonus. Try it if the work itself appeals, and make sure you can come back.
