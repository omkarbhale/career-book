# Myths and popular claims about growth at work: verdicts

Part III research notes. Each claim gets a verdict, the evidence, a grade and source keys, plus a line writers can use instead.
Verdicts: **False** (contradicted by good evidence), **Unsupported** (no traceable evidence; may or may not be true), **Overstated** (a real effect inflated), **Half-true** (true core, misleading packaging), **Holds** (with caveats).
The grade is for the *evidence behind the verdict*. Keys resolve in `sources.yaml`, or in Part I / Part II where marked.

| # | Claim | Verdict | What the evidence says | Grade | Keys | Say this instead | File |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "70% of learning happens on the job, 20% from others, 10% from courses (70-20-10)." | **Unsupported (the numbers); half-true (the direction)** | A critical review finds the "70% rule" rests on weak evidence and "needs to be set aside". A qualitative study found 70:20:10 implementations assumed experience automatically builds capability, and it didn't. The origin is usually traced to 1980s executive-development research and a later planning tool (secondary sources only). | moderate | `clardy2018`, `johnson2018` | "Most of what you know about your job you learned doing it, but only when the doing came with feedback and some deliberate structure. The 70-20-10 split is a slogan, not a measurement." | getting-good.md |
| 2 | "Skills have a five-year half-life." | **Unsupported as a statistic; the direction holds** | The earliest traceable form is a hedged aside in John Seely Brown's slides ("perhaps as low as 5 years"), with no data; by 2010 it was a talk line. Real data: the earnings premium for applied STEM degrees falls by more than half in a decade as job skills change. | myth-or-unsupported (the number); strong (the direction) | `brown2026`, `campbell2010`, `deming2020` | "Tool-specific skills lose market value, fastest in tech. There is no measured half-life, but the decay is real." | long-game.md |
| 3 | "The Dunning-Kruger effect: the incompetent are the most confident." | **Overstated** | The 1999 study found bottom-quartile scorers placed themselves at the 62nd percentile while scoring at the 12th. Much of that pattern is produced by regression to the mean and the better-than-average effect; random numbers reproduce the classic graph. Better methods find a small effect, though a large replication supports a real (modest) metacognitive gap. Self-assessment and ability are positively correlated, so low performers are not the most confident. | strong (that the pop version is wrong); moderate (size of any true effect) | `kruger1999`, `krueger2002`, `nuhfer2016`, `gignac2020`, `gignac2024`, `jansen2021`, `zell2014` | "Most people think they're above average. Low performers overestimate the most, partly a statistical artefact and partly because spotting your own errors takes the skill you lack." | self-assessment.md |
| 4 | "The Peter Principle: you get promoted until you're incompetent." | **Half-true** | Firms do promote top performers on current-job results even when those results predict worse managerial performance (sales data from 131 firms). Some apparent decline after promotion is just regression to the mean after a lucky run. Nothing shows people rise *until* they are incompetent, and firms partly correct for it. | strong | `benson2019`, `lazear2004` | "Firms promote the best doer, not the best manager, and some post-promotion slump is just luck running out." | promotions.md |
| 5 | "Your performance rating measures your performance." | **Overstated** | In two large 360 datasets, rater idiosyncrasy explained 53-62% of rating variance and actual performance 21-25%. Two supervisors agree at about .52. Raters weigh the same facts differently, and ratings carry gender bias in language and "potential". | strong | `scullen2000`, `viswesvaran1996`, `rotundo2002`, `correll2020`, `benson2026` | "A rating is a noisy measurement in which the rater is part of the instrument. Treat one review as weak evidence about you." | reviews.md |
| 6 | "Stack ranking (forced distribution) makes teams better." | **Overstated** | A simulation shows possible early gains from firing the bottom, flattening after a few years. Experiments show forced ratings feel unfair, cut knowledge sharing and team speed, raise stress, and make ratings track gaming more than creativity. Microsoft dropped its curve in 2013. | moderate | `scullen2005`, `schleicher2009`, `loberg2021`, `cardinaels2021`, `shrm2013` | "Forced ranking may weed out some low performers early, but it costs collaboration and rating accuracy." | reviews.md |
| 7 | "Money stops buying happiness above $75,000." | **Overstated (and superseded)** | The 2010 plateau in emotional well-being did not hold in 1.7 million experience samples. An adversarial collaboration found flattening (around $100,000) only for the least happy ~15-20%; for most people happiness keeps rising with log income. Lottery winners' life satisfaction rises for over a decade. | strong | `kahneman2010`, `killingsworth2021`, `killingsworth2023`, `lindqvist2020` | "More money goes with more happiness for most people, with each doubling worth about the same. It doesn't fix the deepest miseries." | money.md |
| 8 | "Do what you love and you'll never work a day in your life." | **False as stated** | Deeply meaningful work brings *more* sacrifice of pay and time. Observers see exploiting passionate workers as more legitimate. Believing passion is fixed makes people drop interests when they get hard. The saying's attribution was not checked. | moderate | `bunderson2009`, `kim2020`, `okeefe2018` | "Loving your work is good for you and convenient for your employer. Keep negotiating and keep boundaries." | motivation.md |
| 9 | "60% of new managers fail (or underperform) in their first two years." | **Unsupported** | Found only on secondary pages attributing it to CEB (now Gartner) research; the wording varies and no primary report or method was found. Real evidence: new managers learn faster with a boss who shows and tells. | myth-or-unsupported | `dragoni2014` (for what is known) | "Becoming a manager is a hard transition, and having a boss who models and explains the job speeds it up." | the-fork.md |
| 10 | "Management is the natural next step / the only way up." | **Overstated** | Dual ladders exist but work unevenly, and your supervisor's track steers yours. Manager occupations pay more at the median (US $175,140 vs $135,980 for software developers in May 2025), but promotions don't reliably raise happiness or health. Engineers' preferences shift toward interesting work with age. | moderate | `allen1986`, `katz1995`, `bls2026a`, `bls2026b`, `johnston2013`, `boyce2012` | "Management is a different job, usually better paid, with no reliable happiness bonus. Choose the work, not the title." | the-fork.md |
| 11 | "Teach to people's learning styles (visual, auditory...)." | **False (as a basis for instruction)** | "Virtually no evidence" for matching instruction to style; properly designed studies "flatly contradict" it. | strong | `pashler2008` | "People have preferences, but matching instruction to them doesn't help. Testing yourself and spacing practice do." | getting-good.md |
| 12 | "A growth mindset transforms performance." | **Overstated** | Two meta-analyses found weak overall effects of mindset and of mindset interventions on achievement; low-SES or at-risk students may benefit. | strong | `sisk2018` | "Believing you can improve is fine, but practice design matters far more than the belief." | getting-good.md |
| 13 | "Years of experience make you better." | **Overstated** | Amount and task-level experience predict performance better than time served. Years of experience predict new-hire performance at only .07. Physicians' quality of care more often *declines* with years in practice. | strong | `quinones1995`, `sackett2022` (Part I), `choudhry2005` | "Ten years can be ten years of growth or one year repeated ten times. Reps with feedback count, not tenure." | getting-good.md |
| 14 | "Find your passion first." | **Overstated** | A fixed "find it" belief narrows interests and makes difficulty more demotivating; passion often develops with investment. But "fit" believers can also thrive. | moderate | `okeefe2018`, `chen2015` | "Interest usually grows with competence. You rarely know in advance where it will grow." | motivation.md |
| 15 | "A promotion will make you happier." | **Unsupported (on average)** | Promotions raise pay, satisfaction with the job and short-term status, but have negligible effects on happiness and health, and mental health worsens two years on. Healthy people are the ones who get promoted, not the reverse. | moderate | `johnston2013`, `boyce2012` | "Promotion is good for your pay and standing and roughly neutral for your mood. Want it for the job itself." | promotions.md |
| 16 | "External hires are better than internal promotions (that's why they're paid more)." | **False (in the best evidence)** | In one bank's data, external hires were paid around 18% more but performed worse for two years and were likelier to leave; they were promoted faster if they stayed. | strong (one firm) | `bidwell2011` | "Outsiders cost more and start slower. The inside path pays less but performs better early." | promotions.md |
| 17 | "Everyone hits a midlife slump around 45-50 (the happiness U-curve)." | **Contested** | Cross-sectional data from 145 countries show a U with a low point around 50, but critics show the longitudinal evidence is mixed and individual paths vary widely. | moderate | `blanchflower2021`, `galambos2020` | "On average, surveys find a midlife dip, but many people don't follow the average curve." | long-game.md |
| 18 | "10,000 hours of practice makes an expert." | **Overstated** (Part II verdict, extended) | Part II: practice explains under 1% of performance variance in professions. Part III adds: 1% among elite athletes, while Ericsson's reanalysis claims more for *strictly defined* deliberate practice. Both sides agree that design beats hours. | strong | `macnamara2014`, `macnamara2019` (Part II), `macnamara2016`, `ericsson2019` | "How you practise matters more than how long. At work, most of the job isn't practice at all unless you make it so." | getting-good.md |
| 19 | "A raise will keep me happy." | **Overstated** | Happiness adapts to income gains within about four years (status adapts less). Satisfaction falls over time in the same job even as wages rise, and losses hurt more than gains help. | moderate | `ditella2010`, `grund2007`, `boyce2013` | "You'll get used to the raise, so don't take a job you dislike for one." | money.md |
| 20 | "You know how good you are." / "Self-assessments are worthless." | **Both overstated** | Self-evaluations correlate about .29 with objective performance, which is real but moderate insight. They are better for specific, objective, familiar tasks. Self-ratings are lenient compared with supervisors in Western samples. | strong | `zell2014`, `freund2012`, `heidemeier2009` | "You have some insight, especially for concrete measurable tasks. Check it against evidence and other people." | self-assessment.md |

## Claims deliberately left out or marked unverified

These came up in research but could not be traced to a primary source opened during this task. **Do not print them as fact.**

- GE's "vitality curve" percentages (20/70/10): no primary GE source opened. (`reviews.md` §3)
- The "Sponsor Effect" percentages (Hewlett and colleagues): also unverified in Part II. (`promotions.md` §4)
- "60% of new managers fail" (CEB/Gartner): secondary only (#9).
- The 2024 "broken rung" figure of 81 women per 100 men: secondary coverage only; the 2025 figure (93) was verified. (`promotions.md` §4)
- "Skills half-life" figures attributed to IBM, LinkedIn or Gartner: secondary only (#2).
- The WEF *Future of Jobs 2025* figure for the share of core skills expected to change by 2030: the WEF site refused access.
- "The average person changes careers 5-7 times": no primary source opened. (`long-game.md` §3)
- "Workers would trade 23% of lifetime earnings for meaningful work": commercial survey, not opened. (`money.md` §5)
- Specific findings of Ashford and Tsui (1991) on negative vs positive feedback seeking: design confirmed, findings not readable. (`self-assessment.md` §4)
- Mentor-side benefits meta-analysis (Ghosh and Reio 2013): not readable. (`getting-good.md` §6)
- Specific big-tech promotion-cycle details (six-month "operating at level" rule, cycle months, named review systems): aggregator sites only. (`promotions.md` §6)
- Linda Hill's specific list of new-manager misconceptions: only the HBR summary was readable. (`the-fork.md` §3)
