## 1. Self-determination theory at work

**Evidence**

- Self-determination theory distinguishes **autonomous** motivation (intrinsic, or values you have fully made your own) from **controlled** motivation (pressure from outside or inside). Satisfying three needs, **competence, autonomy and relatedness**, promotes autonomous motivation, high-quality performance and wellness. **[strong]** `deci2017` (review by the theory's founders)
- Meta-analytic review of 99 studies (119 samples) on need satisfaction at work. It tests whether each need *separately* predicts growth, internalisation and well-being, and cautions about measures and methods. **[strong]** `vandenbroeck2016` (abstract-level; cite for scope, not specific effect sizes)
- **Intrinsic motivation and incentives work together.** Over 40 years of studies (k = 183, N = 212,468), intrinsic motivation predicted performance (ρ = .21-.45) whether or not incentives were present. Intrinsic motivation predicted *quality* better and incentives predicted *quantity* better. When incentives were tied directly to performance, intrinsic motivation mattered less ("crowding out"). **[strong]** `cerasoli2014`
- Job satisfaction and performance correlate about .30, with the direction of causation unresolved. **[strong]** `judge2001`
- Part II: job demands drive strain and resources buffer it (JD-R). `bakker2007` **[strong]**

**Researcher's synthesis**: this gives the book a clean three-part checklist the reader can apply to any job: *am I getting better at something (competence), do I have some say in how I work (autonomy), and do I have people (relatedness)?* It ties back to Part II's first-principles theme: who you are drives how you work, not an act.

## 2. Job crafting: reshaping the job you have

**Evidence**

- **Theory**: employees "craft" their jobs by changing **task** boundaries (what they do), **relational** boundaries (who they work with) and **cognitive** boundaries (how they think about the work). This changes the meaning of the work and their work identity. **[moderate]** `wrzesniewski2001`
- **Interventions work, modestly**: across 14 studies, job-crafting training raised crafting behaviour (g = 0.26), work engagement (g = 0.31) and contextual performance (g = 0.39). Task-performance gains appeared only among healthcare workers, and performance effects ran through engagement. **[moderate]** `oprea2019`
- **Titles as crafting**: employees who wrote their own self-reflective job titles reported less emotional exhaustion five weeks later. **[moderate]** `grant2014`

**Researcher's synthesis**: before deciding the job is wrong, try changing it from inside: add a task you'd like to master, shift which colleagues you spend time with, reframe who the work helps. The intervention evidence says this helps a bit, reliably. It connects to the plateau evidence (`long-game.md` §1): proactivity and added responsibilities go with less plateauing.

## 3. "Follow your passion" vs developing interests

**Evidence**

- People who believe interests are **fixed** ("find your passion") show less interest outside their existing interests and expect boundless motivation once they find a passion. When a new interest becomes hard, their interest drops faster than for people led to see interests as **developed**. "Urging people to find their passion may lead them to put all their eggs in one basket but then to drop that basket when it becomes difficult to carry." **[moderate]** `okeefe2018` (five studies, mostly students)
- **Counterweight**: "fit" theorists (passion comes from finding the right line of work) and "develop" theorists (passion is cultivated) show different priorities, but **both beliefs can lead to vocational well-being and success**. **[moderate]** `chen2015`
- **Practitioner**: "'Follow your passion' is bad advice" (`newport2012` **[weak]**, title and blurb only). Career capital first (`todd2026` **[weak]**).

**Researcher's synthesis**: the evidence doesn't say passion is bad. It says that expecting passion to be pre-formed and effortless backfires when the work gets hard. The book's honest line: **interest usually grows with competence and investment, and you rarely know in advance which field it will grow in**. That links back to SDT's competence need (§1).

## 4. The double edge of meaningful work

**Evidence**

- **Callings bind as well as ennoble.** Zookeepers with a strong calling found deep meaning in their work but were more likely to treat it as a moral duty and to **sacrifice pay, personal time and comfort** for it. **[moderate]** `bunderson2009` (interviews plus a survey across 157 zoos)
- **"Passion exploitation."** Across seven studies and a meta-analysis, observers judged unpaid extra hours and demeaning off-role tasks as **more legitimate when the worker was seen as passionate**. They assumed passionate workers would volunteer anyway and that the work is its own reward. **[moderate]** `kim2020`
- **People pay for meaning.** People accept lower minimum salaries for work they find meaningful. Workers with more meaningful work were more likely to turn down higher-paying offers elsewhere, and this effect grew from 2005 to 2015. **[moderate]** `hu2017`
- Burnout itself is covered in Part II `burnout.md`: the WHO definition (`who2019`) and evidence that organisation-directed interventions beat individual ones (`panagioti2017`).

**Researcher's synthesis**: meaningful work is good for you, and it is also the easiest thing for an employer to underpay. The book can say both: love the work, and still negotiate (Part I `offers-negotiation.md`) and keep boundaries (Part II `relationships.md` on boundaries, and `burnout.md`).

## 5. "Do what you love and you'll never work a day in your life"

- No study supports the literal claim. The evidence above points the other way: deeply meaningful work comes with *more* sacrifice (`bunderson2009`), passion can license exploitation (`kim2020`), and fixed-passion beliefs make difficulty more demotivating (`okeefe2018`).
- The origin of the saying (often attributed to Confucius or Mark Twain) was **not checked** and should not be attributed.
- See `myths.md` #8.
