## 1. Workplace friendship: real benefits, real costs

**Evidence**

- Having more "multiplex" relationships (coworkers who are also friends) related to *higher* supervisor-rated performance, through trust and positive mood, but also to emotional exhaustion and the effort of maintaining them. The authors call it "a mixed blessing". **[moderate]** `methot2016` (two field samples, n = 168 and 182)
- Theory: friendship's defining features (informal, voluntary, communal, emotional) clash with work's (formal roles, constraints, exchange norms, goals), which explains the predictable downsides. **[moderate]** `pillemer2018`
- Friendship closeness tracks hours spent together, especially in leisure and everyday talk. The press release puts it at roughly 50 hours to casual friend, 90 to friend and 200+ to close friend, and notes that time spent working together counts less. **[moderate]** `hall2019`. The hour figures are approximate.
- After conversations, people underestimate how much the other person liked them (the "liking gap"). The gap persisted for months among new dorm-mates and appears in work teams, largest between peers. **[strong]** `boothby2018`; `mastroianni2021` **[moderate]**.
- People underestimate others' interest in connecting and expect solitude to be more pleasant than it turns out. **[strong]** `epley2014`

**Gallup's "best friend at work"**

- Gallup says having a "best friend at work" has become *more* strongly tied to outcomes since the pandemic. **[weak]** `gallup2022`. This is commercial and self-interested: Gallup sells the Q12 survey containing the item, and the page shows no method or effect sizes.
- **Researcher's synthesis**: the item probably captures something real, having someone you trust and can lean on, which agrees with `methot2016` and the loneliness evidence below. But "best friend" is a survey wording, not a scientific construct, and nothing here supports *mandating* friendship. Grade the claim "a best friend at work drives performance" as **[weak]**, and the claim "close, trusting work relationships are associated with better outcomes, with costs" as **[moderate]**.

## 2. When your friend becomes your manager (or you become theirs)

**Evidence** (thin)

- In employee narratives, *promotion* was one of five main causes of workplace friendships deteriorating. People mostly pulled back indirectly, by avoiding personal topics and socialising less. **[weak]** `sias2004` (exploratory qualitative study; the only direct evidence found)
- Framework: friendship's communal norms clash with a manager's formal role and evaluative duties. **[moderate]** `pillemer2018`
- Leader-member exchange research shows that leaders form relationships of different quality with each report, and these shape outcomes. **[strong]** `graen1995`, `dulebohn2012`. Visible favouritism toward a friend is the obvious risk.

**Researcher's synthesis** (opinion): name the change out loud early, separate the evaluative conversations from the social ones, be visibly fair, and accept that the friendship will change shape. Do not pretend nothing happened. The evidence base here is weak, and the book should say so.

## 3. Favours and the Ben Franklin effect

**Evidence**

- Original study (1969): people asked personally by the experimenter to return prize money liked him more than people who were not asked. A single small study; no direct replication found. **[weak]** `jecker1969`
- Modern conceptual replication: Japanese and American participants liked a confederate *more* after she asked them for help, but not when they helped unasked. **[moderate]** `niiya2016` (small samples)
- Direct requests get "yes" far more often than requesters expect. **[strong]** `flynn2008`
- Asking for advice raises perceived competence. **[moderate]** `brooks2015` (flagged co-author)

**Verdict**: "ask someone for a small favour and they'll like you more" is **[weak]**-to-**[moderate]**. Plausible, partially replicated, effect size unknown. Present it as "a decent bet with thin evidence", not as a law. See also `myths.md`.

## 4. Gossip

**Evidence**

- Definition from a systematic review of 324 definitions: a sender talking to a receiver about an absent (or unaware) target. Gossip can be positive, neutral or negative. **[strong]** `dorescruz2021`
- In naturalistic audio sampling (N = 467), most gossip was *neutral* and about social information. Extraverts gossiped more, and the gender stereotype largely failed. **[moderate]** `robbins2020`. A widely quoted "52 minutes a day" figure was not in the abstract checked; don't use it unless verified.
- "Prosocial gossip", warning others about someone who behaved exploitatively, is driven by frustration at unfairness, relieves that frustration, and deters selfishness. **[moderate]** `feinberg2012`
- Gossip plus exclusion sustains cooperation in groups. **[moderate]** `feinberg2014`

**Researcher's synthesis**: gossip is the office's reputation system (callback to `first-principles.md` §1), so "never gossip" is unrealistic advice. Workable norms (author's view): share information that protects people; don't share what you'd be ashamed to have repeated; assume it will be repeated; and be the kind of person about whom the neutral gossip is good.

## 5. Office romance

**Evidence**

- SHRM (2023): 27% of US workers say they are or have been in a workplace romance; of those, 57% cite love as the motivation, 1% job-related reasons. **[weak]** `shrm2023` (professional-association survey, press-release detail only)
- Coworkers see a peer dating a *superior* as job-motivated and as getting unfair advantages; they trust that peer less and share less honestly with them. **[moderate]** `malachowski2012` (212 working adults, vignettes)
- Theory model of formation and effects on the couple, coworkers and the organisation. **[moderate]** `pierce1996`

**Gaps**: no rigorous outcome data were found on breakups at work or on policy effectiveness. Company policies vary (disclosure rules, bans on dating within a reporting line). The book should not invent typical policy statistics.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the key variable is *power*. Romance between peers is mostly a private matter with some gossip risk; romance across a reporting line damages trust in both people and creates real conflict-of-interest and harassment risk.

## 6. Alcohol and work events

**Evidence**

- US national survey: about 15% of workers reported workplace alcohol use or impairment in the past year (9.2% worked with a hangover; 7.1% drank during the workday). Managers reported elevated rates. **[strong]** `frone2006` (data from the early 2000s)
- A moderate dose of alcohol increased positive emotional displays and self-reported bonding among groups of strangers (N = 720 in triads). **[strong]** `sayette2012`. This explains the after-work drinks ritual; it says nothing about what non-drinkers lose.
- Drinkers in US survey data earned 10 to 14% more than abstainers, and men who went to bars monthly about 7% more again; the authors attribute it to social capital. **[weak]** `peters2006`. Correlational, confounded, promoted by a think-tank brief linked to one author.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the bonding is real, but the active ingredient is *showing up and being socially available*, not the alcohol. Non-drinkers can get most of the benefit by attending, staying for the first part, and holding a drink of any kind (author's view). Organisers should design events that don't centre on alcohol.

## 7. Boundaries

**Evidence**

- People differ in whether they want work and home separate or blended, and fit with the workplace predicts conflict, stress and satisfaction. **[moderate]** `rothbard2005`, `kreiner2006`; boundary theory `ashforth2000` **[strong]**.
- Mentally detaching from work during off-hours relates to better life satisfaction and lower strain, with no loss of engagement at work. **[strong]** `sonnentag2012`
- A higher-status person's disclosures of weakness can cost them influence. **[moderate]** `gibson2018` (see `authenticity.md` §5).

## 8. Loneliness at work

**Evidence**

- About 1 in 5 employees worldwide reported feeling lonely "a lot of the day yesterday": fully remote 25%, hybrid 21%, on-site 16%. **[moderate]** `gallup2024` (large survey, commercial publisher, single item, not causal)
- Lonelier employees had lower supervisor-rated performance, explained by being seen as less approachable and feeling less committed. **[moderate]** `ozcelik2018` (672 employees, 114 supervisors, time-lagged)
- The US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness addresses workplaces, but its pages could not be opened during this research (HTTP 403), so it is **not** in the registry. Writers who want it must verify it first.
