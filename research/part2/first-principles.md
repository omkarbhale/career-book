# First principles: cooperation, reputation, trust, reciprocity, warmth and competence

Research notes for Part II (conduct at work). These are notes for writers, not book prose.
Citations are registry keys in `sources.yaml`. Each finding is tagged with its grade:
**[strong]**, **[moderate]**, **[weak]** or **[myth]** (see `README.md`).
Labels used throughout:

- **Evidence**: what studies found.
- **Practitioner model**: a framework from consultants or authors, not tested the way a study is.
- **Researcher's synthesis**: my reading of the evidence, offered as opinion for the writers to accept or reject.

## 1. Why behaviour at work is a repeated game

**Evidence**

- In a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, cooperation based on reciprocity can get started among mostly selfish players, beat a wide range of strategies, and resist invasion once it is common. The key condition is that players expect to meet again ("the shadow of the future"). **[strong]** `axelrod1981`
- Cooperation can also spread through *reputation*: I help you, and a third party who saw it helps me later ("indirect reciprocity"). This works when the chance of knowing someone's reputation is high enough relative to the cost of helping. **[moderate]** `nowak1998`; the five main mechanisms are reviewed in `nowak2006` **[strong]**.
- Many people will pay a personal cost to punish free-riders, even with no future gain to themselves, and this threat makes free-riding less common. **[strong]** `fehr2000`
- Gossip is one way reputations travel. In experiments, sharing reputational information plus the option to exclude people sustained cooperation, and excluded people then cooperated more. **[moderate]** `feinberg2014` (see `relationships.md` §4).

**Caveat**: these are game-theory models and lab money games. Applying them to an office is an *analogy*, a good one, and the book should say so.

**Researcher's synthesis**

A workplace is a long, repeated, many-player game with an audience. Three consequences follow, and the rest of Part II keeps returning to them:

1. **Every interaction is also a reputation event.** You are not dealing only with the person in front of you. You are dealing with everyone they will talk to.
2. **Short-term "wins" that break trust are expensive.** In one-shot games, defecting pays. At work, almost nothing is one-shot.
3. **Being "nice but not a pushover" is a strategy, not just a personality.** The tit-for-tat spirit is: start by cooperating, respond to what others do, forgive quickly, and stay clear and predictable. It maps cleanly onto workplace advice. It is still an analogy, not a tested workplace intervention.

Book angle: a running device, "the shadow of the future", that later chapters on gossip, credit, leaving well and ethics can call back to.

## 2. Trust: what people are actually judging

**Evidence**

- The dominant model defines trust as *willingness to be vulnerable* to someone, based on their perceived **ability**, **benevolence** (they care about your interests) and **integrity** (they keep to principles you accept). **[strong]** `mayer1995`
- A meta-analysis of 132 samples found all three predict trust. Trustworthiness predicted risk-taking, task performance and citizenship behaviour, and lower counterproductive behaviour, partly through trust and partly directly. **[strong]** `colquitt2007`
- Trust is easier to lose than to build. Across many domains, negative events and impressions weigh more heavily and form faster than positive ones. **[strong]** `baumeister2001`
- How you repair trust depends on what broke it. Apologising worked better for competence failures; for integrity accusations, denial worked better unless guilt was later proven. **[strong]** `kim2004` (see `mistakes.md`).

**Practitioner model**

- Maister, Green and Galford's **Trust Equation**: trustworthiness = (credibility + reliability + intimacy) / self-orientation. **[weak]** as evidence, since no published validation was found, but it is a vivid teaching device. `maister2000`
  - Rough mapping (researcher's synthesis): credibility ≈ ability; reliability ≈ integrity over time; intimacy and low self-orientation ≈ benevolence. The equation's one useful addition is putting *self-orientation in the denominator*: people discount everything else you offer if they think you are mainly about you.
  - Flag: promoted by the authors' consulting firm.

**Book angle**: a three-dial diagram (Ability / Benevolence / Integrity) is accurate to the evidence. The Trust Equation can appear as a clearly labelled "practitioner's version".

## 3. Reciprocity: the oldest rule in the office

**Evidence**

- Gouldner proposed a near-universal moral norm: help those who helped you, and don't harm them. **[strong]** as foundational theory. `gouldner1960`
- Classic test: participants who got an unsolicited soft drink from a confederate later bought more of his raffle tickets, and this did not depend on how much they liked him. **[moderate]** `regan1971`. Secondary accounts disagree on the exact multiplier, so cite the direction, not a number.
- The obligation fades. People returned a small favour 5 minutes later but not reliably a week later. **[moderate]** `burger1997`
- Reviews place reciprocity among the core processes of social influence, often operating without awareness. **[strong]** `cialdini2004`
- People underestimate by as much as 50% how likely others are to say yes to a direct request for help, because they forget how awkward refusing is. **[strong]** `flynn2008`

**Generosity, status and its costs**

- In one firm, people seen as generous in exchanging favours had higher status, while those who kept exchanges balanced were more productive. Exchanging favours *frequently* eased the trade-off. **[moderate]** `flynn2003`
- Helping beyond your job description (organizational citizenship behaviour) relates to better performance ratings and rewards, and to better unit performance. **[strong]** `podsakoff2009`
- But helping has costs: time taken from core tasks (a theoretical argument, `bergeron2007` **[moderate]**), role overload, stress and work-family conflict, stronger for women (`bolino2005` **[moderate]**). Women are asked more often, and say yes more often, to "non-promotable" tasks. **[strong]** `babcock2017`. Reviewed in `bolino2016` **[strong]**.

**Researcher's synthesis**: reciprocity is the engine of office relationships. Give first, give often in small amounts, and return favours fairly promptly. But be generous *strategically*: say yes to help that builds relationships and skills, and watch how many invisible, unpromotable tasks you take on (see `power.md` §6 on saying no).

## 4. Warmth and competence: the two questions everyone asks about you

**Evidence**

- People judge others, and groups, on two main dimensions: **warmth** (are your intentions good toward me?) and **competence** (can you act on them?). Many judgments are mixed, such as warm-but-incompetent (pitied) or competent-but-cold (envied). **[strong]** `fiske2002`, `fiske2007`
- Warmth (communion) tends to matter more when judging *others*, and agency (competence) when judging *yourself*. That gap is a classic source of misread workplace behaviour. **[moderate]** `abele2007`
- The framework has been applied to organisations. **[moderate]** `cuddy2011`
- Asking for advice raises perceived competence rather than lowering it. **[moderate]** `brooks2015`, flagged because a co-author has retracted papers elsewhere.
- Humour that lands signals confidence and competence and raises status; humour that fails lowers it. **[strong]** `bitterly2017` (see `formality.md`).

**Researcher's synthesis**: much of Part II amounts to managing both dials honestly at once. Being "overly professional" can read as cold; being too casual can read as less competent (see `formality.md` on emoji and swearing). Authentic conduct means being genuinely warm *and* genuinely good at the work, not performing either one.

## 5. Small acts, big asymmetries

- Rudeness, even witnessed or imagined, reduced performance and helpfulness in experiments. **[moderate]** `porath2007`
- Incivility can spiral as each side returns slightly more than it received. **[moderate]**, theory. `andersson1999`
- Bad is stronger than good. **[strong]** `baumeister2001`. A popular "5:1 positive-to-negative ratio" or "2.9013 positivity ratio" is **not** supported (see `myths.md`: Losada ratio).
