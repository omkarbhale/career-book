# Being new: onboarding, the first 90 days, first impressions

Research notes for Part II. Grades and labels as in `README.md`; keys refer to `sources.yaml`.

---

## 1. What predicts a good start

**Evidence**

- Meta-analysis of 70 newcomer samples: **role clarity, self-efficacy and social acceptance** are the adjustment variables that link organisational socialisation tactics and newcomer information-seeking to satisfaction, commitment, performance and staying. **[strong]** `bauer2007`
- New accountants who sought information more often mastered their jobs, understood their roles and the culture, and integrated socially better over six months. **[moderate]** `morrison1993`
- Newcomers use proactive tactics: information and feedback seeking, relationship building, negotiating job changes, positive framing. Only some related to self-reported performance and satisfaction. **[moderate]** `ashford1996`
- Feedback-seeking declines with tenure, so the early months are the cheapest time to ask. **[strong]** `anseel2015`
- Inviting newcomers to express their "best selves" during onboarding improved retention and customer satisfaction. **[moderate]** `cable2013` (flagged co-author)
- Junior engineers gained the most from sitting near teammates (more feedback, better code). **[strong]** `emanuel2026`

**Researcher's synthesis**: a newcomer's three jobs are clarity (what does good look like here?), competence (small early wins) and connection (be accepted). This "three Cs" device is the author's framing of `bauer2007`'s three adjustment variables, and it is accurate to them.

## 2. Asking questions and asking for help

- Asking for advice makes you look *more* competent, especially on hard problems. **[moderate]** `brooks2015` (flagged co-author)
- People say yes to direct requests far more often than you expect. **[strong]** `flynn2008`

## 3. Social anxiety in a new place

- After conversations, people underestimate how much they were liked (the "liking gap"), and it persists for months among new acquaintances. **[strong]** `boothby2018`; it appears in work teams too. **[moderate]** `mastroianni2021`
- People expect talking to strangers to be worse than it is, and underestimate others' interest. **[strong]** `epley2014`
- People overestimate how much others notice their appearance and actions. **[strong]** `gilovich2000`
- Friendship takes time: roughly 50 hours to become casual friends, and work time counts less than leisure time. **[moderate]** `hall2019`

## 4. First impressions

- Trait judgments from faces form within about 100 ms and barely change with more time; more time mostly adds confidence. **[strong]** `willis2006`
- Silent clips under 30 seconds predicted teacher evaluations. **[moderate]** `ambady1993`
- Warmth tends to be read first, then competence. **[strong]** `fiske2007`
- **Caveat**: fast is not accurate, and impressions do update with information. "You never get a second chance at a first impression" overstates the case: bad first impressions are sticky (`baumeister2001`), not permanent.

## 5. The "first 90 days"

**Practitioner model**

- Watkins's *The First 90 Days*: reach the "breakeven point", when the organisation needs you as much as you need the job, faster by accelerating learning, matching strategy to the situation and securing early wins. **[weak]** as evidence. `watkins2003`
- **Myth check**: "90 days" is a convention (and often a probation period), not an empirically derived threshold. No study found supports 90 days as special. See `myths.md`.

## 6. Calibrating as the new person

- Breaking formality norms raises status only for people already credited with status and familiar with the norms. **[moderate]** `bellezza2014`
- Smileys in first contact lowered perceived competence. **[moderate]** `glikson2018`

**Researcher's synthesis**: as a newcomer, start slightly more formal and observant, then relax as you learn the norms. Ask lots of questions early, while asking is cheap.
