# Team dynamics: psychological safety, feedback, conflict, difficult conversations

## 1. Psychological safety

**Evidence**

- Edmondson defined team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. In 51 teams, it predicted learning behaviour, which predicted performance. **[strong]** `edmondson1999`
- Its roots: hospital units with better relationships and more coaching had *higher* detected drug-error rates. Edmondson proposed this reflected more willingness to report, not more errors. **[moderate]** `edmondson1996` (see `mistakes.md`).
- Meta-analysis (136 samples, over 22,000 people, nearly 5,000 groups): psychological safety relates to task performance and citizenship beyond related constructs. **[strong]** `frazier2017`; review `edmondson2014` **[strong]**.
- **Limit**: psychological safety can also lower average work motivation ("slacking off in comfort"), with the net effect depending on context. **[moderate]** `deng2019`. Researcher's synthesis: safety is not the same as low standards, and the book should present it as "safe to speak up", not "comfortable".

**Google's Project Aristotle and its limits**

- Google's re:Work guide says that in its study of 180 teams (115 engineering, 65 sales), *how* a team worked together mattered more than *who* was on it. In order of importance: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, impact. **[weak]** `google2015`
- Limits: an internal study with no published data or effect sizes, not peer-reviewed, from one unusual company; the page is now an archived "record". It is popular because it agrees with peer-reviewed work (`edmondson1999`, `frazier2017`), and that work is what the book should lean on.

## 2. Feedback: giving it

**Evidence**

- The classic meta-analysis (607 effect sizes): feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), but **more than a third made performance worse**. Feedback helps less as it draws attention from the task to the self. **[strong]** `kluger1996`
- The "feedback sandwich" (praise, criticism, praise): medical students *believed* it improved their work, but it did not. **[weak]** `parkes2013` (narrow setting). Enough to say the sandwich lacks evidence, not that it always fails.
- Speaking up with *challenging* ideas led managers to rate employees as worse performers, compared with *supportive* voice. **[moderate]** `burris2012`; voice and silence reviewed in `morrison2014` **[strong]**.

**Practitioner model**

- Kim Scott's *Radical Candor*: "care personally and challenge directly"; the failure modes are ruinous empathy, obnoxious aggression and manipulative insincerity. **[weak]** as evidence: no peer-reviewed test was found, and it is sold through workshops. `scott2017`
- **Researcher's synthesis**: Radical Candor's core advice (be specific, keep feedback about the task and behaviour, show you are on the person's side) agrees with `kluger1996`'s finding that feedback aimed at the *self* backfires. The 2×2 grid is a teaching device, not a validated model.

## 3. Feedback: seeking and receiving it

- Feedback-seeking declines with tenure and age and rises with learning orientation and good leader relationships. Its *direct* link to performance is small. **[strong]** `anseel2015`
- New joiners who seek information more adjust better. **[moderate]** `morrison1993` (see `newcomers.md`).
- Asking for advice raises perceived competence. **[moderate]** `brooks2015` (flagged co-author)

## 4. Conflict: task vs relationship

**Evidence**

- Early field study: task conflict hurt routine-task groups but not non-routine ones; norms encouraging open conflict *increased* relationship conflict. **[moderate]** `jehn1995`
- Meta-analysis (2003): *both* relationship and task conflict related negatively to team performance and satisfaction, contradicting the textbook claim that task conflict helps. **[strong]** `dedreu2003`
- Updated meta-analysis (116 studies, 8,880 groups): relationship and process conflict are reliably bad. Task conflict has no reliable overall negative effect and can help when relationship conflict is low, in top management teams, and for decision quality. **[strong]** `dewit2012`

**Researcher's synthesis**: the useful rule is to disagree about the work while protecting the relationship. Task conflict is only "good" when it doesn't leak into relationship conflict, so the skill is keeping the two apart (e.g. separating idea from person, and making disagreement routine rather than personal).

## 5. Difficult conversations

**Evidence** (adjacent)

- Venting anger while ruminating on the provoker *increased* anger and aggression; doing nothing worked better. **[moderate]** `bushman2002`
- Incivility spirals and reduces performance and helping. **[moderate]** `andersson1999`, `porath2007`
- One persistently negative team member (withholding effort, expressing negativity, violating norms) can damage the whole team. **[moderate]** `felps2006`. A widely reported "planted bad apple" experiment was **not** verified here.

**Practitioner models**: books such as *Difficult Conversations* and *Crucial Conversations* are widely used. They were **not** reviewed or verified in this research pass and have no entries in the registry. If the book uses them, cite them as practitioner models and verify first.

## 6. Collective intelligence: a cautionary claim

- A widely reported 2010 paper found a "c factor" for groups, linked to social sensitivity and equal turn-taking. **[weak]** `woolley2010`
- A reanalysis of six samples found weak support for a general factor. **[strong]** `crede2017`
- **Verdict**: don't present "collective intelligence" as established. Equal airtime as a good meeting norm can be argued on other grounds.
