# Myths and over-claims: verified, debunked or downgraded

Research notes for Part II. Each myth gets a verdict, the evidence, and a one-line "say this instead" for writers.
Verdicts: **Debunked** (contradicted by strong evidence), **Overstated** (a kernel of truth, stretched too far), **Unsupported** (no good evidence either way), **Holds (with caveats)**.
Keys refer to `sources.yaml`.

---

## 1. "93% of communication is non-verbal" (Mehrabian's 7-38-55): Debunked as usually stated

- Origin: two 1967 lab studies of single words and facial expressions conveying like or dislike. `mehrabian1967a`, `mehrabian1967b`
- Mehrabian's own website says the 7%/38%/55% equation came from experiments on communicating *feelings and attitudes*, and "unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable". **[strong]** source for the debunk. `mehrabian1971`
- **Say this instead**: when words and tone *conflict* about how someone feels, people tend to believe the tone and face. That's all the studies show.

## 2. Power poses change your hormones and boldness: Debunked (the hormones); Overstated (the feelings)

- Original claim: two one-minute expansive poses raised testosterone, lowered cortisol and increased risk-taking (N = 42). `carney2010`
- A larger replication found no hormonal or behavioural effect, only higher self-reported feelings of power. **[strong]** `ranehill2015`
- A p-curve of the 33 supporting studies found no evidential value. **[strong]** `simmons2017`
- The original first author: "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real." **[strong]** `carney2016`
- Proponents' reply claimed evidence for *felt* power (`cuddy2018` **[weak]**). A critique showed that effects come mainly from slumped poses making people feel *worse*, not expansive poses making them feel better. **[moderate]** `crede2019`
- A journal special issue of pre-registered power-pose studies was published in 2017; only its editorial was checked here, so cite it for its existence, not for specific results. **[weak]** `jonas2017`
- Meta-analysis (128 studies, 10,000+ people): expansive or upright postures modestly raise self-reported confidence compared with slumped ones; no evidence for hormone effects; behavioural effects partly reflect study design. **[strong]** `korner2022`
- **Say this instead**: don't slump before a big meeting; it may make you feel a little worse. Standing tall won't change your hormones.

## 3. "People leave managers, not companies": Overstated

- Source of the slogan's statistics: Gallup says half of 7,272 US adults had at some point left a job "to get away from their manager", and that managers account for "at least" (elsewhere "up to") 70% of the variance in team engagement. **[weak]** `gallup2015` (commercial; Gallup sells manager development)
- Pew 2022: low pay (63%), lack of advancement (63%) and feeling disrespected (57%) were the most-cited reasons for quitting in 2021. **[strong]** `pew2022`
- Turnover meta-analyses show many causes. **[strong]** `rubenstein2018`, `griffeth2000`
- **Say this instead**: a bad manager is one of several big reasons people leave, alongside pay and progression. Managers matter a lot, but not to the exclusion of everything else.

## 4. "10,000 hours makes you an expert": Overstated

- Origin: deliberate-practice research on music students, later popularised as "10,000 hours". `ericsson1993` **[moderate]**
- Meta-analysis: deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and **less than 1% in professions**. **[strong]** `macnamara2014`
- Direct replication of the violin study did not reproduce the core finding. **[strong]** `macnamara2019`
- **Say this instead**: practice matters, especially in structured domains, but at work, hours alone explain little. Feedback, the right tasks and the environment matter too.

## 5. The "critical positivity ratio" (2.9:1, or Losada's team ratio): Debunked

- Claim: flourishing people (and high-performing teams) have positive-to-negative ratios above about 2.9. `fredrickson2005`
- The fluid-dynamics mathematics was misapplied and the claim is "entirely unfounded". **[strong]** `brown2013`; a formal correction was published (`fredrickson2013`).
- **Say this instead**: negative interactions weigh more than positive ones (`baumeister2001`), but there is no magic ratio.

## 6. The Ben Franklin effect: Holds weakly

- One small 1969 study (`jecker1969` **[weak]**), plus a small modern conceptual replication (`niiya2016` **[moderate]**).
- **Say this instead**: asking for a small favour probably doesn't hurt and may help a relationship, and people say yes more than you expect (`flynn2008` **[strong]**).

## 7. "Task conflict is good for teams": Overstated

- Meta-analysis found task conflict *negatively* related to performance (`dedreu2003` **[strong]**); a later one found no reliable overall effect, with benefits only when relationship conflict stays low (`dewit2012` **[strong]**).
- **Say this instead**: disagreeing about the work can help, *if* it doesn't become personal.

## 8. "Group brainstorming produces more ideas": Debunked

- Groups produce fewer and no better ideas than the same people working alone. **[strong]** `mullen1991`

## 9. "Open-plan offices increase collaboration": Debunked (in the best evidence)

- Face-to-face interaction fell about 70% after two headquarters moved to open plans. **[moderate]** `bernstein2018`

## 10. "Venting anger gets it out of your system": Debunked

- Venting while ruminating increased anger and aggression. **[moderate]** `bushman2002`

## 11. "Millennials / Gen Z are fundamentally different at work": Overstated

- Generational differences in job attitudes are small to near zero and partly reflect age or tenure. **[moderate]** `costanza2012`

## 12. "Emotional intelligence matters more than IQ": Overstated

- EI correlates about .24 to .30 with job performance and adds some prediction beyond IQ and personality (`oboyle2011` **[strong]**); ability EI's link to performance is inconsistent and depends on emotional labour (`joseph2010` **[strong]**).
- **Say this instead**: emotional skills matter at work, modestly. "More than IQ" is not supported.

## 13. "Dunbar's number: you can only maintain 150 relationships": Unsupported

- Reanalysis gives averages from about 16 to 109 with confidence intervals of roughly 2 to 520; no single limit can be derived this way. **[strong]** `lindenfors2021`

## 14. "Willpower is a limited resource" (ego depletion): Overstated

- 23-lab registered replication: effect near zero (d = 0.04). **[strong]** `hagger2016`

## 15. The Hawthorne effect (as usually told): Debunked

- The original illumination data show the famous dramatic patterns were "entirely fictional"; only subtle effects remain. **[strong]** `levitt2011`

## 16. "Multitasking makes you worse at everything": Overstated

- Heavy media multitaskers did worse at filtering distraction in an influential study (`ophir2009` **[moderate]**), but later evidence is mixed and causality unclear (`uncapher2018` **[moderate]**).

## 17. "It takes 23 minutes to get back on task after an interruption": Unsupported (as attributed)

- The usual citation (`mark2008`) reports that interrupted people worked *faster* with no quality loss but more stress. The "23 minutes" figure is not among its reported findings. **[moderate]** for what the paper does show.

## 18. Popular "feedback sandwich": Unsupported

- Students believed it helped; it didn't improve performance. **[weak]** `parkes2013`. One third of feedback interventions backfire anyway. **[strong]** `kluger1996`

## 19. "The first 90 days" as a magic window: Unsupported as a number

- A practitioner convention (`watkins2003` **[weak]**); no evidence that 90 days is special. Early adjustment does matter (`bauer2007` **[strong]**).

## 20. "Google proved psychological safety is the key to great teams": Overstated

- Internal, unpublished study (`google2015` **[weak]**); the peer-reviewed evidence (`edmondson1999`, `frazier2017`) is what supports psychological safety.

## 21. "First impressions are formed in 7 seconds and never change": Overstated

- Judgments form even faster (about 100 ms, `willis2006` **[strong]**), but they are not necessarily accurate, and bad impressions are *sticky*, not permanent (`baumeister2001`).

## 22. Retracted "findings" still circulating

- "Signing at the top reduces cheating" (`shu2012`) and "Inauthenticity makes people feel immoral" (`gino2015`): both **retracted**. Do not cite as evidence.
- Several valid, unretracted papers in this registry have a co-author with retracted work elsewhere (flag `coauthor-with-retractions`). They are usable but should be paired with converging evidence.
