## 1. Mistakes loom larger to you than to others

**Evidence**

- After a blunder or public failure, people expect to be judged more harshly than they actually are. **[strong]** `savitsky2001`
- People overestimate how much others notice them (the "spotlight effect"). **[strong]** `gilovich2000`
- The "pratfall effect": a highly competent person who spilled coffee became *more* likeable, while a mediocre person became less likeable. **[weak]** `aronson1966`. A single small 1966 study of male students listening to recordings. It only helps people already seen as competent; don't present it as a general law.
- But bad is stronger than good: negative impressions form faster and stick longer. **[strong]** `baumeister2001`

**Researcher's synthesis**: both are true. Others notice your mistake less than you fear, *and* the ones they notice weigh heavily. So own it quickly, fix it visibly, and move on rather than dwelling.

## 2. Teams and organisations that learn from errors

- Hospital units with better relationships and coaching had *higher* detected drug-error rates; Edmondson proposed that people in those units were more willing to report. **[moderate]** `edmondson1996`
- Firms with an "error management culture" (talking openly about errors, catching and fixing them fast) performed better. **[moderate]** `vandyck2005` (65 Dutch and 47 German organisations; correlational)
- Psychological safety predicts learning behaviour. **[strong]** `edmondson1999` (see `teams.md`).

## 3. Apologies

**Evidence**

- How to repair trust depends on the violation: apologising worked better for *competence* failures; for *integrity* accusations, denial worked better, unless guilt was later shown, in which case an apology was better. **[strong]** `kim2004`
- Apologies with more components were more effective, some components mattered more than others, and apologies worked better after competence violations than integrity violations. **[moderate]** `lewicki2016`. Which component ranked highest was not checked; don't name one without reading the paper.
- People withhold good apologies because of low concern for the victim, threat to their self-image, and the belief that apologies won't work. **[moderate]** `schumann2018`
- Even "superfluous" apologies for things you didn't cause increased trust by signalling empathy. **[moderate]** `brooks2014`

**Researcher's synthesis** (author's view, informed by the above): a good workplace apology names the specific thing, takes responsibility without "but", says what you'll do to fix it and to prevent a repeat, and then *does it*. Never deny an integrity failure you actually committed; `kim2004` shows denial backfires once guilt is evident.
