# Formality calibration: register, email and chat, humour, swearing, emoji

Research notes for Part II. Grades and labels as in `README.md`; keys refer to `sources.yaml`.

**Gap note**: there is little rigorous research on "when to be formal vs casual" as such. The evidence below comes from adjacent questions (tone in email, emoticons, humour and status, profanity, first impressions). Much everyday etiquette advice has **no evidence base at all**, and the book should present it as convention or the author's view.

---

## 1. Text loses tone, and we don't notice

**Evidence**

- People overestimate how well their tone (sarcasm, humour, seriousness) comes across in email, because they "hear" their own intent when rereading. **[strong]** `kruger2005`
- Receivers overestimate how quickly senders expect replies to non-urgent after-hours emails ("email urgency bias"), which adds stress. A one-line note from the sender about expected reply time reduces it. **[strong]** `giurge2021`
- Expectations to monitor email after hours raise anxiety and harm health and relationship satisfaction, for employees and their partners, independent of time actually spent on email. **[moderate]** `becker2021`, `belkin2020` (see `burnout.md`).

**Researcher's synthesis**: practical rules that follow from the evidence: say your tone outright when it matters ("Quick question, no rush"); assume sarcasm will not survive text; state when you expect a reply. These are inferences from the studies, not tested interventions, except the reply-time note, which *was* tested.

## 2. Emoji, exclamation marks and first contact

**Evidence**

- In work-related *first impressions*, a smiley emoticon did not increase perceived warmth and *lowered* perceived competence, which reduced information sharing. The effect depended on how formal the context was. **[moderate]** `glikson2018`
- In professional email lists, exclamation points mostly worked as markers of friendliness. **[weak]** `waseleski2006`
- Faces are judged for trustworthiness and competence within about 100 ms, and more exposure mostly adds confidence, not different judgments. **[strong]** `willis2006`. Silent clips under 30 seconds of teachers predicted end-of-term evaluations. **[moderate]** `ambady1993`. Caveat: fast judgments are not *accurate* judgments.

**Researcher's synthesis**: match formality *down*, not up. Start one notch more formal with strangers, seniors and new clients, and let the other person set the casual tone. Once a relationship exists, emoji and exclamation marks mostly signal friendliness. This "match down" rule is the author's heuristic, consistent with `glikson2018`.

## 3. Register matching

**Evidence (indirect)**

- People naturally converge in function-word style, and in romantic contexts more style matching predicted mutual interest and stability. **[weak]** for work. `ireland2011`
- Deliberate non-conformity, such as casual dress in a formal setting, can *raise* perceived status and competence, but only when observers know the norms, see it as intentional, and when it comes from someone whose status is already secure. **[moderate]** `bellezza2014`, flagged because a co-author has retracted papers elsewhere.

**Researcher's synthesis**: breaking the dress or formality code is a privilege of established status, which is another reason to calibrate rather than default to casual when new (see `newcomers.md`).

## 4. Humour and status

**Evidence**

- Successful humour raises the teller's status by signalling confidence and competence. Inappropriate or failed humour signals confidence but *low* competence, and lowers status. **[strong]** `bitterly2017` (8 experiments)
- In one experiment, the same humour raised status for men and lowered it for women, affecting performance evaluations and perceived leadership. **[moderate]** `evans2019`
- Meta-analysis: positive humour by employees and supervisors is associated with better performance, satisfaction, cohesion and health, and lower burnout and withdrawal. **[moderate]** `mesmermagnus2012`. Correlational; the authors warn that derogatory humour is a different thing with legal and relational risks.

**Researcher's synthesis**: humour is a high-variance bet: the upside is real, the downside is lopsided, and the audience decides whether it landed. Safest forms: self-directed, situational, inclusive. Riskiest: humour at someone's expense, and anything that leans on group identity. This is the author's view, informed by the above.

## 5. Swearing

**Evidence** (thin)

- Speakers who used profanity were rated lower on overall impression, intelligence and trustworthiness in a vignette study. **[weak]** `defrank2019` (N = 138)
- Qualitative case studies argue swearing can build solidarity and relieve stress in permissive cultures. **[weak]** `baruch2007`
- A widely shared finding linked profanity to *honesty*. **[weak]** `feldman2017`. Correlational, crude measures; do not use it to justify swearing at work.

**Researcher's synthesis**: profanity is register. Mirror the room, never lead with it upward or with strangers, and never aim it at a person.

---

## 6. Gratitude and small courtesies

- People who express gratitude underestimate how good recipients feel and overestimate the awkwardness, which keeps them from saying thanks. **[moderate]** `kumar2018`
- A brief thank-you increased helpers' further help, including to other people, by making them feel valued; a manager's thanks increased fundraisers' calls in a field test. **[moderate]** `grant2010`, flagged because a co-author has retracted papers elsewhere.
- "Superfluous" apologies ("I'm sorry about the rain") increased trust by signalling empathy. **[moderate]** `brooks2014` (see `mistakes.md`).

## 7. A myth to keep out of this chapter

- "93% of communication is non-verbal" (Mehrabian's 7-38-55) is a misreading. Mehrabian himself says the equation applies only to communicating feelings and attitudes. **[myth]** `mehrabian1971`, `mehrabian1967a`, `mehrabian1967b`. See `myths.md`.
