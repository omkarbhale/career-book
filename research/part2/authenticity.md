# Authenticity vs adaptation

Research notes for Part II. Grades and labels as in `README.md`; keys refer to `sources.yaml`.

The captain's brief asks for conduct that is "not an act, but simply who you are". The evidence supports a more careful version: **who you are includes how you adapt**. Felt authenticity is about acting on your values and your best tendencies, not about refusing to adjust. That is good news for the book's thesis, and it needs stating precisely.

---

## 1. Self-monitoring: chameleons do get ahead (mostly)

**Evidence**

- *Self-monitoring* is the tendency to read social cues and adjust how you present yourself to fit. **[strong]** foundational measure. `snyder1974`
- A meta-analysis (136 samples, N = 23,191) found high self-monitoring relates to job performance and to leadership emergence. Men scored somewhat higher on average. **[strong]** `day2002`
- Over five years, high self-monitoring MBA graduates moved employers and locations more and won more promotions, both across companies and internally. **[moderate]** `kilduff1994`
- High self-monitors more often held central positions in a firm's social network; self-monitoring and centrality each predicted performance. **[moderate]** `mehra2001`
- Newer meta-analysis (55 samples, N = 9,029): self-monitoring relates to leadership emergence and effectiveness, *but usually not when subordinates do the rating*. **[strong]** `lei2025`

**Researcher's synthesis**: adapting your self-presentation helps you get noticed, promoted and seen as a leader by those above and around you. It does less for how the people you lead experience you. Good material for a "two audiences" joke or diagram.

## 2. What makes people *feel* authentic

**Evidence**

- In experience-sampling studies, people felt most authentic when acting extraverted, agreeable, conscientious, emotionally stable and intellectually engaged, *regardless of their own traits*. Acting "in character" was not what made them feel real. **[moderate]** `fleeson2010`
- State authenticity, the momentary feeling of alignment with your real self, has identifiable triggers and links to well-being. **[moderate]** `sedikides2017`
- Meta-analysis (75 studies, N = 36,533): felt authenticity correlates with well-being (r = .40) and work engagement (r = .37). **[strong]** `sutton2020`. Caveat: correlational, and the effect may run both ways (feeling good can make people feel more like themselves).
- Workplace authenticity research is fragmented across 10 constructs. A major review says it "both reinforces and tempers" popular enthusiasm, and that power shapes who can afford authenticity. **[strong]** `cha2019`; see also the special-section overview `hewlin2020` **[moderate]**.
- Onboarding that invited newcomers to express their "best selves" improved customer satisfaction and retention at six months, compared with onboarding focused on company pride. **[moderate]** `cable2013`, flagged because a co-author has retracted papers elsewhere.
- **Excluded:** Gino, Kouchaki & Galinsky (2015), "The moral virtue of authenticity", is **retracted**. `gino2015` Do not cite it as evidence.

**Practitioner view**

- Ibarra's "authenticity paradox": a rigid view of authenticity ("that's just not me") keeps people from growing into new roles. She recommends borrowing from many role models and treating style as something to experiment with. **[moderate]** `ibarra2015`. This is a practitioner essay, but it fits the evidence above.

**Researcher's synthesis**

- Authenticity is about **values and intentions held constant** while **style varies by context**. A good test is "would I be comfortable if the people in my other contexts saw this?" That is the author's heuristic, not a finding.
- Faking *feelings* is costly (next section). Adjusting *register* and *style* is not inherently fake.

## 3. Emotional labour: the cost of faking feelings

**Evidence**

- Hochschild coined "emotional labor": managing feelings to produce the display a job requires. She distinguished **surface acting** (faking the outward display) from **deep acting** (working to actually feel it). **[strong]** foundational qualitative work. `hochschild1983`
- Grandey reframed emotional labour as emotion regulation. **[strong]** theory. `grandey2000`
- Coworker-rated affective delivery fell with surface acting and rose with deep acting, and only surface acting related to stress. **[moderate]** `grandey2003`
- Meta-analysis (95 studies): surface acting, and feeling a clash between felt and required emotions, relate substantially to impaired well-being (ρ ≈ .39 to .48) and worse job attitudes. Deep acting has weak links to strain and positive links to customer satisfaction. **[strong]** `hulsheger2011`

**Researcher's synthesis**: this gives the "not an act" thesis an evidence base. Pasting on a mask is what hurts. Reappraising a situation so you genuinely feel differently (deep acting) is closer to a skill than to a lie. The book can contrast "acting" with "adjusting".

## 4. Code-switching and who pays for "professionalism"

**Evidence**

- Black employees who racially code-switch were rated more professional than those who did not, by Black and White raters alike. That shows how strong the incentive is. **[moderate]** `mccluney2021`
- The same authors argue that code-switching carries a psychological toll and can contribute to burnout. Their summary of findings is "largely self-reported". **[moderate]** `mccluney2019`, a practitioner summary.
- Theory: employees may present "facades of conformity", pretending to share organisational values, especially minority-status employees, with distress as a likely cost. **[moderate]** `hewlin2003`
- Theory: deciding whether to disclose an invisible stigmatised identity is a continuing decision, and disclosing differently across life domains creates strain. **[moderate]** `ragins2008`

**Researcher's synthesis**: "be yourself" costs different people different amounts. The book should say so plainly and avoid moralising at readers who adapt to survive. This connects to `culture.md` (norms differ) and `power.md` (status affects what you can get away with).

## 5. "Bring your whole self to work": what the evidence supports

**Evidence for**

- Felt authenticity correlates with well-being and engagement. **[strong]** `sutton2020`
- Inviting newcomers to express their best selves helped retention. **[moderate]** `cable2013`

**Evidence against, or for caution**

- When a *higher-status* colleague disclosed a personal weakness, they lost status and influence and task conflict rose; a peer's identical disclosure did not hurt. **[moderate]** `gibson2018`
- Theory: across demographic and status differences, personal disclosure can sometimes widen the gap instead of closing it. **[moderate]** `phillips2009`
- People differ in whether they *want* work and home blended. "Integrating" perks lowered satisfaction for people who prefer separation, and fit between preference and workplace predicted conflict and stress. **[moderate]** `rothbard2005`, `kreiner2006`; boundary theory `ashforth2000` **[strong]**.
- No study was found that directly tests the slogan "bring your whole self". The case against it rests on disclosure, status and boundary research.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the defensible version is to **bring your whole *values* and your *chosen* self, at a pace and depth that fits your role and the relationship**. Self-disclosure is a dial, not a switch. This anticipates `relationships.md` on boundaries and `formality.md` on calibration.
