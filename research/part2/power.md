# Power and politics: political skill, visibility and credit, managing up, mentors and sponsors, saying no, meetings

Research notes for Part II. Grades and labels as in `README.md`; keys refer to `sources.yaml`.

**Framing note**: "office politics" is often treated as dirty. The evidence treats *political skill* as a measurable competence that predicts performance and career success. The book can reframe politics as "the social side of getting things done", while keeping the ethical lines from `ethics.md` in view.

---

## 1. Political skill

**Evidence**

- Political skill is defined and measured as social astuteness, interpersonal influence, networking ability and apparent sincerity. It predicted manager performance ratings and is unrelated to general mental ability. **[strong]** `ferris2005`
- Meta-analysis: political skill relates to self-efficacy, satisfaction, commitment, performance, citizenship, career success and reputation, and predicts task performance *beyond* the Big Five and cognitive ability. Networking, influence and apparent sincerity matter; astuteness less so. **[strong]** `munyon2015`
- Meta-analysis: political skill predicts task and contextual (citizenship) performance, contextual more strongly, and the task link strengthens in more socially demanding jobs. **[strong]** `bing2011`
- High self-monitors move into central network positions; centrality predicts performance. **[moderate]** `mehra2001` (see `authenticity.md` §1).

**Researcher's synthesis**: "apparent sincerity" is the most quotable dimension, and the most ironic. The book's thesis implies that the most reliable way to *seem* sincere is to *be* sincere (author's view).

## 2. Influence tactics

- Meta-analysis: ingratiation and rationality (reasoned argument) had positive effects on work outcomes; other tactics mattered only in certain situations. **[moderate]** `higgins2003`
- Reciprocity, consistency and liking are core influence processes. **[strong]** `cialdini2004` (see `first-principles.md`).

## 3. Visibility and credit

**Evidence**

- People recall their own contributions to joint work more easily and claim more responsibility than others give them. **[strong]** `ross1979`. Popular retellings say spouses' estimates "summed to well over 100%"; this could **not** be verified, since the paper reportedly used line scales, so do not repeat it.
- Getting people to think about each collaborator's contribution reduces over-claiming. **[moderate]** `savitsky2005` (title-level)
- Credit for group work is biased: female economists were less likely to get tenure the more they coauthored, while men were not penalised. **[strong]** `sarsons2021`
- Self-promoters overestimate how happy listeners feel and underestimate their annoyance, so they over-promote. **[moderate]** `scopelliti2015`
- Self-promotion raised women's perceived competence but cost them likability and hireability ("backlash"). **[moderate]** `rudman1998` (1990s experiments)
- Simply being *seen* at work leads observers to infer dependability and commitment. **[moderate]** `elsbach2010` (see `remote.md`).

**Researcher's synthesis**: since everyone over-credits themselves, the generous norm, crediting others specifically and publicly, is both kind and strategic: it corrects a known bias and builds reputation. Make your own work visible through *information* (status updates, results, documentation) rather than *claims* (author's view).

## 4. Managing up

**Evidence**

- Leader-member exchange: leaders form relationships of different quality with each team member. **[strong]** `graen1995`. Meta-analysis: LMX quality is driven most by leader behaviours, but also by follower characteristics and the relationship, and it mediates many outcomes. **[strong]** `dulebohn2012`
- Managers rated employees who voiced challenging ideas as worse performers than those who voiced supportive ones. **[moderate]** `burris2012`

**Practitioner model**

- Gabarro & Kotter, "Managing Your Boss" (1980): consciously work with your boss for the best result for you, the boss and the company, by understanding their goals, pressures and preferred working style, and your own. **[weak]** as evidence. `gabarro1980`

**Researcher's synthesis**: how to raise a challenge so it is heard as loyalty, not threat (framing it around shared goals, raising it privately first, bringing options) follows from `burris2012`'s finding that perceived loyalty and threat drive the reaction. These are inferences, not tested scripts.

## 5. Mentors vs sponsors

**Evidence**

- Meta-analysis: mentoring is linked to career benefits, but effects on objective outcomes such as pay are *small*; subjective benefits are larger. **[strong]** `allen2004`. Across youth, academic and workplace mentoring, effects are generally small. **[strong]** `eby2008`
- Meta-analysis of career success: organisational sponsorship relates more strongly to *career satisfaction*; human capital and demographics relate more strongly to salary and promotion. **[strong]** `ng2005`

**Practitioner view**

- Ibarra, Carter & Silva (2010): high-potential women were over-mentored and under-sponsored. Mentors advise; sponsors use their influence to advocate for you. **[moderate]** `ibarra2010`. HBR article drawing on a Catalyst survey (Catalyst is an advocacy organisation); its specific statistics were not checked.
- **Not verified**: popular statistics from Hewlett's "Sponsor Effect" research (e.g. "X% more likely to be promoted") came up only in promotional text, and they are not in the registry. Don't use them unless verified.

**Researcher's synthesis**: the mentor/sponsor distinction is useful, but the evidence that sponsorship *causes* promotion is weaker than practitioner books suggest. Treat it as plausible, not proven.

## 6. Saying no

**Evidence**

- People underestimate by up to 50% how likely others are to say yes to a direct request, partly because they underestimate how hard it is to say no. **[strong]** `flynn2008`
- People who decline invitations overestimate how angry or hurt the inviter will be. **[moderate]** `givi2024` (social invitations, not work requests)
- Women are asked more, and accept more, requests for low-promotability tasks. **[strong]** `babcock2017`
- Helping beyond the role can overload you. **[moderate]** `bolino2005`, `bergeron2007`

**Researcher's synthesis**: refusing costs less than you fear (`givi2024`), and accepting costs more than you think (`bolino2005`, `babcock2017`). A good "no" offers an alternative or a reason linked to priorities. That last point is the author's view; no test was found.

---

## 7. Meetings

**Evidence**

- Satisfaction with meetings is a distinct part of job satisfaction and predicts it, more so for people with many meetings. **[moderate]** `rogelberg2010`
- Brainstorming in groups produces fewer and no better ideas than the same people working alone; the loss grows with group size. **[strong]** `mullen1991`
- During remote work at an Indian IT firm, time in meetings rose and uninterrupted work fell, alongside lower productivity. **[moderate]** `gibbs2023`

**Researcher's synthesis**: generate ideas alone, then discuss together; keep meetings small; and know why each person is there. The first is evidence-based (`mullen1991`), the others are author's view.
